Binance October 2026 theft

Research date: 10 November 2026. A deep OSINT case file on the largest crypto theft on record — what was reported, how the attack unfolded, what it cost, what techniques and indicators were published, and what is still unknown. Figures differ between counters by scope, valuation moment and chain coverage; they are shown side by side, never added together.

Confirmed by multiple independent sources: a drain from Binance hot wallets on 29 October 2026, EIP-7702 delegation abuse, roughly $2.7–2.85bn frozen or blocked, Binance covering losses from company funds, and FBI attribution to North Korea's TraderTraitor on 31 October. Disputed: the attribution itself, and every headline total.

Summary

On 29 October 2026 an attacker drained Binance hot wallets, most of it in about 20 minutes from 20:05 UTC, in what every major tracker calls the largest crypto theft on record — roughly four times the ~$1.5bn Bybit theft of February 2025.

Binance's 3 November initial report traces a months-long campaign: a tampered Homebrew installer in June, a fake job interview and malicious code repository in September, a pivot via an internal AI tool on 20 October, a container deployment with a stolen deployment token on 29 October, abuse of the internal wallet signing service, and direct transfers on most chains plus an EIP-7702 delegation sweep of “Binance 14” on Ethereum.

Binance says no wallet private key leaked; the attacker instead got Binance's own signing infrastructure to sign. That distinction is central, and is corroborated on-chain by TRM, Elliptic, Chainalysis and Blockaid: valid signatures on many chains, and 50 pre-signed EIP-7702 authorisations.

Frozen is not recovered, and recovered is not replenished. Frozen or blocked assets are issuer and network freezes, not money returned to users. Replenishment is Binance buying replacement tokens with company, SAFU and Futures-insurance money. The attacker still held roughly $2.4–3.3bn in unf freezable assets in the 30 October–3 November snapshots, depending on scope and timing.

Reporting timeline

All times UTC unless stated; Binance's 3 November report uses UTC+8, converted here where noted.

  • June 2026 — tampered installer. Engineer A, in wallet development and operations, searched online for a popular AI coding tool and downloaded a tampered Homebrew installer. Binance security detected the anomaly, replaced the device and rotated credentials — but Engineer A's role and wallet-team colleagues had already been exposed. Binance does not name the AI tool, the search term or the installer host.
  • 2026 to date — background. Binance says it detected and prevented 57 suspected North Korea-linked incidents in 2026. Whether any of the 57 is this same campaign is not stated.
  • September 2026 — fake job interview. Software Engineer B, whose profile on a legitimate job platform mentioned Binance work, was contacted by someone impersonating a project director. Round one was a live video interview with a purported HR representative who shared a social-media account with personal photos and videos to build trust. Round two was via a popular messaging app with someone claiming to be the CEO of a fictitious company. Near the end, Engineer B was asked to download an “assignment” code repository onto a work computer; malicious code executed during installation. Neither the platform, the fictitious company, the repository nor any person is named.
  • Malware behaviour (Binance, file operations only — no hash, domain or IP published). Obfuscated, evasion-capable, described as zero-day at the time; it replaced a legitimate development-tool component with a malicious version to execute commands and contact an attacker-controlled server, concealed activity and erased traces. Published operations: CREATE /opt/homebrew/Cellar/pyenv/2.8.0/libexec/pyenv_ (3,414 B), DELETE the legitimate pyenv (3,344 B), RENAME pyenv_ to pyenv, DELETE /opt/homebrew/bin/pyenv, then a new symlink and a spawned zsh.
  • 20 October 2026 — internal AI-tool pivot. Attackers used Engineer B's compromised computer and an internal AI tool to take control of devices belonging to Engineer A and Engineer C, another wallet software engineer, and quickly found a token on Engineer A's computer with container-deployment permissions. The AI tool is not named, and Binance does not describe the AI-tool vulnerability or mechanism.
  • 28 October, morning — on-chain staging. The attacker operator wallet received three small Tornado Cash withdrawals (Elliptic: three × 0.1 ETH). Early on 29 October it deployed and tested code on two wallets, rehearsed the final version on a third, and routed test funds back.
  • 29 October, 15:59 UTC — sweeper deployed. The operator deployed the sweeper contract later used on Binance 14, about four hours before use, after rehearsals moving $2–$6.
  • 29 October, 17:15–19:56 UTC — signing-service infiltration. Attackers logged into Engineer C's computer, deployed a container using credentials stolen from Engineer A, and ran a malicious script to launch the attack.
  • 29 October, 20:05–20:09 UTC — first drain, XRP Ledger. Five Binance hot wallets sent 233.46m XRP to one attacker address, leaving minimum balances; that XRP had not moved in later snapshots.
  • 29 October, shortly after 20:10 UTC — Solana, Optimism (and, per Elliptic, NEAR, Base). Binance: about 970,500 SOL and 44.2m OP to attacker addresses, then an internal alert on large transfers and an investigation.
  • 29 October, 20:20 UTC — Bitcoin, Tron and EIP-7702 delegation. A Binance Bitcoin hot wallet sent 18,270 BTC (peel-chained into roughly 1,000 / 100 BTC pieces in the same block, per TRM); within about two minutes a Tron wallet sent 612.7m USDT + 46.4m TRX. At 20:20:59 UTC the operator sent a type-4 transaction to Binance 14 carrying 50 EIP-7702 authorisations signed with the Binance 14 key, nonces 18,914,775–18,914,824; one matched (ThreatMon: nonce 18,914,809).
  • 29 October, 20:24:23 UTC — the $2.5bn sweep. The operator called sweep on Binance 14: 167,464 ETH, 1.615bn USDT and roughly 60–65 other tokens to collection address 0x5227f603C1eE34CE3C06eFe6477003BDfCCB2f8c, leaving 1 ETH. Elliptic values the single transaction at about $2.53bn. Within 12 minutes, 10 more Binance hot wallets on four chains sent funds to the same address.
  • 29 October, 20:36 / 20:44 UTC — payouts stop. Last customer payouts on Solana, BNB Chain and Tron at 20:36 UTC, Ethereum at 20:44 UTC. Binance hot-wallet payouts then fell 99.8% over 54 hours.
  • 29 October, 20:41–23:56 UTC — standing drain. Because the delegation persisted, Binance 14 was swept again at 20:41, 20:59, 21:04, 21:44, 22:39 and 23:56 UTC. It also pulled 823m USDC (780m + 43m) from Binance-owned smart account “Hot Wallet 34” via its owner rights at 20:41:23 UTC. The 21:44 sweep alone took 200m USDT + 13 tokens deposited after the first sweep. Binance's own later top-up of Binance 14 was also swept — the mechanism, not just the initial balance, is the lesson.

Attack path / kill chain

Binance's account unless marked on-chain. Step by step, with the largest gap stated plainly.

  1. Reconnaissance and target selection. The June Homebrew incident exposed Engineer A's wallet-team role and colleagues. Binance does not say how the June installer was distributed in its own report; secondary coverage describing a malicious Google ad is unverified secondary detail, not Binance's wording.
  2. Initial access — two parallel footholds, one successful chain. (a) June tampered Homebrew installer (supply-chain, detected and device replaced — but exposure persisted). (b) September fake-interview repository (social engineering + malicious code execution on install, pyenv replacement, command-and-control contact). Binance presents these as one multi-stage campaign; it does not explicitly state that the June foothold and the September foothold are the same operator's, nor which foothold persisted to October — the October pivot is described from Engineer B's machine.
  3. Persistence and evasion. Malicious pyenv component, obfuscation, trace-erasure, and (per Teng via Elliptic) a self-destruct mechanism. No persistence mechanism beyond this is published; no scheduled task, service or implant name is given.
  4. Lateral movement via internal AI tool (20 October). From Engineer B's machine, via the unnamed internal AI tool, to Engineers A and C. Binance gives no detail on what the AI tool does, what permission it held, or how it was abused — this is the largest technical gap in the published kill chain. Do not fill it with a generic “prompt injection” claim: no source says that.
  5. Credential access and functional privilege escalation (as published). Discovery of a container-deployment token on Engineer A's computer. Binance does not call this privilege escalation, does not say how the token was stored or extracted, and does not publish a separate escalation step. The token plus Engineer C login is the functional escalation: deploy rights, then container, then signing-service access.
  6. Execution and signing-service abuse (29 October, 17:15–19:56 UTC). Login to Engineer C's computer, container deployed with Engineer A's credentials, malicious script run. The attacker then used unauthorised access to the internal wallet signing service to initiate transactions outside the normal withdrawal process. Binance's later wording: EIP-7702 delegation “did not pass through our normal approval controls”.
  7. EIP-7702 persistence (Ethereum). 50 pre-signed authorisations (nonces 18,914,775–18,914,824), one valid delegation, sweeper contract controls Binance 14, repeated sweeps of new deposits without new signatures for 3h50m. MPC design slowed revocation: no single private key could immediately revoke; a specialised revocation process had to be designed and executed.
  8. Cross-chain exfiltration in parallel. Ordinary signed transfers on XRP Ledger, Solana, Optimism, Bitcoin, Tron, Arbitrum, Avalanche, Base, Polygon (and NEAR per Elliptic). TRM's inference, widely repeated: a single actor obtaining valid signatures across chains with different key types and signing schemes points to signing-infrastructure compromise, not per-wallet key theft. Binance's first statement (“no evidence of any wallet private key leak”) is consistent with this.
  9. Laundering — a race against freezes. Conversion was to ETH, BTC and TRX (unfreezable natives) and via DeFi, lending and bridges, at steep discounts (see Impact).
  10. Containment limits. Suspending withdrawals did not stop EIP-7702 transfers already authorised on-chain, nor already-signed cross-chain transfers; only the zero-address re-authorisation (00:10 UTC, 30 October) ended the delegation. Binance says all exploited entry points are now closed and attack-path systems rebuilt or replaced — a claim not yet independently verified (independent review invited, not published).

AI — two different questions, do not conflate them

AI as attack pivot: confirmed as Binance's account — an internal AI tool was used on 20 October to reach Engineers A and C. Tool name, function and abuse method undisclosed.

AI agents as launderers: not evidenced. Elliptic (2 November, via CoinDesk): laundering is automated but looks like human-supervised scripts, not an AI agent making decisions; failed bridge transactions look like script retries, not agent learning; six bridges first used within 100 minutes on 30 October does not establish who or what selected them. Elliptic's main article: “Whether the automation involves AI agents… cannot be determined from the on-chain data.” Elliptic's 9 November update adds that the speed has led to speculation about AI agents, but the evidence points to conventional automation — fixed timing, set amounts, blind retries, new tactics introduced in steps hours apart, and pauses that look like human working patterns; nothing observed shows an AI agent making decisions.

Likewise, generic TraderTraitor lure domains and IPs in secondary playbooks (e.g. fake Teams / Zoom lures) are not tied to Binance in any primary source read. Any threat-intel page that lists them under “Binance IOCs” without that caveat is conflating campaigns.

Attribution — evidence and dispute

For DPRK / TraderTraitor

  • TRM (30 October, high confidence): robust on- and off-chain intelligence (undisclosed off-chain sources); playbook matches Bybit (February 2025) and KelpDAO (April 2026): small Tornado Cash withdrawals, staging well before theft, pre-obtained signatures in one burst.
  • Chainalysis (30 October, 00:30 ET): strongest evidence = Binance funds commingled on-chain in real time with September Bitget theft proceeds, already attributed to North Korea; plus laundering patterns initially suggesting TraderTraitor.
  • Elliptic (pre-FBI: “likely”; post-FBI: confirms its assessment): (1) Binance funds laundered alongside Bitget proceeds, combined in several wallets (first reported by ZachXBT; Elliptic later: ~$16m sent to addresses holding Bitget proceeds); (2) common service usage with three earlier TraderTraitor-attributed thefts (BigONE, WOO X, AFX); (3) long preparation, staff targeting, abuse of victim's own signing systems resembling Bybit; (4) scale — very few actors could coordinate 11 chains in ~3 hours. Elliptic caveats (important): overlap with Bitget is small; laundering slower than usual for the group; Binance had not published a root cause at that point.
  • ZachXBT / Blockaid / Pangolin Intelligence (secondary): ZachXBT “likely Lazarus”; Pangolin Intelligence also pointed to North Korea. Independent primary posts not re-verified live here.
  • FBI (31 October, formal): “The Democratic People's Republic of Korea (North Korea) was responsible for the theft of more than $6 billion USD in virtual assets from Binance, on or about October 29, 2026. The FBI refers to this specific North Korean malicious cyber activity as 'TraderTraitor'.” With USSS + IRS-CI; observed conversion primarily into ETH, expected further laundering via DeFi and instant swaps; 628 addresses published; report via local field office or ic3.gov. The FBI alert publishes no forensic reasoning, no malware analysis, and no Binance kill-chain detail — its evidential weight rests on the attribution statement + address list, not on published proof.
  • Binance's position: 30 October (via Al Jazeera): CEO Richard Teng — “Our investigation has not attributed responsibility to any individual.” 3 November report: “According to the latest information released by the FBI, the DPRK was responsible” — i.e. Binance cites the FBI; it does not present its own attribution evidence.

Against, dissent and caution

  • North Korea denial (KCNA, reported 3 November): Kim Yo Jong (sister of Kim Jong Un, ruling-party director) denied the FBI announcement, calling it a “grave political provocation” (CoinDesk) / evidence of “US hostile policy” (Reuters), and said there was “no need” for dialogue with Washington. Elliptic rejected the denial, telling CoinDesk its analysis supports the FBI attribution to TraderTraitor.
  • Pre-FBI caution in the record: Vietnam Blockchain Association's ChainTracer (morning 30 October) said identities and locations had not been determined and did not conclude TraderTraitor. A “person familiar with crypto investigations” (via Finance / BigGo, 30 October) cautioned only a handful of groups could execute a theft this size but evidence did not yet conclusively identify Lazarus. ThreatMon (pre-FBI, updated 1 November) rated TraderTraitor “plausible, moderate–high” and noted no government confirmation at that time — now superseded on the government point by the FBI alert, but its caveats about undisclosed off-chain intelligence remain valid.
  • Naming precision: “TraderTraitor” is a US-government activity codename, not a company or org chart; it overlaps vendor names Jade Sleet (Microsoft), UNC4899 (Google / Mandiant), Slow Pisces (Palo Alto Unit 42), and the broader Lazarus umbrella / APT38 / BlueNoroff financial cluster. Equating “TraderTraitor = Lazarus Group” without that note is an oversimplification flagged by multiple secondary analysts. No source in this research names individual operators.

Binance response, remediation and lessons

Immediate response (Binance): internal alert, investigation, withdrawal suspension, emergency controls (freeze coordination with issuers and partners, revocation via zero-address authorisation), law-enforcement + blockchain-intelligence cooperation. Binance says all exploited entry points closed, attack-path systems rebuilt or replaced.

Remediation as of 3 November (Binance): rebuilt affected wallet systems in a fresh environment; rebuilt internal AI tool + strengthened access controls; replaced affected devices; disabled credentials and tokens of affected engineers; enhanced scans of all employee devices; enhanced AI-based behavioural anomaly detection; strengthened wallet-service risk controls.

Next steps (Binance, not yet delivered in sources read): (1) independent third-party cybersecurity review — firms invited, scope and findings to guide remediation, findings to be published in some form (not stated whether the full report will be public); (2) expanded investment in AI security and prevention, endpoint protection, dev-environment security, behavioural anomaly monitoring; (3) security research fund with AI companies, industry participants and security organisations, focused on AI security in blockchain. Some measures will remain confidential (Binance).

Proof-of-reserves status: see Customer impact above. In short: 31 October snapshot published, 10 major tokens ≥1:1 verifiable via zk proof expected 3 November — delivery not confirmed in sources read to 4 November; 4 November snapshot taken 00:00 UTC, 44 tokens, confirmed in Recovery Update #7, with its full zk proof expected in about 50–60 hours (around 6–7 November; Binance said on Friday 6 November the proof has been generated and users can now verify ≥1:1 backing — Decrypt; TechFlow via BlockchainReporter — confirmed live on the public proof-of-reserves page on 7 November at about 23:07 SGT, with Audit Time 04/11/26 00:00:00 UTC, zk-SNARKs, a published Merkle root hash and reserve ratios for all 44 tokens at or above 100%); proof of reserves proves customer-asset backing at snapshot time — it does not prove SAFU / Futures-fund balances (separate pages), does not audit the theft, and does not verify Binance's replenishment purchases beyond the snapshot balances.

Lessons and defensive controls (sourced, not invented)

  • Signer-enforced policy before signing (Blockaid / TRM): destination allowlists, per-transaction and per-window value caps, new-counterparty and mixer-funding checks, and explicit rejection of unexpected EIP-7702 authorisations; a batch of authorisations across a nonce range = IOC. An independent co-signer / policy layer between signing infrastructure and chain (Blockaid's Cosigner model) would have rejected the delegation.
  • Monitor EOA code state (TRM / Blockaid / ThreatMon): alert on any eth_getCode change on a treasury or hot wallet; pre-prepare, pre-approve and rehearse a revocation transaction (zero-address authorisation) — Binance's 3h50m delegation window is the cautionary metric; MPC alone does not solve revocation.
  • Segregate signing by chain and wallet tier (TRM): one compromise must not yield valid signatures on 10 chains (TRM scope); separate approval paths; cap hot-wallet balances to near-term withdrawal need.
  • Separate “can deploy” from “can move money” (TRM / secondary): DevOps laptops and deployment tokens must never be one hop from a signing service; short-lived, hardware-key, audited sessions; no standing path from engineer endpoints to production signers.
  • Endpoint + dev-toolchain hygiene (Binance's own account): verify installer provenance (Homebrew / AI-coding-tool downloads), block running interview “assignments” on work machines, treat fake-recruiter contact via job platforms as a monitored threat (Binance blocked 57 such attempts in 2026 — yet one succeeded), endpoint detection must cover tool-replacement (pyenv swap) not just known malware hashes.
  • AI-tool governance (Binance's remediation): internal AI tools are privileged lateral-movement paths; rebuild + access-control hardening, behavioural anomaly detection on AI-tool use, and treat AI-tool sessions as production access.
  • Freeze-speed and screening (Chainalysis / Elliptic / TRM / Bitquery): rescreen deposits and counterparties against FBI + Elliptic + TRM labels continuously (lists grow from hundreds to thousands in days); follow value through receipt tokens, pool tokens and chained bridges, not just direct transfers; screen several hops downstream, including bridge payouts matched by amount and timing; expect laundering via lending protocols (Aave / JustLend) designed to defeat issuer freezes.
  • BFSI translation: the failure was not broken cryptography or a stolen cold-storage key — it was governance of the approval path. Boards and auditors should test who and what decides what a signer signs, not only where keys live; a reimbursement pledge is not assurance without the independent review, and the proof-of-reserves proof for the 44-token snapshot is now live on the public page (confirmed 7 November, 23:07 SGT), with all 44 tokens at or above 100% — though a reserve snapshot is a point-in-time check, not an audit of the theft or of the replenishment purchases.
