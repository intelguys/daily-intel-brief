Executive summary

1

AI-driven intrusion is now a named, government-warned threat to finance: the FBI and Cisco Talos both published on ARTEX in the same window. ARTEX is an open-source, LLM-driven agentic pentesting framework in use by malicious actors since at least August 2026. The FBI’s FLASH-20261113-001 names financial services among ten targeted sectors, cites a South Korean financial-sector campaign that exposed personal data of about 67,000 people, and the ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise that reached 2,250+ downstream customer environments. Talos counts more than 2,500 ARTEX-associated C2 IP addresses and says campaigns range from opportunistic scanning to targeted intrusion and supply-chain activity, including Storm-3358 hitting financial organisations in Southeast Asia. Some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. The framework needs almost no operator skill, which is the point: it compresses the time banks have to detect and respond, and it often leaves no known indicators behind.

2

The RMM crisis is now a second wave, and the N-able chains are being exploited faster than vendors can patch. Microsoft published its own teardown of SCKit, the worm abusing cloud-hosted ScreenConnect panels, and Huntress reported a second wave on 13 Nov — 205 hosts across 41 organisations, a new installer host and new markers. The technician-trap spread remains designed behaviour, not an observed infection path. Separately, the N-able 4EverAble2Shell chain was exploited within about five hours of its public PoC: researchers count 21 production N-central servers mining Monero, 19 of them already on Hotfix 2, while N-able says it has seen no compromise using the new flaws and has still not shipped a fix. The one solid control is disabling the Legacy API, which is not the default. CISA added the Check Point Gaia privilege-escalation flaw chained with PHP2Shell (CVE-2026-123039) to its exploited-vulnerabilities catalogue on 13 Nov.

3

A wave of confirmed financial-sector breaches landed overnight, led by India’s largest bank. State Bank of India filed a police case a month after hackers stole a 375,000-record set from its Global IT Centre; only 3,897 records in that set have been confirmed to hold customer personal data, and the bank says no account numbers, balances, passwords, one-time codes, card data, PAN or Aadhaar were in the identified records — the 375,000 figure is the size of the dark-web dataset, not a confirmed victim count. KB Capital in South Korea confirmed a breach of 1,811 people, and Brazil’s National Treasury confirmed a leak of about 30,000 registrations and about 4,000 Pix keys. INC Ransom claimed Raymond James, Game Changer Wealth and Jamaica’s JETS payment network — all unverified. Trezor confirmed hardware implants in three Safe 5 wallets sold by a Southeast Asian reseller. Singapore’s CSA says reported cyber incidents have tripled since September. Binance has still not published Recovery Update #10 or its full zero-knowledge proof, while the US Justice Department says more than $2.7bn of the stolen funds have been frozen.

ARTEX operators — agentic intrusion frameworks

Financial services and cryptocurrency; ten sectors named by the FBI, including finance

TTPs
No MITRE mapping published in either the FBI FLASH or the Talos post. ARTEX is an open-source, LLM-driven agentic pentesting framework (Go, no model of its own; plugs into GPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Grok, GLM and Kimi) that plans and executes intrusions with little operator input. Talos found co-deployed tooling including ScopeWeaver, AntiFox, CyberStrikeAI, PentAGI (more than 700 reachable instances found via Censys), VShell, AdaptixC2, Mimikatz, Cobalt Strike, GoPhish, Nessus, Acunetix, Fscan and Chisel. Named campaigns: Storm-3358, financially motivated and Cambodia-based, targeting financial organisations primarily in Southeast Asia and deploying RATs (VShell, AdaptixC2, WISP); an unattributed Chinese-speaking, financially motivated campaign against South Korean financial organisations that resulted in data exfiltration; VAPOR PANDA (CrowdStrike, from 17 Oct) against 200+ organisations in 14+ countries including the financial sector, with confirmed post-compromise activity at nine; Storm-0940 using ARTEX to accelerate sign-ins against 2,250+ downstream Microsoft 365 tenants after the ConnectWise credential compromise; unattributed agents searching public GitHub for credentials and AI API keys, in one case moving from GitHub search to Azure Key Vault exfiltration within five minutes. Amazon Threat Intelligence’s companion MadPot post the same day captured ARTEX AI-agent requests, including AI-credential theft and a KYC-deepfake campaign.
Impact
Confirmed (FBI FLASH-20261113-001, 13 Nov; Cisco Talos, 13 Nov 20:57 UTC / 14 Nov 04:57 SGT). The FBI says ARTEX has been used by a variety of malicious actors since at least August 2026, that some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours, and that more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles were found by early November. Talos identified more than 2,500 ARTEX-associated C2 IP addresses. The South Korean financial-sector campaign cited by the FBI exposed personal data of about 67,000 people. The framework lets low-skill actors run complex multi-target campaigns, and Talos warns future chains will likely culminate in autonomous data exfiltration and ransomware activation. Snort and ClamAV coverage is published in the Talos post. Critique: the FBI and Talos agree on the shape of the threat but neither publishes a MITRE mapping or a victim list, so detection rests on the behavioural indicators in the FLASH and the IOCs in the Indicators section — and Talos is explicit that there are often no known IOCs at all. The 67,000-person figure is the FBI’s, for one cited campaign; it is not a total for ARTEX activity.

Unknown actor — ScreenConnect SCKit worm

Managed service providers and their clients; MSPs serve small banks and financial firms

TTPs
No MITRE mapping published in the Microsoft post. Microsoft’s teardown (13 Nov) confirms ConnectWise’s reporting: attackers use the MSP’s panel Run Command to silently install a rogue ScreenConnect client (MSI with a c=Exploited<N> marker) connected to attacker infrastructure; a batch launcher fetches a PowerShell decoder from ns[.]mo7sen[.]dev, which AES-256-CBC-decrypts (PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256, device-specific MachineGuid key) and extracts the SCKit bundle. Persistence via scheduled tasks (TrapWatch) and Run registry entries, plus Defender path exclusions. RelayTrap rebuilds a malicious ScreenConnect client from local libraries and is designed to deliver the launcher to a connecting technician’s workstation; a separate component steals .ASPXAUTH session cookies from Chrome, Edge and Opera (database and memory-scanning routes, DPAPI/Chromium key decryption) and uses them with ToolboxRelayUploader.exe to push payloads through the victim’s management console to other managed devices. Initial access remains unconfirmed; ConnectWise is investigating abuse of the RMM+ extension (v1.0.31/1.0.32 accepted an empty API key when none was configured), bypassing console accounts, roles and MFA. ConnectWise states the fix for CVE-2026-84869 blocks the endpoint-to-technician spread.
Impact
Confirmed (Microsoft Threat Intelligence, 13 Nov; Huntress update, 13 Nov 17:30 ET / 14 Nov 06:30 SGT). Second wave on 13 Nov: at 16:12 UTC, 205 hosts across 41 organisations were hit, again deploying rogue clients and checking for sckit-decoder.ps1 (no follow-on executions observed). New installer host 185.241.211[.]197 (attributed by Huntress to 1337 Services GmbH, a bulletproof hoster); installer marker now c=ExploitedNOTUSA; also Exploited529 and Exploited809 (190 downstream clients from a single panel). Wave 1 total stands at 288 hosts across 48 organisations. Huntress still has not observed the technician-takeover succeeding in recovered host logs — the spread is a designed capability, not a confirmed infection path. Microsoft stresses removing the rogue client alone does not remove persistence or invalidate exposed web sessions, and gives Defender XDR hunting queries. Critique: the wave-2 count is a separate Huntress observation; do not add it to the wave-1 total, since overlap between the waves is not established. The campaign is now in its second wave with no sign of stopping, and the entry route is still unconfirmed.

Unknown actor — N-able 4EverAble2Shell / Agent2Shell

RMM platform used by MSPs serving small banks and financial firms

TTPs
MITRE IDs from a secondary summary of the chains (SockAnalyst), not vendor-published: T1190, T1072, T1136, T1552, T1496, T1068. 4EverAble2Shell: unauthenticated RCE as root on the N-central server, chaining Apache Axis CVE-2026-90436 (9.8) with a separate N-central privilege escalation (no CVE yet; abuses the privileged setLDFileOwner helper and a symlink to make a root-owned script writable, then invokes serverControl). Requires the Legacy API to be enabled (non-default). Agent2Shell: chains 10+ flaws to run commands as SYSTEM on managed Windows agents; also requires the Legacy API. PoCs published 12 Nov by Sean Bales (repo under the renamed account MSPNightmare/CVEs), tested against Hotfix 2 (2026.4.1.26).
Impact
Exploitation confirmed by researchers, contested by the vendor — flag, do not resolve. SecurityWeek (13 Nov 07:20 UTC / 15:20 SGT) reports the chain was exploited within about five hours of the PoC: Huntback counted 21 production N-central servers mining XMRig, 19 of them on the Hotfix 2 build, across the US (9), South Africa (4), Canada (4), UK, Germany, France and Sweden; the miner uses about half the CPU cores and the payload is pulled by curl from 157.20.241[.]16. N-able’s 13 Nov update (03:50 UTC / 11:50 SGT) says it has identified additional IOCs and is working with specific affected customers, has turned off the Intune integration as a precaution and restricted the Legacy API — but N-able has not publicly published the IP/domain/hash IOCs itself (the IOCs in the Indicators section come from researchers), and its 12 Nov text still said it had “seen no signs of compromise”. There is still no fix for the new chains; a follow-up hotfix is planned. N-able says hosted N-central is not affected and servers on HF2 with Legacy API disabled are not exposed. Previdian warns the public PoC is unverified and may contain malware. Critique: the two accounts cannot both be complete — either the mining servers are compromised through this chain and the vendor has not confirmed them, or the miners arrived another way. Either way, Hotfix 2 is explicitly not sufficient, and the Legacy API is the control that matters.

Unknown actor — State Bank of India

India’s largest bank; Global IT Centre, CBD Belapur, Navi Mumbai

TTPs
No MITRE mapping published. No intrusion detail published. Hackers using the name “Siriusim” allegedly accessed the bank’s Global IT Centre server on 9 Oct at about 16:13 and extracted data until about 18:00 on 15 Oct. The attribution to “Siriusim” is a claim in the police complaint, not confirmed by the bank.
Impact
Confirmed as an incident by the bank (stock-exchange filing, 21 Oct) and by police (FIR registered 12 Nov; press reports 13 Nov). The stolen set was about 375,000 records — 32 CSV files from the YONO 2.0 and RAAS applications, plus JSON files and 2,721 PDF files — and was posted on the dark web. The breach was detected 16 Oct. SBI’s filing says it was alerted 16 Oct by its threat-intelligence provider, the Defence Cyber Agency and CERT-In, and that preliminary analysis of the dark-web records identified 3,897 customer records containing personal data (names, mobile numbers, dates of birth, gender, email, address, branch/CIF and loan or lead metadata). The bank says core operations were not materially affected and it has engaged a CERT-In-empanelled agency for an independent assessment. Police say SBI told them the data had been removed from the dark web and had not been misused — that claim is unverified in the reporting. Critique: the 375,000 figure is the size of the dark-web dataset, not a confirmed count of breached customers — do not present it as such. The one-month gap between detection (16 Oct) and the police complaint (12 Nov) is unexplained, and removal from one dark-web posting does not prove the data was not copied or sold first. The bank says no account numbers, balances, passwords, one-time codes, card data, PAN or Aadhaar were in the identified records, which limits direct account-takeover risk but leaves the confirmed personal data as live impersonation material.

Unknown actor — KB Capital

Capital-finance arm of KB Financial Group, South Korea; vehicle lease asset management system

TTPs
No MITRE mapping published. Intrusion method and attacker unidentified. The company detected repeated abnormal information lookups from a domestic IP address; the IP itself was not disclosed.
Impact

Critique: direct account-takeover risk is limited, but staff and partner account details are impersonation and business-email-compromise material — and the pattern (an internal, non-transaction system reached from a domestic IP) echoes the FSI breach the day before, though no link has been established.

Unknown actor — Crédit Mutuel Arkéa

Cooperative banking group, France

TTPs
No MITRE mapping published. New detail in-window (13 Nov, AFP interview with chair Julien Carmona, reported via Boursorama): the attackers created fake IBANs, including from the group’s online bank Fortuneo, to trigger payment orders from Arkéa’s own account through instant-payment systems — calling it a “heist of a new kind” and the first successful fraudulent attack of its kind against a French or European bank. The chair said the attackers took material from a hacked supplier to get into the bank, and that the group worked from 1 November to make the illegitimate transfers appear legitimate. The bank detected the abnormal transactions on 3 November; Fortuneo’s systems and customers were not affected. The service provider’s identity is still undisclosed.
Impact
Confirmed. The mechanism is now described, but the loss figure is still not final: the bank has validated only the order of magnitude of the reported $133 million (about €118 million) figure, and says losses were lower than that figure. A portion of funds was blocked and recovery efforts are under way; on 6 November the confederation president said part of the missing money had already been recovered, without saying how much. Critique: the fake-IBAN/instant-payment mechanism is the new, material fact — it is a third-party-platform-to-bank-account route, not a customer-account compromise, and it is exactly the pattern peers should be checking their own instant-payment controls against. The final amount and the service provider’s identity remain undisclosed, and the “hacked supplier” detail comes from the chair’s interview as re-reported, not from a bank statement — treat the provider’s own breach status as still unconfirmed.

Supply chain & third-party risk

Trezor Safe 5 hardware implants — NEW (confirmed, 13 Nov)

Trezor confirmed three Safe 5 devices sold by Southeast Asian reseller CryptoBilis contained malicious hardware implants — an extra circuit board designed to capture the wallet recovery phrase from the screen and exfiltrate it over a cellular network. All three were bought from CryptoBilis between 20 Aug and 23 Sep 2026; 48 CryptoBilis customers have contacted Trezor since its 30 Oct warning. Firmware 2.13.1 (released 11 Nov) stops the implants found so far from capturing anything, but cannot protect phrases already captured. No affected customer has reported lost funds. Trezor has cut its reseller network by about 80%.

Critique: a crypto-custody supply-chain case, not a bank breach — but the pattern (a trusted reseller channel compromised, a firmware fix that cannot undo already-captured secrets) is the same one banks face with any third-party hardware or software in the custody chain. Clients holding crypto should be told to buy only from the vendor directly and to treat any recovery phrase entered on a reseller-bought device as compromised.

Indian NBFC payout APIs — “Crafty Falcon” fraud wave — NEW (confirmed reporting, 13 Nov)

Cybercriminals exploited the payout APIs of Indian non-banking financial companies, with three Telangana lenders reporting 304 fraudulent transactions between 19 October and 6 November: VIVIFI Indian Finance detected 183 fraudulent transactions worth Rs 3.34 crore within 88 minutes on 19 October; Spandana Sphoorthy Financial reported 82 transactions amounting to Rs 7.8 crore on 1 November; Keerthana Finserv detected 39 transactions worth around Rs 7 crore on 6 November. Similar attacks were reported in six other states and Union territories (Karnataka, Maharashtra, Delhi, Chandigarh, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry), with cumulative losses across India estimated in the hundreds of crores of rupees — an estimate, not a confirmed total. Siphoned funds were diverted through 50–60 bank accounts in each case before being transferred to gaming and betting applications, including Dafabet, 1xBet, WinZO, Betway, My11Circle and Bet365. The Telangana Cyber Security Bureau attributes the exposure to exposed testing environments, outdated APIs, credentials embedded in source code and partner tokens that remain valid for too long. CERT-In’s advisory of 4 November flagged an AI-assisted campaign designated “CRAFTY FALCON” involving exposed testing systems, shared credentials, Rs 1 test transfers and unauthorised payouts, and repeat attacks were reported where remediation was incomplete.

Critique: whether AI was used in the Telangana incidents remains under examination, and the attackers have not been identified — traced IP addresses in six countries do not by themselves establish the attackers’ location. What is established is the pattern: exposed test environments, long-lived partner tokens and Rs 1 test transfers are the early warning, and the same CERT-In advisory already covered this campaign on 4 November. The Rs 1 test-transfer pattern is a concrete detection signal for fraud teams either way.

MEXC Global cut off from the US financial system — NEW (FinCEN order)

The US Treasury’s Financial Crimes Enforcement Network has cut crypto exchange MEXC Global off from the US financial system over sanctions evasion and cybercrime ties. Critique: this is an enforcement action, not a breach — but a $16bn illicit-flow figure attached to an exchange that banks’ customers use is a screening and de-risking signal, and Binance is already rejecting MEXC-linked transfers.

AI in the current threat landscape

The three AI roles are kept separate. Vendor forecasts are marked as forecasts.

AI as a target

Railway AI-secrets breach — confirmed (via SentinelOne roundup, 13 Nov). Cloud host Railway confirmed attackers escaped build-filesystem isolation on 20 Oct via an unknown flaw and stole decrypted customer auth secrets, including AI-provider API keys — likely for resale or model distillation — hitting at most about 1.85% of workspaces (about 100,000 environments) and linked-account tokens for 1,852 users. About $740,000 of unauthorised AI usage followed. No customer databases were accessed and no private Git repositories were downloaded; tokens were rotated and Railway will cover unreimbursed fraudulent charges. Attackers used autonomous AI agents in a 26-hour contest with defenders.

ARTEX agents hunting AI keys (Talos, 13 Nov). Unattributed agents searched public GitHub for credentials and AI API keys and, in one case, moved from GitHub search to Azure Key Vault exfiltration within five minutes. Datadog-observed ARTEX activity in AWS included compromised long-term keys, public Cognito pools and CloudWatch searches for financial information and secrets.

wfx-loot (status update). The CI secret stealer continued to harvest AI service keys (OpenAI, Anthropic, Cursor, Copilot) from compromised repositories — see the supply-chain section.

AI as a weapon or accelerator

ARTEX — the item of the day (FBI FLASH + Talos, 13 Nov). An open-source, LLM-driven agentic pentesting framework is being used in live intrusions since at least August 2026, letting low-skill actors run complex multi-target campaigns at machine speed — some intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. Financial services are explicitly named among the targeted sectors. Co-deployed tooling includes AntiFox, CyberStrikeAI and PentAGI. Talos’s forecast — marked as a forecast — is that future chains will likely culminate in autonomous data exfiltration and ransomware activation.

KYC-deepfake campaign (Amazon MadPot, 13 Nov). Amazon Threat Intelligence’s companion honeypot post captured ARTEX AI-agent requests including AI-credential theft and a KYC-deepfake campaign. This is a honeypot observation, not a confirmed bank-targeted campaign — do not present it as one.

No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a named bank was found in this window beyond the MadPot observation above. The FBI’s FaceStealer announcement ($400m+, 900+ webcam videos) is 12 November reporting, already covered in the 13 November edition; its 13 November coverage adds no new facts.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

AI agents as a new attack surface — research walkthrough, not a real incident (Token Security, via BleepingComputer, 13 Nov). Researchers showed how an AI invoice-processing agent could be tricked by hidden text in an email into changing vendor bank details in an ERP system — a hypothetical walkthrough of five enterprise security gaps in agentic systems, not a real incident. It is usable as a control lesson, not as evidence of an attack: firms deploying invoice or payment agents should treat agent tool calls (especially bank-detail changes) as privileged actions with human approval, and should test for prompt injection through email and document content.

CSA Singapore (13 Nov) says AI is letting attackers find and exploit weaknesses faster — the same acceleration theme as the ARTEX reporting, stated by a regulator rather than a vendor.

Taiwan’s FSC has issued an agentic-AI security advisory for the sector (covered in the 13 November edition); no new development in this window. Railway’s 26-hour contest between autonomous attacker agents and defenders is a live example of AI-versus-AI incident response, and of the detection-time compression banks should plan for.
