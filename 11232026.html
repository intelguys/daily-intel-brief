Cleverbridge — payment processor breached; card numbers and CVVs copied (NEW, confirmed, 22 Nov)

Cleverbridge, which processes checkout, payments and subscriptions for software and SaaS vendors, disclosed on 22 November that hackers breached its systems and copied payment card data stored on its platform. It detected unauthorised activity on 4 November, took systems offline the same day and restored them the same day; its investigation later determined a third party had copied the data. A breach notification shared with BleepingComputer says the stolen data includes the card number, expiry date and card security code (CVV/CVC) used for a purchase through Cleverbridge, and that no other personal data such as name, contact or billing details was copied. The company has not said how attackers got in, how long they were inside, or how many customers were affected, and has not disclosed which of its listed clients’ buyers were hit — its client list includes Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf, Sony and Parallels. It confirmed the incident was not ransomware and nothing was encrypted. It has notified data protection authorities, law enforcement, payment card networks and payment partners, and is working with TransUnion’s Cyberscout on notifications and complimentary credit monitoring. Malwarebytes is the only merchant so far to confirm impact, saying only “a small subset” of its customers was affected. Issuing banks should watch for unauthorised transactions and consider replacing affected cards; breach-themed phishing using the Cleverbridge name is a predictable follow-on.

KEPCO — bank and card data of about 650,000 customers leaked in a second breach (NEW, confirmed, 22 Nov)

State-run Korea Electric Power Corp. confirmed personal data of about 1.52 million people leaked from two systems: 650,487 users of its KEPCO ON customer-service and bill-payment platform and 869,761 users of its electronic procurement system (SRM), for a combined 1,520,248 records. The KEPCO ON leak exposed 12 categories including names, resident registration numbers, phone numbers, addresses, bank names, account numbers, account-holder names, card issuers, card numbers and cardholder names, plus billing records. The SRM leak exposed corporate representatives’ and employees’ names, phone numbers and emails. KEPCO says an external attacker accessed both systems; it discovered the breach on 20 November, blocked external IP access and suspended some services, filed with the Personal Information Protection Commission on 22 November (within 72 hours), and is working with financial companies to prevent secondary damage, with a 24-hour call centre staffed. Its president issued a public apology. This is a second breach in as many months, after an October leak of employee records. The victim is an energy utility, not a bank, but the exposed data is financial data: Korean banks should expect phishing and voice-phishing using it. Per-system counts differ slightly between outlets (650,487 vs 654,487 KEPCO ON users); the totals here are Yonhap’s and the Korea Herald’s.

PLAID KARTE — the 754-company key exposure is the consequential detail; per-company data from tomorrow (UPDATE)

Plaid’s 20 November investigation results, now corroborated by multiple outlets, put the leaked or potentially leaked data in four overlapping groups: integration credentials (secret keys and access tokens) for 754 client companies; part of the data customers linked into KARTE for 570 companies; end-user behaviour data for 487 companies; and cached data treated as potentially leaked for up to 409 companies (an estimate, because the cache is overwritten quickly and Plaid cannot tell which items were actually read). Plaid also confirmed 79,249 KARTE account email addresses and 45,816 sales/support contact records leaked. The 754-company credential figure is the most consequential for financial firms: integration keys held by a marketing vendor can reach email delivery, Salesforce and other connected services, and only the issuing service can revoke them. Plaid warned that unrevoked keys could allow unintended data writes or leaks at linked services; as of 20 November no misuse of the keys at linked services had been disclosed. The breach also reached companies whose contracts had ended — one former customer, overflow, ended its contract in November 2023 yet its data remained for about three years — the same post-contract retention problem seen with Stripe International and Kaitec. New company counts in the window: JINS, about 18.29 million member records (member IDs, email addresses, surnames, dates of birth; no passwords or card data), and the MUFG group total of about 8 million records across its five businesses. Published counts across company disclosures still top 140 million records — a tally of company disclosures, not 140 million people. KARTE stays offline with a staged restart targeted for mid-January 2027; Plaid will start giving each affected company its detailed data from 24 November.

Rackspace Spot — leaked credentials let intruders read customer Kubernetes Secrets (NEW, confirmed, 21 Nov)

Attackers used leaked credentials to access Rackspace Spot, a managed Kubernetes cloud platform, and could act as legitimate users inside some customer organisations — creating pods, reading Kubernetes Secrets (database passwords, API keys, tokens), and listing nodes and volumes. Any Secret in an affected user’s clusters, plus Spot API tokens and refresh tokens, could have been read. The intrusion ran 17 November 00:20–22:00 UTC; Rackspace discovered it on 18 November after a customer report and disclosed it to affected customers on 21 November. Rackspace revoked affected tokens and sessions, removed unauthorised pods, rotated keys and hardened access controls; the Spot admin console was down for days during clean-up, delaying customer credential rotation, and new sign-ups are paused until 25 November. Rackspace has not said whose credentials leaked or how, how many customers were affected, or whether data was copied out; attribution is unknown. Affected customers must treat every secret in hit clusters as compromised. IOCs: IP 23.224.79.106; range 23.224.0.0/15; suspicious pod/object name prefixes i1, glm-, probe.

Visa Fall 2026 Biannual Threats Report — third-party incidents up 62% (CATCH-UP, confirmed primary, 17 Nov)

AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — kept separate, with forecasts marked as forecasts.

AI as a target

Dragos canary research — prompt theft from inside AI inference providers (NEW, confirmed — Dragos, 19 Nov; OpenRouter action, 22 Nov). Dragos seeded about two million prompts with unique canary URLs and credentials between July and November 2026 and watched where they surfaced. Three findings: attackers were actively stealing data from inside at least three AI inference providers — the earliest breach began mid-July and none of the three knew until Dragos told them; marketplace resellers could view and retain customer prompts using standard logging, with no exploit needed; and providers appeared not to honour privacy settings — prompts sent to US providers were transmitted to Chinese AI organisations, and one seeded link was posted into a Feishu company chat 24 days later and opened within two minutes from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. In the largest breach, a couple of hundred planted AWS keys were stolen in late-October windows (every request in those windows, including requests the provider rejected before a model ran); the thief tested keys within hours, listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, tried creating Bedrock API keys and adding IAM users, driving tens of thousands of AWS API calls. OpenRouter removed three providers on 22 November, removed 125 model endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility (some as a precaution), and notified potentially affected customers. Provider notices: Phala (10 November) — an attacker took control of an inference server; requests to DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 routed via OpenRouter 21 October 00:00 UTC to 8 November 05:35 UTC should be treated as exposed, all secrets rotated; contained 8 November. Together AI (21 November) — prompt samples kept in a private Hugging Face repository for testing were downloaded by an unauthorised party after an AI coding agent uploaded the repository credential to a public repo; five tested credentials were Dragos canaries. Novita (22 November) — some MiniMax requests from 28 May to 11 November were forwarded to MiniMax’s official API instead of Novita-hosted infrastructure. Whether the three providers Dragos caught are the same three OpenRouter removed is unconfirmed — neither side has named them; do not present them as identical. Operation ArgonHunt (CATCH-UP, reported — SOCRadar, first reported 17 Nov, re-reported 20 Nov): an attacker staging server serving its /tmp directory over HTTP exposed about 700 MB / 1,600+ files, including an automated pipeline harvesting AI API keys — 44,096 unique keys across Gemini (26,633), OpenAI (14,504), OpenRouter, Anthropic and xAI, of which the operator’s own rechecks marked 2,686 as active, plus 519 Google Cloud tokens and sniffed live service-account tokens. The same operator compromised an unnamed Türkiye-based crypto exchange (see Could not verify). The open item stays LiteLLM CVE-2026-40217 (KEV 18 Nov, due 2 December): no new development in-window.

AI as a weapon or accelerator

Ukraine / FBI arrests (NEW, confirmed — announced 18–19 Nov, catch-up). AI-generated voice calls posing as IT support were the initial-access method for a ransomware organisation that took more than $40.5 million from at least 28 companies. Cairn / MONAI Label (UPDATE, confirmed — Amazon, 20 Nov). The zero-day the agents found autonomously now has a CVE number, CVE-2026-133968 (9.8), with still no patched release. AI-agent exploit development on ruby2shell (NEW, confirmed repository — genaforvena, 20 Nov). An autonomous AI agent (OpenCode, about €500 credit budget) took the one-byte Ruby heap overflow CVE-2026-132825 from crash reproduction to tcache poisoning in a single session, then to what the repo grades as rung 5 (working remote command execution) under lab conditions — one multipart POST driving a forged CRuby T_DATA finalizer, with an independent clean-archive replay. The repo itself states the limits plainly: it needs custom grooming/GC middleware plus heap oracles, ASLR stays on, and “stock-app oracle-free RCE/persistence are not claimed”. The same repo grades PHP2Shell at rung 4 (demonstrated impact, no RCE). No complete stock-Rails RCE chain is published; Veria Labs’ own PoC is still due about 25 November. The defensive read is that AI agents can now do hands-on exploit development on a critical flaw in a banking-stack language within days of disclosure — the window between patch release and public exploit technique is compressing. That is not evidence of in-the-wild exploitation (none reported). No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a named bank was found in-window. Unit 42’s Com vishing campaign is conventional vishing and is not labelled an AI campaign by Unit 42 — do not re-label it.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

No new MAS, FCA/BoE/PRA, EU or US AI-in-finance governance development appeared in-window. The control problem this window sharpens is concrete and comes from the target-side findings above: third-party AI inference supply chains (routers, resellers, marketplaces) can see, retain and leak prompts and credentials despite Zero Data Retention flags — the flag binds only the first hop — and AI provider keys are being harvested and validated at scale. Banks should inventory which third-party inference providers and routers handle prompts containing customer data or credentials, verify privacy settings actually change routing, prefer direct first-party providers or self-hosting for sensitive workloads, and seed canary credentials in AI pipelines as tripwires (Dragos’ own recommendation). The other control lessons carry: exposed AI gateways concentrate provider API keys (LiteLLM, carried); agent frameworks can be left publicly accessible without authentication (Cairn lesson, carried — the operator’s own control panel was exposed); and “agentic” labels from vendors are not evidence — ask what the agent did, with what tools, and what it could reach (Bell Canada, carried — the “agentic” claim rests on one word from Bell with no published evidence).
