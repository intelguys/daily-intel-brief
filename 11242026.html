CERT-In CIAD-2026-0062 — payment-workflow abuse and agentic offensive frameworks (CATCH-UP, confirmed advisory, 20 Nov; coverage 23 Nov)

CERT-In’s critical advisory “Abuse of Business Payment Workflows and Use of AI-Driven Agentic Offensive Frameworks across Sectors” warns that attackers alter business records or approval arrangements, then use otherwise legitimate payment processes for unauthorised transactions — affecting BFSI entities and technology service providers supporting government-linked payment processes and vendor/partner payout platforms. It also reports AI-driven agentic offensive frameworks used across sectors for automated reconnaissance, credential attacks, rapid exploit preparation, parallel multi-agent reconnaissance/privilege discovery and adaptive script rewriting; in the financially motivated campaign, agentic tooling compressed initial access to impact “from days to a few hours”. AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — kept separate, with forecasts marked as forecasts.

AI as a target

OpenRouter names the three removed providers (UPDATE, confirmed — BleepingComputer, 23 Nov). OpenRouter removed Together AI, Phala and Novita for violating stated data-handling policies and exposing sensitive data, and revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints. A fourth provider, Atlas Cloud, said it would voluntarily withdraw its models from OpenRouter pending security testing, following a third-party security incident. This resolves yesterday’s open question — whether the three providers Dragos caught are the same three OpenRouter removed: they are, exactly the three whose incident notices were published (Together AI, 21 November; Phala, 10 November; Novita, 22 November). The provider-specific exposure windows are unchanged and were confirmed again in this report: Together — prompt samples from June–July requests to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, Kimi K2.6, exposed after an AI coding agent uploaded a repository credential to a public repo; Phala — DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests via OpenRouter 21 October 00:00 UTC to 8 November 05:35 UTC; Novita — MiniMax requests misrouted to MiniMax’s official API 28 May to 11 November. OpenRouter says there was no breach of its own systems, it is adapting Dragos’ canary tests for ongoing checks, and Zero Data Retention eligibility will require validation before restoration. Banks routing prompts containing customer data or credentials through OpenRouter or third-party inference providers should treat requests to these providers in the stated windows as exposed and rotate secrets; the Zero Data Retention flag binds only the first hop. The open item stays LiteLLM CVE-2026-40217 (KEV 18 November, due 2 December): no new development in-window. Operation ArgonHunt (carried, reported — SOCRadar, 17/20 November): 44,096 stolen AI API keys stockpiled and validated automatically; no follow-up in-window.

AI as a weapon or accelerator

Wiz “Agentic Attacks in the Wild” (NEW, confirmed — Wiz, 23 Nov). Explosive growth in successful agentic attacks in the last three weeks — 20 additional vendor-disclosed incidents since the Black October post, and Wiz’s incident-response team has seen a 3x increase in engagement requests. The ARTEX-Harvester campaign (late October to mid-November, 12+ organisations at varying confidence) used a distinctive boto3 build, incrementing Intent IDs and proxy-pool overlap; almost every request used a different residential or ISP proxy exit — over 220 IPs in a single target. One success: compromised version-control credentials, abuse of CI workload-identity trust, secrets and Terraform state exfiltrated; a stolen service-account token from an application pod created privileged pods and deployed a node-level collector that harvested kubelet PKI and every projected service-account token, one of which (cert-manager) could read ArgoCD secrets, leading to cluster-admin. A separate anonymised case: CyberStrikeAI ran the operation across two AWS accounts and seven EKS clusters over four days — Anthropic API keys harvested from 50 hosts, employees’ AI coding-assistant session histories mined for production SSO credentials, forged admin tokens giving access to 3,392 users and live session tokens, 50 SSH private keys from a decrypted bastion database. ARTEX’s web console ships exposed to the internet by default, often on default credentials — Censys counted more than 1,400 exposed consoles in early November — and the exposed operator directories reveal prompts, planner intents and agent transcripts. Wiz notes the attacks are noisy and self-labelling: pentest, probe, recon user agents, pentest-* workspaces and pods, incrementing pentest-iNNNNNN task counters — detections should key on that behaviour, not on IP lists, since IP-based blocking does very little against rotating residential proxies. CERT-In CIAD-2026-0062 (see Supply chain). Agentic frameworks used for automated reconnaissance, credential attacks, rapid exploit preparation, parallel multi-agent reconnaissance/privilege discovery and adaptive script rewriting; initial access to impact compressed from days to hours. MONAI Label 0.8.6 (CORRECTION — see Vulnerability watchlist). The patch shipped on 22 November; the Cairn precedent stands — the first publicly documented case of offensive AI agents finding a novel vulnerability during a live intrusion. AI-agent exploit development (genaforvena, 24 Nov 00:54–01:02 UTC). Three new commits extended the Mishe agent’s work to sdlmixer2shell, munt2shell and libvips2shell — new families, no new RCE claim; low BFSI relevance (audio emulator and image libraries, not banking-stack components); Veria Labs’ own ruby2shell PoC still due about 25 November. No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a named bank was found in-window. Unit 42’s legal-themed vishing (see Emerging fraud) is conventional vishing and is not labelled an AI campaign by Unit 42 — do not re-label it.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

No new MAS, FCA/BoE/PRA, EU or US AI-in-finance governance development appeared in-window. The control problem this window sharpens is concrete and comes from the target-side findings above: third-party AI inference supply chains (routers, resellers, marketplaces) can see, retain and leak prompts and credentials despite Zero Data Retention flags; AI provider keys are being harvested and validated at scale (see Wiz — Anthropic keys from 50 hosts); and agentic frameworks label their own activity in user agents, resource names and session names, so agent-tool detection should hunt for those markers rather than relying on IP blocklists. Banks should inventory which third-party inference providers and routers handle prompts containing customer data or credentials, verify privacy settings actually change routing, prefer direct first-party providers or self-hosting for sensitive workloads, and seed canary credentials in AI pipelines as tripwires (Dragos’ own recommendation). The other control lessons carry: exposed AI gateways concentrate provider API keys (LiteLLM, carried); agent frameworks can be left publicly accessible without authentication (Cairn lesson, carried — the operator’s own control panel was exposed); and “agentic” labels from vendors are not evidence — ask what the agent did, with what tools, and what it could reach (Bell Canada, carried — the “agentic” claim rests on one word from Bell with no published evidence).
