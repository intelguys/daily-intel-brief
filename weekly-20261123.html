1. Plaid KARTE — one marketing platform, about 2,200 companies' data

What happened. Plaid's KARTE customer-experience platform was attacked on 13 November: a vulnerability behind a public data-retrieval route was exploited, then stolen credentials were used to reach the KARTE admin console. Plaid's investigation was completed on 19 November and published on 20 November. It found data from about 2,200 companies leaked or potentially leaked, in four overlapping groups: integration credentials (secret keys and access tokens) for 754 client companies; part of the data customers had linked into KARTE for 570 companies; end-user behaviour data for 487 companies; and cached data treated as potentially leaked for up to 409 companies — an estimate, because the cache is overwritten quickly and Plaid cannot tell which items were actually read. No attribution has been published. KARTE stays offline with a staged restart targeted for mid-January 2027; per-company data handovers begin 24 November; Plaid forecasts a ¥2 billion loss.

Company disclosures stacked up all week. JINS disclosed about 18.29 million member accounts on 17 November. MUFG Bank disclosed about 6.5 million records on 18 November (the wider MUFG group totalled about 8 million records across five businesses — a different scope, not a conflict). SMBC disclosed up to about 14 million records on 20 November — the largest single-bank figure, and a maximum, not a confirmed count; the exposed fields were date of birth, age band and sex, with no names, contacts or account numbers. ZOZO disclosed about 29.18 million records and Marui Group about 17.33 million (including about 8.5 million EPOS card holders) on 20 November. DHC disclosed about 19.11 million and Sharp up to 3.26 million on 18–19 November; the Bank of Yokohama disclosed about 116,500. Published counts across company disclosures passed 140 million records — a tally of company disclosures with overlaps, not 140 million people: the same person can appear in several companies' counts, and no deduplicated count of affected people has been published. The breach also reached companies whose contracts had ended: Stripe International's data remained after it ended KARTE in May 2026, and one former customer, overflow, ended its contract in November 2023 yet its data remained for about three years. (Separately, the SMBC Consumer Finance incident, up to 320,118 people, is a different incident and is not part of the KARTE total.)

Why it matters strategically. The record counts are the least important number. The consequential detail is the 754 companies whose integration credentials leaked: each must check whether its keys were misused at linked services — none disclosed as of 20 November — and rotate them, which turns a marketing-platform breach into a credential incident for hundreds of firms at once. The second stage is built in: the per-company handovers starting 24 November will produce a further wave of disclosures, and KARTE staying offline until mid-January 2027 prolongs the disruption for firms that built customer journeys on it. The post-contract retention finding is a vendor-management lesson in itself: data held after a contract ends is data still at risk, and former clients must now ask what a supplier kept. For banks, the SMBC and MUFG disclosures show how a non-banking supplier can put bank customer data in a breach without touching a core banking system.

2. Rakuten Securities — up to about 15.7 million accounts, regulator now involved

What happened. Rakuten Securities disclosed on 17 November that customer data held in a test-environment system may have leaked; the leak was confirmed on 18 November. Up to about 11.25 million accounts' login IDs were exposed, and up to about 4.47 million accounts had names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, employers, withdrawal bank accounts, declared income and financial-asset data exposed — the two groups do not overlap, so the maximum is about 15.72 million accounts. The company detected possible unauthorised access on 16 November and found the test-environment data may have leaked on 11 November; the cause is under investigation. No passwords or PINs are confirmed leaked and no unauthorised transactions attributable to the incident have been confirmed. Japan's Financial Services Agency issued a reporting order on 19 November; Financial Services Minister Satsuki Katayama addressed the leak on 20 November and the FSA will consider a business improvement order if information-management flaws are found. Rakuten Bank shares fell about 15% on 18 November. A lawyer-led class-action recruitment site passed 20,000 registrants by 14:00 JST on 20 November. A fake "compensation policy" screenshot is circulating; Rakuten has announced no compensation, and contacts asking for lawsuit fees are fake.

Why it matters strategically. This is the week's clearest example of a test environment becoming a production-grade incident: the data was real customer data sitting in a system that was evidently not treated as production for security purposes. For the sector, the FSA's reporting order and the prospect of a business improvement order signal that Japanese regulators are moving from incident response to examining information management across the sector — and the 20,000-strong class-action recruitment within three days shows how fast customer-trust damage now converts into legal exposure. The share-price fall shows the market is pricing that in the same week, not later.

3. n0n publishes Visa, JPMorgan Chase and Mastercard customer data — via a shared supplier, reported

What happened. After its 48-hour deadline set on 16 November expired, the n0n group published customer marketing data attributed to all three firms on 19 November. JPMorgan confirmed third-party service providers were targeted and marketing data that may include customer names and email addresses was accessed; Visa confirmed an incident at an Asia-Pacific third-party provider with its own systems unaffected; Mastercard confirmed a limited exposure from one benefit programme. The published sets hold about 16,000 JPMorgan Reserve customer email addresses (plus 124 employee emails), over 6,000 Visa premium customers' details in Asia-Pacific, and about 2,000 alleged Mastercard entries that researchers say look like test data rather than genuine customer records. No card numbers, passwords or balances are in the published sets. BleepingComputer reported on 20 November that n0n claims it attacked data-platform provider Qubole on 11 November and used compromised Qubole credentials to reach customers' AWS environments; Qubole has not responded and none of the three firms has named a vendor, so that link stays reported, not confirmed. Bitdefender's controlling caveat stands: 100+ n0n listings against just over 20 real targets — the group inflates victim counts.

Why it matters strategically. Three of the world's largest financial brands were hit in one publication, and none of them was the direct target — the entry point, on the reported account, was a shared data-platform supplier whose credentials opened customers' cloud environments. That is the supplier-concentration pattern in its purest form: a vendor that can assume cloud roles across many clients is a single point of failure for all of them, and the question every bank should now ask of every such vendor is what customer data it holds, which cloud roles it can assume, and whether those roles are still needed. The data itself was marketing data, not payment data, but premium-customer lists (Reserve, premium APAC) are high-value material for targeted phishing and impersonation — the harm is downstream, not in the leak itself. The Mastercard set being likely test data is also a useful discipline: not everything a ransomware group publishes is what it claims to be.

4. Korea — the FSI audit becomes a sector-wide inspection, then another securities firm is breached

What happened. The Financial Security Institute (FSI) breach set the sequence in motion before this window: a webshell attack at 13:54 on 9 November failed, and an SMS-authentication bypass was then used to create a fake FSS-level account. The Financial Services Commission (FSC) gave that timeline to the National Assembly on 13 November, and its special audit of the FSI began on 16 November. The FSI's evaluation system was still shut down at the end of the window, with no restoration date, and 2,912 firms form the assessment population whose appeals are blocked. Reporting put the wider Korean wave at 37 firms and about 473,000 records in one month.

On 17 November the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) called in CISOs and security officers from 227 electronic-finance and 47 online investment-linked firms — 274 firms in total — for an intensive external-attack-surface inspection, with first-round self-assessments due 30 November. The same day it disclosed new incidents at Spoon (Giftiell, about 18,000 people's data) and Zoomfund (about KRW 300 million in customer deposits), and began surprise on-site inspections of high-risk branches at four banks, the first in about ten years. The FSC will require large firms to run vulnerability assessments twice a year instead of once, and to identify and classify all IT assets.

The wave kept producing victims: Coinone's breach via its customer-support contractor MetaM (disclosed 15 November; passwords, resident registration numbers, bank account numbers and wallet addresses may have leaked; victim count unconfirmed; disclosure took about 70 hours 40 minutes); KOMSCO's chak gift-certificate platform breach (3,083 customers confirmed); and Mirae Asset Life Insurance (10,418 victims as of 5 November, up from about 6,800 — crossing the 10,000-person 72-hour reporting duty). The KB Kookmin count was revised from 119 to 2,210 after FSS inspection. Then, on 23 November, Samsung Securities disclosed that an unauthorised outsider accessed its Voice of Customer complaints system — traces found on the afternoon of 21 November — and exfiltrated attachments from 8,241 customers across 13 fields, including name, account number, address, contact details and driver's licence number. Only customers who had filed through the complaints system are affected; trading and payment systems are not reported breached. The firm blocked the IP, suspended the service, reported to the FSS and the Korea Internet & Security Agency, and its chief executive, Park Jong-moon, apologised; it says it will compensate customers if actual loss is confirmed. The firm is described as another Korean securities firm in the current breach wave, not numbered, because the other firms are not named in the wire entry or any briefing in the window.

Why it matters strategically. One shared assessment body fails, so the regulator cannot trust the sector's own evaluation system — and responds by inspecting the sector itself, on a deadline. That is the regulatory amplifier in action: a single incident at the FSI became a 274-firm inspection with a 30 November deadline, twice-yearly assessments for large firms, and branch inspections not seen in a decade. Each new disclosure widens the same lesson: the entry point is rarely the core banking system. It is a support contractor (MetaM), a gift-certificate platform, or a customer-facing complaints form. The 8,241 Samsung Securities complainants now hold a verified customer list for impersonation calls and phishing citing the incident — the follow-on fraud risk is the live exposure, not the breach itself. Small and mid-sized operators are the FSS's stated concern: intrusion attempts via external contact points continue there.

5. KEPCO — a utility breach whose exposed data is financial data

What happened. State-run Korea Electric Power Corp. confirmed on 22 November that personal data of about 1.52 million people leaked from two systems: 650,487 users of its KEPCO ON customer-service and bill-payment platform and 869,761 users of its electronic procurement system (SRM), for a combined 1,520,248 records. The KEPCO ON leak exposed 12 categories including names, resident registration numbers, phone numbers, addresses, bank names, account numbers, account-holder names, card issuers, card numbers and cardholder names, plus billing records; the SRM leak exposed corporate representatives' and employees' names, phone numbers and emails. KEPCO says an external attacker accessed both systems; it discovered the breach on 20 November, blocked external IP access and suspended some services, filed with the Personal Information Protection Commission on 22 November, and is working with financial companies to prevent secondary damage. Its president issued a public apology. This is a second breach in as many months, after an October leak of employee records. Per-system counts differ slightly between outlets (650,487 vs 654,487 KEPCO ON users) — neither figure is chosen here.

Why it matters strategically. KEPCO is an energy utility, not a bank, but the data it lost is bank and card data — account numbers and card numbers for about 650,000 bill-payment users. For Korean banks, this is a fraud problem arriving from outside the sector: phishing and voice-phishing using verified bank and card details, against customers who may never connect the call to a utility breach. It also shows the limits of sector-based risk assessment — a firm's BFSI relevance is set by the data it holds, not the industry it belongs to. A second breach in as many months raises the harder question of whether the first incident's remediation held.

6. Paydon / Infcurion — a payments platform halted across four banks

What happened. Infcurion, the contractor running the Wallet Station payment platform behind Japan's Paydon app, confirmed unauthorised access on 19 November. A total of 251,172 records leaked, including member names, dates of birth, phone numbers, sex, email addresses and passwords, plus bank account information, point balances and regional promotion voucher point balances, transaction history and merchant information. Four Kagoshima financial institutions — Kagoshima Bank, Minami Nippon Bank, Kagoshima Mutual Credit Cooperative and Kagoshima Shinkin Bank — halted all Paydon services at 06:20 JST on 20 November. Infcurion's third report (noon JST, 21 November) confirms no new unauthorised access since the system was cut off on 19 November; from 20 November it paused Wallet Station for providers that were not the target of the leak so emergency checks could be carried out, and providers whose checks found no intrusion and no leak began restarting in stages from 21 November. The provider whose data leaked remains suspended with no restart date, and no misuse of the leaked information had been confirmed. The item was missed by the 20, 21 and 22 November briefs and caught as a wire catch-up on 22 November.

Why it matters strategically. Four regional banks halted a payment service in unison at 06:20 on a Friday morning — a coordinated, visible service interruption that customers notice immediately. Regional and mutual banks share platforms like Wallet Station precisely because they cannot each build their own; the trade-off is that one contractor's breach becomes four banks' outage. The staged restart is the right pattern (check first, restart only the clean providers), but the leaked provider's open-ended suspension leaves its customers without the service indefinitely. Passwords were in the leaked data, which raises the credential-stuffing risk across other services where customers reuse them.

Source: Paydon / Infcurion official notice (20 Nov, translated from Japanese)

7. FFG Industries — a vendor-run analytics tool leaks corporate customer data

What happened. Fukuoka Financial Group announced on 19 November that a data analysis tool used by FFG Industries to aggregate data from its ordering and other systems — set up and run by an outside vendor — was accessed by a third party, and that part of the data in the databases connected to the tool may have leaked. Up to 1,221 corporate customers are affected so far: company names and addresses, contact persons' names, email addresses and phone numbers, login IDs and password hashes, and meeting records. No impact on Fukuoka Bank's core banking systems or account data has been found, the group says. No person or record count has been published.

Why it matters strategically. A small incident with a familiar shape: a vendor-operated analytics tool, connected to ordering-system databases, holding login IDs and password hashes for corporate customers. The core bank was untouched — but corporate customers' credentials and contact data are exactly what business-email-compromise and supplier-impersonation fraud runs on. It belongs in the same pattern as KARTE and Paydon: the vulnerable surface is the tool a vendor runs on a bank group's behalf, not the bank's own perimeter.

8. Banco do Brasil — a misused valid credential, no count disclosed

What happened. Banco do Brasil identified unauthorised access to customer data on 12 November and told affected customers on 18 November. The access came from misuse of a valid credential by third parties; the credential has been revoked and monitoring reinforced. Exposed fields may include names, CPF tax IDs, email addresses, branch numbers and, in some cases, account numbers. The bank says there was no invasion of its core systems and that passwords, tokens, security codes and financial transactions were not exposed or linked to the incident. It has reported the case to the authorities under Brazil's LGPD data-protection law. It has not disclosed how many customers were affected, whose credential was misused, or how the third parties obtained it. A forum sale claiming 22.7 million records is unverified and is not used here.

Why it matters strategically. The bank's own framing — "no invasion of its systems" — is technically accurate and strategically beside the point: a valid credential used improperly is access, and the exposed fields (name, CPF, branch, account number) are exactly what a caller needs to sound like the bank. The bank itself named phishing and social engineering as the main risk, and warned customers it never asks for passwords, codes, app installations or transfers to a "safe account". For the sector, the lesson is about credential governance for internet-facing applications: revocation after detection is necessary but not sufficient — the open questions are how long the access ran before 12 November and how the credential was obtained. The undisclosed victim count also matters: without a number, other banks cannot judge whether this was a targeted pull or a broad harvest.

9. Wellington IT — a credit-union platform breach, disclosed weeks late

What happened. Wellington IT, a Belfast-based IT provider whose CU Online platform runs mobile banking for more than a third of Ireland's credit unions, was exploited through a vulnerability in its software. The attack ran between 2 and 4 October, when an automated script using valid credentials made about five million requests for data and successfully retrieved saved payee records — payee names, bank names, IBANs, BICs and payment reference codes — linked to member accounts. (The Irish Times reported a 6 October attack date and about 1.4 million requests; the flaw was closed on 6 October.) About 40 credit unions in the Republic of Ireland are affected (press reporting put the figure at 43; the Data Protection Commission has received around 40 breach notifications), and the data may cover payees of hundreds of thousands of account holders. Member accounts, balances, passwords and PINs were not accessed, and the vulnerability has been closed. The Department of Finance was first made aware on 17 November; affected credit unions were told on 17–18 November — weeks after the attack. Minister of State Robert Troy, who met Wellington IT on 19 November, said the delay was "not acceptable"; Wellington IT said it alerted customers as soon as it had "confirmed the potential risk". At present there is no evidence members' funds have been accessed or fraudulent activity has occurred, and the full extent is still being established by external cybersecurity experts.

Why it matters strategically. This is the week's sharpest supplier-concentration case outside Japan: one provider serves more than a third of a country's credit unions, so one vulnerability becomes a sector-wide incident, and the exposed data — payees' IBANs and payment references — is payment-system data even though no member's own account was touched. The disclosure delay is the second lesson: an attack in early October, customers told in mid-November. Troy's public criticism puts supplier notification timelines on the regulatory agenda in Ireland, and other jurisdictions will read it the same way. For credit unions, the immediate risk is phishing using verified payee relationships — a caller who knows who you pay is a convincing caller.

10. Cleverbridge — card numbers and CVVs stolen from a payment processor

What happened. Payment processor Cleverbridge disclosed on 22 November that hackers breached its systems and copied payment card data: card number, expiry date and card security code (CVV/CVC) used for a purchase through Cleverbridge. It detected unauthorised activity on 4 November, took systems offline the same day and restored them the same day; its investigation later determined a third party had copied the data. The company has not said how many customers were affected, how attackers got in, how long they were inside, or which merchants' buyers were hit — its listed clients include Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf, Sony and Parallels. It confirmed the incident was not ransomware and nothing was encrypted, and says it has notified data protection authorities, law enforcement, payment card networks and payment partners, and is working with TransUnion's Cyberscout on notifications and complimentary credit monitoring. Malwarebytes is the only merchant so far to confirm impact, saying only "a small subset" of its customers was affected.

Why it matters strategically. Card number plus expiry plus CVV is the full set needed for card-not-present fraud, and it came from a processor, not from any single merchant — so the affected buyers cannot be enumerated merchant by merchant, and issuing banks must watch for unauthorised transactions and consider replacing affected cards without knowing the full population. The 16-day gap between detection (4 November) and customer notifications beginning (20 November) is the kind of timeline regulators and card schemes will ask about. For merchants, the lesson is that a processor breach is a breach of your customers' payment data with no action available to you except waiting for the processor's notification list.

11. BankWeaver — bank middleware turned into a backdoor

What happened. Forescout Research (Vedere Labs) disclosed "BankWeaver" on 19 November: attackers abused a Temenos Quantum Fabric authentication service configured for anonymous access and reachable from the internet, exchanged an anonymous token for an administrator token without credentials, and deployed two malicious Java components — FabricShell for command execution and database queries, and FabricShadow for host reconnaissance and data exfiltration. One unnamed financial services institution was confirmed compromised in a breach running from early September 2026. Six further banks appear to share the same exposure, but compromise has not been confirmed for them, and Temenos confirmed the cause is a misconfiguration. No MITRE IDs were published.

Why it matters strategically. This is the only item this week where the attacker did not need a vulnerability in the usual sense — the door was configured open. Quantum Fabric sits between a bank's digital channels and its back-end systems, so an administrator token there is a position from which to query databases and exfiltrate data across the bank's digital estate. The exposure is checkable in minutes (does your Quantum Fabric authentication service accept anonymous access, and is it internet-reachable?), which makes it a rare case where the sector can self-audit immediately — and where the six further banks sharing the exposure should be doing so now, not after a second victim is named. It also reframes "misconfiguration" as a first-class risk class for banking middleware, not a hygiene footnote.

Source: Forescout Research — BankWeaver (19 Nov)

12. OPTIMJACK — a trusted script becomes a skimmer on 178 organisations' sites

What happened. On 16 November, attackers used a vulnerability in Optimizely's Web Experimentation platform — a flaw in the account signup flow that allowed administrator impersonation and unauthorised changes to Project JavaScript — to inject malicious JavaScript into customer projects. Researcher Scott Helme (Report URI) identified 188 infected snippets associated with 178 organisations, including HSBC, Equifax, Ledger, Zoom, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab and IKEA. The payload (track.js) replaced cryptocurrency wallet addresses, captured payment-card details entered into affected first-party forms, collected non-HttpOnly cookies and page screenshots, and attempted to deliver exploit kits to some iPhone and iPad users. It could not read card fields inside cross-origin hosted payment iframes. Optimizely fixed the vulnerability and removed the injected code the same day; the campaign was largely cleaned by 05:52 SGT on 17 November. No organisation's own servers were breached. 32 of the 178 organisations were in banking and finance. Crypto amounts taken are unreconciled ($7,040 vs $2,300 in different reports) — neither figure is used here as the total; the card-loss count is unknown.

Why it matters strategically. No bank was breached, yet bank customers were skimmed on bank websites — the distinction between "our systems were not compromised" and "our customers were harmed on our site" collapses when the malicious code arrives through a script the bank itself chose to trust. Third-party JavaScript is an unexamined trust boundary for most financial firms: it runs with the site's privileges, it is served from the vendor's CDN under a valid certificate, and most content-security policies treat it as trusted. The exposure window was under a day, but the payload evolved 26 times in about 16 hours, adding card skimming mid-campaign — speed of attacker iteration, not dwell time, is the metric that matters. For BFSI, the control question is concrete: which third-party scripts run on payment and login pages, what can they read, and would anyone notice if one changed?

What happened. 1. Korea — one shared assessment body fails, and the regulator inspects the sector itself

What happened. The Financial Security Institute (FSI) breach set the sequence in motion before this window: a webshell attack at 13:54 on 9 November failed, and an SMS-authentication bypass was then used to create a fake FSS-level account. The Financial Services Commission (FSC) gave that timeline to the National Assembly on 13 November, and its special audit of the FSI began on 16 November. The FSI's evaluation system was still shut down at the end of the window, with no restoration date, and 2,912 firms form the assessment population whose appeals are blocked. Reporting put the wider Korean wave at 37 firms and about 473,000 records in one month.

The regulator then widened the net. On 17 November the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) called in CISOs and security officers from 227 electronic-finance and 47 online investment-linked firms — 274 firms in total — for an intensive external-attack-surface inspection, with first-round self-assessments due 30 November. The FSS said intrusion attempts through external contact points were continuing, above all at small and mid-sized operators, and warned it would hold firms strictly accountable if a breach followed a poorly done inspection — including checking whether the advance checks had been properly carried out. The same day it disclosed new incidents at Spoon (Giftiell, about 18,000 people's data) and Zoomfund (about KRW 300 million in customer deposits), and began surprise on-site inspections of high-risk branches at four banks, the first in about ten years. The FSC will require large firms to run vulnerability assessments twice a year instead of once, and to identify and classify all IT assets. The wider programme announced on 5 November covers full inspections of external access points at about 580 financial companies. NoCutNews, 17 Nov, translated from Korean · NoCutNews, 18 Nov, translated from Korean · The Fair Economy, 17 Nov — 580-firm programme figure

The wave kept producing victims through the week: Coinone's breach via its customer-support contractor MetaM (disclosed 15 November; passwords, resident registration numbers, bank account numbers and wallet addresses may have leaked; victim count unconfirmed; disclosure took about 70 hours 40 minutes), KOMSCO's chak gift-certificate platform breach (3,083 customers confirmed), and Mirae Asset Life Insurance (10,418 victims as of 5 November, up from about 6,800 — crossing the 10,000-person 72-hour reporting duty). Then, on 23 November, Samsung Securities disclosed that an unauthorised outsider accessed its Voice of Customer complaints system — traces found on the afternoon of 21 November — and exfiltrated attachments from 8,241 customers across 13 fields, including name, account number, address, contact details and driver's licence number. Why it matters strategically. This is the clearest case this week of a regulator becoming the cascade. The FSI is the body that evaluates the sector's security; once it was breached, the regulator could no longer trust the sector's own evaluation system, so it replaced self-assessment with direct inspection — on a deadline, with accountability attached to the quality of the checks. That turns a single incident into sector-wide work for 274 named firms and a standing programme across about 580. The second strategic signal is where the breaches keep landing: not the core banking system, but a support contractor, a gift-certificate platform, a complaints portal. The FSS's own framing — external contact points, third-party areas handling customer information and funds — points at the same perimeter the inspections are meant to close.

Who is exposed next. The 274 summoned firms, above all small and mid-sized operators; firms whose 30 November self-assessments surface unpatched external exposure; and the 8,241 Samsung Securities complainants, whose verified customer details can now be used for impersonation calls and phishing citing the incident. Korean banks should also expect phishing and voice-phishing using data from the separate KEPCO breach (about 1.52 million people, disclosed 22 November) — a utility breach whose exposed data is financial data.

Still unknown. Who attacked Samsung Securities and how they entered the Voice of Customer system; whether any of the leaked data has been misused or any customer has suffered a loss; whether the Samsung incident is linked to the wider wave or stands alone — no link has been established; and the FSI audit's findings and the evaluation system's restoration date.

CISA added four exploited flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalogue on 20 November — Apache Roller (CVE-2026-129448), VMware vCenter (CVE-2026-123890), SAP NetWeaver (CVE-2025-42884) and Cisco Expressway (CVE-2026-76466) — taking the catalogue to version 2026.11.20 with 1,790 entries. The 18 November additions (Synology, LiteLLM, Apache Axis, REDCap) fell due on 21 November, and the 13 November additions fell due on 16 November. CISA alert, 20 Nov

Why it matters strategically. A KEV due date is the remediation deadline for US federal civilian agencies. It does not mean the vulnerability is fixed everywhere, that exploitation has stopped, or that the deadline binds private-sector firms — it is not an expiry date for the risk. Its power is as a forcing function: vendors, auditors and customers treat the date as a patching deadline even where it does not legally apply, and four flaws sharing one due date concentrates that pressure into a single change window. The state-linked thread is the vCenter flaw: Google Threat Intelligence Group disclosed on 19 November that suspected PRC-nexus actor ARMORY CASTLE exploited CVE-2026-123890, an unauthenticated authentication bypass in vCenter Server, as a zero-day in early September, using a custom Go tool to create a vSphere single-sign-on administrator account, exfiltrate the directory database and steal the STS SAML signing key. Broadcom patched it the same day; there is no workaround. Google Threat Intelligence Group, 19 Nov

Cascade mechanism. The four flaws differ in ways that matter. vCenter was exploited as a zero-day since September by a suspected state-linked actor; proof-of-concept code is circulating in unverified repositories, at least one reported fake, and Rapid7's Metasploit pull request still had no code as of 23 November 09:00. Expressway saw its first in-the-wild exploitation, reported by F6 on 18 November (covered 20 November SGT), days after the public proof of concept — two internet-facing boxes breached, then a pivot to internal cluster nodes and root access. Roller was already actively exploited when added; NetWeaver is the oldest flaw of the four (a 2025 CVE). One due date therefore covers four different exploitation timelines, and the weakest-patched of the four sets the sector's exposure.

Who is exposed next. Organisations running any of the four products unpatched, above all internet-facing vCenter and Expressway deployments, and firms whose vendors or MSPs run them. The due date passing does not reduce that exposure.

Still unknown. Patch uptake across the four products; whether the due date passing changes attacker behaviour; and which circulating vCenter proof-of-concept repositories, if any, are genuine.

7. United Kingdom — three regulators publish one cyber-resilience consultation

What happened. On 19 November the Bank of England, the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA) and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) jointly published a consultation on cyber risk and resilience management (PRA CP17/26, FCA GC26/9). It sets out principles-based, outcome-focused expectations for how firms manage cyber risk and recover from incidents, bringing together existing requirements and lessons from supervisory experience rather than introducing new rules. It is accompanied by separate "effective practices" documents on AI and quantum risks. The consultation closes on Friday 26 February 2027, with final guidance expected in 2027. The draft guidance applies to banks, insurers, financial market infrastructures and FCA-regulated firms subject to existing operational-resilience requirements. Bank of England — CP17/26 and GC26/9, 19 Nov

Why it matters strategically. Three regulators speaking with one voice is itself the signal: the UK is consolidating cyber expectations into a single supervisory yardstick instead of letting each regulator's requirements drift apart. The timing is deliberate — the Bank's latest Systemic Risk Survey cited cyber as one of the risks that would have the greatest impact on the UK financial system, and October 2026 saw the second-highest monthly number of cyber incidents reported to the FCA in five years. The AI and quantum effective-practices documents matter most for the forward look: the regulators are explicitly telling firms to plan for AI-enabled attackers and for quantum risk to data privacy now, while the main guidance clarifies how existing duties on governance, operational resilience, operational risk and third-party risk apply in a cyber context. Because the guidance clarifies existing rules rather than creating new ones, firms can be supervised against it once final — the consultation is the last window to shape what "good" looks like.

Who is exposed next. UK banks, insurers, payment and e-money firms, and financial market infrastructures in scope — and, in practice, their third-party providers, since the guidance reaches third-party risk management. Firms operating in both the UK and the EU will now face two consolidating regimes at once.

Still unknown. What the final guidance will say after consultation; whether the AI and quantum effective practices harden into firmer expectations; and how the FCA's incident-reporting trend develops into 2027.

8. Singapore — the regulator draws a line on AI agents, and the national CERT warns on Apache

What happened. On 19 November, speaking at the Singapore FinTech Festival, Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) managing director Chia Der Jiun said consumers should not give personal AI agents the login credentials to their financial accounts, because there is insufficient assurance that such access can be provided safely. A bank receiving instructions from an agent may not know whether the agent has the customer's full authorisation, whether it is staying inside its mandate, or who is responsible if it transacts incorrectly or beyond its instructions. MAS will facilitate a "know your agent" protocol for trusted information exchange between customer agents and financial institutions — covering the agent's identity, whom it represents, what authority it has, what mandate it operates under, and its origins and reliability — as the next phase of its Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime (SAFR) programme, and will set up an Agentic Money Sandbox with the Future of Finance Institute for corporate treasury management and payment services. No delivery date was given for the protocol. MAS — keynote address by the Managing Director at the Singapore FinTech Festival 2026, 19 Nov

Two days earlier, on 17 November, the Cyber Security Agency of Singapore (CSA) published alert AL-2026-164 on CVE-2026-119618, a race-condition remote-code-execution flaw in Apache HTTP Server's mod_http2 module (versions 2.4.55 to before 2.4.71, only where mod_http2 is enabled). CSA said the flaw is "reportedly being actively exploited, and a proof-of-concept exploit is publicly available", and advised updating immediately. Apache had fixed it in 2.4.71 on 11 November. CSA Singapore — AL-2026-164, 17 Nov

Why it matters strategically. The two moves are two halves of one regulator posture. The MAS warning treats the AI agent as a new kind of account holder: one that cannot yet prove who it is, what it may do, or who pays when it goes wrong — and the answer offered is identity infrastructure ("know your agent"), not a ban. That is a notable shift from warning about AI as a threat to governing AI as a financial actor, and it lands the same week AI-voice vishing was confirmed as a ransomware entry point in Ukraine and AI tooling was confirmed in the European Commission campaign. The CSA alert shows the other half: a national CERT acting as the exploitation warning system for a widely used web-server component, with the careful wording ("reportedly") that keeps the claim honest while still forcing patching. Together they show Singapore regulating both the new agent economy and the old infrastructure in the same week.

Who is exposed next. Any bank or payment provider whose customers hand logins to personal AI agents — the practice MAS says is already happening; firms building agent interfaces into financial services without a trusted channel; and any organisation running internet-facing Apache with mod_http2 enabled and unpatched.

Still unknown. When the "know your agent" protocol will arrive and what it will require technically; who bears liability when an agent transacts beyond its mandate; and who is being attacked through CVE-2026-119618, or at what scale — CSA gave no victim or attribution detail.

9. Supplier failures draw governments in — Ireland, France and Brazil

(a) Ireland — Wellington IT and 40 credit unions: the state steps in over a notification delay

What happened. Wellington IT, a Belfast-based supplier of banking software to Irish credit unions, was attacked between 2 and 4 October: an authorisation flaw in its CU Online platform was exploited and saved payee records were retrieved — payee names, IBANs, BICs, sort codes, addresses and payment references. Around 40 Irish credit unions were affected, roughly a quarter of the national total. The Department of Finance was first made aware on 17 November; many affected credit unions only learned of the incident in recent days. On 19 November, Minister of State Robert Troy convened Wellington IT and the credit-union sector in the Department of Finance. Wellington IT said the vulnerability has been identified and closed, and that there is no evidence members' funds were accessed or fraudulent activity involving members' accounts has occurred. The incident is limited to payee details associated with mobile banking apps operated by Wellington IT. The company has engaged external cybersecurity experts for a comprehensive review, and is engaging with the National Cyber Security Centre, An Garda Síochána, the Data Protection Commission and the Central Bank of Ireland. The exact number of accounts and users affected is not yet established. Department of Finance (Ireland) — statement from Minister of State Robert Troy, 19 Nov

Why it matters strategically. The incident itself is a supplier-concentration failure of the week's familiar shape — one provider, dozens of small financial institutions. What makes it geopolitical is the government response: a minister publicly calling the notification delay "not acceptable", convening the supplier and the sector, and pulling in four authorities at once (cyber centre, police, data-protection regulator, central bank). The delay — an October attack surfacing in mid-November — is the point the minister chose to press, and it is the question other governments will now ask their own suppliers: how fast would you tell us, and who would you tell first? The full scope is still being established because the firm lacked activity logs that would specify which member data was compromised — a logging gap that is itself a regulatory finding in waiting.

Still unknown. The exact number of accounts and users affected; how the attacker obtained the access used; and whether the review finds wider exposure than payee details.

(b) France — Maileva: a postal subsidiary's breach becomes a criminal investigation

What happened. La Poste confirmed to AFP on 14 November that hackers had stolen the first names, last names and postal addresses of 22 million people from Maileva, the Docaposte subsidiary that sends letters on behalf of businesses — recipients of mail, roughly a third of France's population, not Maileva's direct customers. The attack took place in mid-September; Maileva suspended services on 23 September and restored them on 29 September. Maileva notified France's data protection authority, the CNIL, filed a criminal complaint, and the Paris prosecutor opened a judicial investigation on 15 November. No bank details for recipients were in the exposed data (business-client IBANs were in billing data). The disclosure and the investigation opening fall just before this window; the item was carried into the week as a catch-up in the 18 November briefing. Digital Watch Observatory, 15 Nov

Why it matters strategically. Maileva shows the state treating a supplier breach as a criminal matter from the start — CNIL notification plus a prosecutor's judicial investigation, with the cybercrime unit leading — rather than leaving it as a company disclosure. The data itself (names and postal addresses at national scale) is a phishing and impersonation asset, not a payments asset, which is why the follow-on risk is fake invoices and payment reminders using real names and addresses. The case also illustrates the disclosure lag that recurs this week (September attack, November confirmation), and the fact that a state-owned group's subsidiary can be the single point of failure for hospitals, housing bodies and employers across a country.

Still unknown. The entry point (Maileva has not officially described it); the attackers' identity; whether the stolen data has been published, sold or used in fraud; and the full extent of any access to document contents.

(c) Brazil — Banco do Brasil: a misused credential, and the regulator's notification regime

What happened. Banco do Brasil identified unauthorised access to customer data on 12 November and told affected customers on 18 November. A third party misused a valid credential to reach an internet-facing application; exposed fields may include names, CPF tax IDs, email addresses, branch numbers and, in some cases, account numbers. The bank says its systems were not invaded, no passwords, tokens or security codes were exposed, no fraudulent transactions have been found, and the credential has been revoked. It is notifying authorities under Brazil's data protection law (LGPD), which requires reporting to the national data protection authority (ANPD) and notification of affected people where there is relevant risk. The bank has not disclosed how many customers were affected or whose credential was misused. A forum sale claiming 22.7 million records is unverified and was not used. CyberSIXT reporting of the bank's customer notice, 18 Nov

Why it matters strategically. The case is small in confirmed scope and large in pattern: a valid credential, an internet-facing application, no system intrusion — the same "legitimate access, misused" shape as the Korean FSI's SMS-auth bypass and the NBFC loan diversions. The LGPD notification duty is doing the work here: the bank's disclosure, the ANPD report and the customer warning about phishing all flow from it. The open question is the same one Ireland's minister asked of Wellington IT — whose credential, how it was obtained, and how long the access ran before 12 November — and the bank has not answered it.

Still unknown. The number of customers affected; whose credential was misused and how it was obtained; how long the access lasted; and whether the ANPD opens a review.

10. Binance — a state-attributed theft, a freezing order, and laundering that accelerates anyway

What happened. Binance's 198-token zero-knowledge proof of reserves went live, formally announced on 14 November (snapshot 12 November 00:00 UTC, block height 975,493; BTC at 100.05%). Withdrawals had reopened on 87 networks, with full reopening aimed at about 22 November — but no completion confirmation had appeared by the 23 November briefing, and no final root-cause report had appeared in the window's briefings. Meanwhile the stolen funds kept moving. Elliptic's 20 November update says at least 2,310 BTC (about $149 million) of the stolen funds has gone into coinjoin mixers, about 90% of it since 17 November — almost all coordinated by Kruw, a Wasabi Wallet coordinator — and five of the nine largest ETH wallets were emptied since 17 November (about 488,000 ETH, about $860 million). About $2.35 billion remains in attacker-linked wallets and about $2.85 billion is frozen; very little has been cashed out. The 18,270 BTC stolen directly from Binance has still not moved. The laundering spans more than 27,000 wallets on at least 16 blockchains, about 55 services and about 90,000 transactions. Binance obtained a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing order from the Abu Dhabi Global Market Courts on 18 November (per the 22 Nov briefing record, not the Elliptic article). Attribution to North Korea's TraderTraitor is per the FBI (31 October). Elliptic, updated 20 Nov

Why it matters strategically. This is the week's state-linked financial-crime cascade: a theft attributed to North Korea, a freezing order from a Gulf financial court, and laundering through mixers and decentralised infrastructure that no single jurisdiction controls. Proof of reserves and withdrawal reopening address customer confidence; they do not touch the laundering. The freezing order, the mixers and the dispersed wallets form a second, slower cascade through the crypto ecosystem: over-the-counter brokers, mixers and receiving exchanges must now decide what to do with funds that are publicly traced but still moving, and the acceleration after 17 November — 90% of the coinjoin volume in a few days — suggests the operators moved faster once the proof and reopening timetable were public. The test for governments is whether a freezing order of this size can actually be enforced across 16 blockchains and 55 services.

Who is exposed next. OTC brokers, mixers and exchanges receiving the flows; Binance customers still waiting for full withdrawal reopening; and regulators watching whether the freezing order bites.

Still unknown. Whether full withdrawal reopening was completed, and when; the final root cause; how much of the frozen $2.85 billion is recoverable and returns to customers; and the remaining-holdings total — Elliptic puts about $2.35 billion in attacker-linked wallets, while Lookonchain counts 602,402 ETH at about $1.09 billion. Those estimates are unreconciled and count different things; they must not be added together.

Cross-cutting read: what the week's geopolitical picture adds up to

Three patterns run through all ten cascades:

  1. Supplier concentration is the main cascade. The FSI, MetaM, KARTE's 754 keys, Wellington IT's credit-union platform, Maileva's postal pipeline, and the suspected shared vendor behind the n0n Visa / JPMorgan / Mastercard publications (Qubole — reported, not confirmed; Qubole has not responded) all show the same shape: one provider's failure becomes dozens of firms' incidents. The question to ask of every vendor is now the one the n0n case forces: what customer data does it hold, which of your cloud roles can it assume, and are those roles still needed?
  1. AI is the entry point, not only the payload. ARTEX (reported, unconfirmed at the Commission), AI-voice vishing (confirmed, Ukraine / FBI), CyberStrikeAI in the NBFC fraud (reported in one case), and agentic tooling elsewhere in the week all lower the cost of the first step — a call, a scan, a diverted loan — rather than the last one. The regulators noticed in the same week: MAS is building identity infrastructure for AI agents, and the UK consultation carries separate AI effective practices.
  1. Regulators are the amplifier — and this week they amplified in concert. The FSS's 274-firm call-in and 30 November deadline, Japan's FSA reporting order to Rakuten, India's Supreme Court directions and the RBI's 30 November KYC timetable, the UK's joint BoE / FCA / PRA consultation, Singapore's MAS warning and CSA alert, Ireland's minister convening a supplier and four authorities, France's prosecutor opening a judicial investigation, and the US KEV due-date cluster all convert single incidents into sector-wide work. The 30 November convergence — Korean self-assessments and RBI KYC notification due the same day — is a coincidence of two separate regimes, not a coordinated deadline, but firms operating in both will feel it as one. The deeper convergence is the direction of travel: from incident response toward standing duties — twice-yearly assessments in Korea, clarified resilience expectations in the UK, "know your agent" identity in Singapore, and compensation liability for banks in India. The week's incidents were mostly not new kinds of attack; the week's regulation mostly is.

1. VMware vCenter zero-day — CVE-2026-123890

What it is. An unauthenticated authentication bypass in vCenter Server's Directory Service. An attacker with network access to vCenter and no credentials can gain access and take control. Broadcom rates it 9.8. The flaw sits in the Secure Remote Password (SRP-6a) mechanism over DCE/RPC, reachable through the rpc_srp_verifier service on TCP/2012; it is not the same bug as CVE-2026-59309, an earlier LDAP-path bypass — the attacker's tool ran successfully against a server already patched for that flaw.

Who is affected. vCenter 9.1.x, 9.0.x, 8.0 and 7.0, and VMware Cloud Foundation 5.x. vCenter 7.0 needs an extended support contract for a fix.

Exploitation status: exploited in the wild, as a zero-day. Google Threat Intelligence Group disclosed on 19 November that suspected PRC-nexus actor ARMORY CASTLE (formerly UNC5221) exploited the flaw from early September 2026, after first breaking in through a BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day (CVE-2026-102764). Using a custom Go tool, BONESAW, the actor created a vSphere single-sign-on administrator account, exfiltrated the directory database and stole the STS SAML signing key, which allows forging authentication tokens, bypassing MFA and impersonating any user. It also added an SSH public key to the ESXi root account and stole data from ESXi filesystems.

Fix status. Broadcom patched it the same day in VMSA-2026-0009: fixed in vCenter 9.1.1.0200, 9.0.2.0200 and 8.0 U3m, and VMware Cloud Foundation 5.2.5. There is no workaround. CISA added the flaw to KEV on 20 November, with federal remediation due 23 November.

PoC status: none verified. Several public proof-of-concept repositories exist; none has been verified, at least one is reported fake, and Rapid7's Metasploit pull request #22173 still contained no code as of 09:00 SGT on 23 November. Do not run unverified exploit code, and do not link any of these repositories.

Prioritisation. First on the list. Patch immediately, and treat patching as only half the job: any vCenter reachable during the September–November window must be hunted for a rogue SSO administrator account, a stolen or abused STS SAML signing key, and unauthorised SSH keys on ESXi hosts. Restrict network access to vCenter management services (TCP/2012 and the directory service) to trusted administrators only. This is the week's most consequential vulnerability for any bank running vSphere, including through a vendor or MSP.

2. NGINX2Root — five critical flaws, lead CVE-2026-123007

What it is. Five unauthenticated remote-code-execution flaws in NGINX, disclosed 18 November. The lead flaw, CVE-2026-123007, is an integer underflow in HTTP/2 flow-control accounting that can produce a heap buffer overflow; it has been present since NGINX 1.9.5 (2015) and needs only HTTP/2 enabled — serving static content is enough. The other four are CVE-2026-123633 (HTTP/2 use-after-free), CVE-2026-123634, CVE-2026-121810 (HTTP/3) and CVE-2026-113425 (mail proxy / resolver paths), each reachable only in a particular configuration. F5 rates each flaw 9.2 under CVSS 4.0 and 8.1 under CVSS 3.1. Reaching root on the host needs chaining with a separate, unpatched Linux kernel flaw; the NGINX flaws on their own give code execution as the NGINX worker.

Who is affected. NGINX Open Source 1.9.5–1.30.6 and 1.31.0–1.31.8 (ranges differ per flaw: CVE-2026-123633 from 1.19.7, CVE-2026-123634 from 0.6.18, CVE-2026-121810 from 1.25.0, CVE-2026-113425 from 0.5.33), NGINX Plus, and F5's NGINX Ingress Controller, Gateway Fabric, Instance Manager and WAF for NGINX builds. NGINX sits in front of banking web, API and payment-gateway services, so exposure is broad even where the bank does not run NGINX directly — check vendors and cloud front-ends too.

Exploitation status: PoC public, no in-the-wild exploitation reported. Wiz demonstrated remote code execution with an ASLR bypass against official 1.31.7 and 1.31.8 builds, and Rapid7 published a working proof of concept for CVE-2026-123007 on disclosure day. Fake nginx2root exploits are also circulating; do not run them. The flaws were not in CISA KEV as of 23 November.

Fix status. Fixed in NGINX Open Source 1.30.7 (stable) / 1.31.9 (mainline) and NGINX Plus 37.0.8.1 / 37.3.0.1 for CVE-2026-123007; CVE-2026-123633 is fixed in Plus 37.0.7.1 / 37.2.0.1, so Plus users should take 37.0.8.1 or 37.3.0.1 to cover both. Workaround where patching must wait: disable HTTP/2 (remove http2 from listen directives; on NGINX 1.25.1 and later, also set http2 off).

Prioritisation. Patch now, alongside the exploited items — the combination of a public working PoC, a decade of affected versions and NGINX's position in front of customer-facing services makes the window between disclosure and exploitation likely to be short.

3. Java4Shell — CVE-2026-114938

What it is. A heap out-of-bounds write in the HotSpot JVM's AVX-512 Base64 decoder that can allow unauthenticated remote code execution on x86-64 systems with AVX-512 VBMI. Affected: OpenJDK 17.0.6 and later, and lines 21, 25 and 27. Scores differ by scorer and are not merged: Calif rates it 9.2 (CVSS 4.0), Oracle/OpenJDK 8.1 (CVSS 3.1), Red Hat 9.8.

Who is affected. Any Java application running on x86-64 hardware with AVX-512 VBMI — application servers, middleware, and products that bundle a JDK, including in banks' own estates and in vendor products. ARM systems are not affected.

Exploitation status: probing only, zero successful intrusions. GreyNoise recorded 261 Java4Shell-shaped sessions from 7 IPs as of 21 November, still 261 sessions from the same 7 IPs with zero successful intrusions on 22–23 November. The flaw was not in CISA KEV as of 23 November. A claim that CISA added it on 19 November under an invented "CISA-2026-040" advisory is false. Two proof-of-concept repositories were verified by reading their code and are linked on the Java4Shell tracker: arkrwn's crash/leak reproducer and ikow/java4shell-poc (Qi Deng; heap corruption confirmed on three products, not RCE). A crash/leak reproducer is not an RCE shell, and neither repository is a weaponised exploit.

Fix status. Fixes shipped broadly and fast after CISA's urgent alert on 17 November: Oracle fixed builds on 17 November as part of its November CPU; Eclipse Temurin fixed binaries for all four lines on x64 Linux on 18 November, with Windows and macOS builds landing by 20 November; Microsoft shipped fixed builds on 18 November; Red Hat's Build of OpenJDK fixed builds shipped on 18 November and all six RHEL errata followed on 19 November; BellSoft confirmed its fixed builds; Debian shipped DSA-6639-1 / DSA-6640-1 / DLA-4917-1 on 19 November; Keycloak 26.8 container images ship the fixed runtime; Okta published fixed versions on 19 November. Still outstanding at the end of the window: Cisco's affected products with fixes still to be determined (advisory rev 1.3, 20 Nov; Catalyst Center was corrected to not affected in rev 1.3); Elastic's fix slated for bundled JDK 27.0.1.1+4; Siemens Industrial Edge Management Pro (V1, V2 and Virtual) affected in all versions with no fix — block direct internet access; Temurin Java 27 for Windows x64 still in testing, and Temurin will not build Java 27 for macOS x64. PaperCut NG/MF 25.0.0–25.0.13 and 26.0.0–26.0.6 bundle an affected Java version on AVX-512 VBMI hardware; PaperCut planned 26.0.7 with the fixed Java for the week of 23 November, with the manual mitigation -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics in the meantime (no fix confirmed yet for 25.x).

Workarounds. -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics (safe on ARM) or -XX:UseAVX=2 (fails to start on ARM).

Prioritisation. Patch all Java runtimes now, and inventory bundled JDKs — the long tail is the risk here, in products whose vendors have not yet shipped a fix. Where a vendor fix is still outstanding, apply the JVM flag and restrict network exposure. Keep watching for a KEV addition or a confirmed exploitation report; neither had appeared by the close of the window.

4. PHP November security release — CVE-2026-115560 and six other critical flaws

What it is. PHP's 19 November security release fixes 17 CVEs, seven of them rated critical by the PHP project at 9.2 (CVSS 4.0). The lead flaw, CVE-2026-115560, is a session serializer mismatch in the session upload-progress feature that can lead to remote code execution: session data written with one serializer and read with another, with a gadget chain in the application. The fix turns session upload progress off by default and blocks runtime changes to session settings while it is enabled. The other critical flaws include CVE-2026-129289, a PHP-FPM request-smuggling flaw that lets an unauthenticated attacker forge a backend request and execute attacker-controlled PHP code where the front end reuses FastCGI connections, and CVE-2026-126714, partially overwritten session files.

Who is affected. PHP 8.2.0–8.2.35, 8.3.0–8.3.36, 8.4.0–8.4.27 and 8.5.0–8.5.12 (some CVEs affect only some branches). PHP 8.1 and older receive no fix. This closes the thread the PHP2Shell page had tracked as awaiting the next regular release; servers patched only in October remain exposed until they take the 19 November builds. PHP 8.6.0, released the same day, already includes the fixes.

Exploitation status: PoC public (lab only), no in-the-wild exploitation reported. Lab proofs of concept for CVE-2026-115560 have been public since October. No exploitation in the wild has been reported, and none of the November CVEs was in CISA KEV as of 23 November. Exploiting CVE-2026-115560 needs session upload progress enabled with mismatched serializers plus a gadget chain; CVE-2026-129289 needs a front end that reuses backend connections and an application path that does not read the whole request body — so not every PHP server is reachable, but the preconditions are common enough to treat the release as urgent.

Fix status. Fixed in 8.5.13, 8.4.28, 8.3.37 and 8.2.36, released 19 November. Workaround for CVE-2026-115560: set session.upload_progress.enabled = 0; for CVE-2026-129289, disable FastCGI connection reuse on the front end.

Prioritisation. Patch in the normal cycle this week, ahead of the 8.2 deadline: PHP 8.2 security support ends 31 December 2026, so 8.2.36 is one of the last fixes that branch will get — plan the move off 8.2 now, and off 8.1 immediately, since 8.1 gets nothing at all.

5. ruby2shell — CVE-2026-132825

What it is. A one-byte heap overflow in Ruby's string case conversion (GHSA-34m4-m9fh-gqp5), present since Ruby 2.4.0, in single-byte encodings such as ISO-8859-1 and Windows-1252. Ruby rates it Critical; NVD scores it 7.4 on a local vector with high complexity — the scoring disagreement is noted, not re-rated: the CVE score describes the memory primitive, while the researchers argue a stock Rails application exposes a network path to it. 1. Ransomware — n0n turns claims into publications at three global financial brands

What happened. After a 48-hour deadline set on 16 November expired, the n0n extortion group published customer marketing data attributed to Visa, JPMorgan Chase and Mastercard on 19 November. JPMorgan confirmed third-party service providers were targeted and marketing data that may include customer names and email addresses was accessed; Visa confirmed an incident at an Asia-Pacific third-party provider, with its own systems unaffected; Mastercard confirmed a limited exposure from one benefit programme. The published sets hold about 16,000 JPMorgan Reserve customer email addresses (plus 124 employee emails), over 6,000 Visa premium customers' details in Asia-Pacific, and about 2,000 alleged Mastercard entries that researchers say look like test data rather than genuine customer records. No card numbers, passwords or balances are in the published sets.

The supplier thread — reported, not confirmed. BleepingComputer reported on 20 November that n0n claims it attacked data-platform provider Qubole on 11 November and used compromised Qubole credentials to reach customers' AWS environments. Qubole has not responded and none of the three firms has named a vendor, so that link stays reported. Wiz and Sentra documented an October campaign abusing Qubole credentials in which a threat actor assumed cross-account IAM roles customers had created to trust Qubole's AWS account — that campaign is documented, but it has not been verified as n0n's work. Actor-claimed volumes for the Qubole theft (53.9 GB across 30,743 files) are unverified and are not used here.

Targeting. High-value customer-marketing data at global payment and banking brands, reached — on the reported account — through a shared data-platform supplier rather than through the brands themselves.

What it signals. This is the week's clearest actor-behaviour shift: n0n moved from leak-site listings to actual publications against named financial firms, and the publications were corroborated by the victims within a day. It also shows the supplier pattern in its sharpest form: one provider's credentials can put three household-name brands on a leak site at once. At the same time, the group's inflation habit continues — Bitdefender's standing caveat is that n0n lists 100+ victims against just over 20 real targets — so listings alone remain a poor measure of impact.

BFSI relevance. Direct. Three global payment/banking brands had customer data published; the exposure is marketing data, so the immediate risk is targeted phishing and impersonation using real customer names and emails, not card fraud. Banks should treat any shared data-platform vendor with cross-account cloud access as a concentration risk and check what customer data it holds and which cloud roles it can assume.

3. Ransomware — Ukraine/FBI arrests confirm AI-voice vishing as a ransomware entry point

What happened. Ukrainian police, working with the FBI, arrested four suspected key members of an international ransomware organisation on 18–19 November after a two-year investigation. The group built its own platform using AI-generated voice messages to automate fraudulent calls impersonating IT support, tricking employees into disclosing credentials or installing remote-access software (TeamViewer / AnyDesk). In one incident an attacker kept an employee on the phone while another operator accessed the company's systems. At least 28 companies paid more than $40.5 million in ransoms. The organisation had at least 27 members from Ukraine, Kazakhstan, Russia and other countries, within a wider network of more than 60 people across eight jurisdictions. Named victims in court records include Warren Oil Company (August 2024, 1.4 TB copied, $5 million paid after a $10 million demand), Newpark Resources (December 2024, $1.5 million) and Quantum Health (June 2026, $5 million after a $15 million demand). Police carried out 66 simultaneous searches and seized computers, phones, bank cards, about $370,000 in cash and €20,000. The four face up to 15 years. No group name was published and no MITRE mapping was published. Ukrainian authorities have not identified the four as former Conti members or confirmed links to named ransomware brands — do not present that link as established.

Targeting. Oil and gas, pharmaceutical, manufacturing, logistics, financial sector and critical infrastructure — any organisation whose employees can be reached by phone and talked into handing over access.

What it signals. AI voice moves vishing from a fraud problem to a ransomware initial-access problem. The control that fails is not email filtering but help-desk verification: a convincing voice, a plausible IT-support pretext, and a live human kept on the line while a second operator moves inside. The arrests validate the threat model; they do not remove the tooling, which is built on publicly available voice synthesis.

BFSI relevance. Direct. Financial firms are named among the victim sectors, and a single help-desk interaction can hand over VPN or remote-access credentials. Firms relying on SMS-based MFA are doubly exposed, since the same social-engineering path defeats it. The fix is procedural — callback verification and phishing-resistant MFA for help-desk resets — not another email filter.

4. Vishing at scale — Unit 42 "Dialing for Data": the Com's voice-first extortion waves

What happened. Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 reported on 20 November that Com-affiliated threat actors have run numerous waves of vishing attacks in recent months, accelerating sharply around August 2026, leading to data theft and extortion. The pattern: a caller posing as internal IT support steers an employee to a fake sign-in page (an adversary-in-the-middle proxy) that captures the password and the MFA approval in real time; the attackers then register their own MFA device — in the Vortex cluster, within minutes of logging in, in several cases reusing one device across multiple compromised accounts — and exfiltrate mailbox, OneDrive and SharePoint content with automated scripts. Extortion demands then arrive through Microsoft Teams or internal email, pointing to a Tor-hosted negotiation site. In one cluster (Helix), investigators found a malicious browser extension impersonating Google Chrome, sideloaded in Developer Mode, with keylogging, cookie theft, QR-code harvesting, webcam streaming and remote control via Chrome DevTools Protocol. Named actors include Bling Libra (aka ShinyHunters) and clusters CL-CRI-1116 (Redact, formerly BlackFile), CL-CRI-1147 (Pink), CL-CRI-1182 (Falcon), CL-CRI-1192 (Helix), and the newly uncovered CL-CRI-1264 (Vortex) and CL-CRI-1274 (Cinder), which overlap with activity Google tracks as UNC6671.

Targeting. Data-rich industries — financial services, healthcare and legal services — primarily in the US, UK and Australia. In finance the attackers focused on investment portfolios and deal data; at law firms, partner communications, firm strategy and pending contracts.

What it signals. This is the industrialised version of the §3 arrests: voice calls as the front door, MFA enrolment as the persistence mechanism, and cloud file stores as the target — no malware needed until the extension stage. The two newly named clusters (Vortex, September 2026; Cinder, September 2026) show the activity is still growing, and the Chrome-extension tradecraft shows the groups are adding endpoint persistence to a previously cloud-only play.

BFSI relevance. Direct and immediate. Financial services is a named target sector, and the attack defeats push-based and number-matching MFA through the proxy, then defeats account recovery by enrolling attacker-controlled MFA devices. Phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys) for privileged and finance staff, plus help-desk verification for MFA enrolment and resets, are the controls that matter; extension allowlists close the Helix path.

5. Fraud — NBFC loan-diversion fraud in India: approved loans, diverted before disbursement

What happened. Delhi Police reported that 12 non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) in Delhi-NCR lost over ₹65 crore in three months to loan-disbursement fraud, with more than 150 NBFC complaints nationwide totalling over ₹300 crore. The fraud diverted approved-but-undisbursed loans: one lender lost ₹41.27 crore in about 90 minutes on 16 November, and Delhi Police report "traces of AI" in the frauds. CyberStrikeAI — an agentic offensive-AI tool — was used in at least one October case. A wider figure of about ₹105.67 crore across 14 NBFCs is also in circulation. The two figures remain unreconciled — they may cover different periods, geographies or counting methods — so neither is used here as the loss total.

How it works. The fraud does not breach a perimeter; it abuses a legitimate business flow. Loans that have been approved but not yet disbursed are redirected before the money reaches the borrower. That makes it a disbursement-control failure, and the speed (₹41.27 crore in about 90 minutes) points to automation rather than manual fraud.

What it signals. AI-assisted fraud is moving from the call centre to the back office: the same agentic tooling that scans networks is being pointed at loan pipelines. The "traces of AI" wording is vague — how far the tooling went beyond assistance is not established — but the CyberStrikeAI link in one case ties this fraud directly to the offensive-AI-tooling story of the week.

BFSI relevance. Direct. NBFCs and fintech lenders with approved-but-undisbursed loan pipelines are exposed, and any firm operating a payout or disbursement API without tight controls on who can redirect a disbursement is exposed to the same replay. Customers of affected lenders face follow-on phishing citing the incidents.

6. Fraud — PayU payout-API theft: ₹25.67 crore through 265 transactions

What happened. PayU Payments' cloud-server breach saw a manipulated payout API siphon ₹25.67 crore (about US$2.9 million) through 265 unauthorised transactions to 156 accounts. Two men were arrested on 15 November. The access method is still under audit, and the case follows a CERT-In / CSIRT-Fin payment-API advisory of 9 October.

How it works. A payout API — the interface a payment firm uses to send money out to accounts — was manipulated so that unauthorised payouts were issued to attacker-controlled accounts. The confirmed facts are the amount, the transaction count and the arrests; how the attackers reached the API is not yet established.

What it signals. Payout-API abuse is now a repeat pattern, not a one-off: PayU follows the same advisory that warned the sector in October, and it sits alongside the NBFC loan-diversion fraud (§5) as the week's second case of money being moved through a legitimate disbursement flow rather than stolen through a perimeter breach.

BFSI relevance. Direct. Any firm operating a payout API — payment processors, lenders, marketplaces — should treat payout endpoints as a fraud control point, not just an integration point: transaction-velocity limits, destination-account allowlists and out-of-band approval for new payout destinations are the relevant controls.

7. Fraud — digital-arrest scams and the Supreme Court hearing: outcome still unverified

What happened. "Digital arrest" scams — video calls from someone impersonating police, CBI or customs, threatening the victim and demanding money transfers — reached India's Supreme Court as a suo motu case, In Re: Victims of Digital Arrest Related to Forged Documents. In an order dated 29 October, uploaded on 10 November, the Court directed WhatsApp and other intermediaries to tell the Court what measures they propose to prevent such scams and to submit those proposals to the Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C); asked the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for a list of authorised financial institutions; and directed state law-enforcement agencies to report the FIRs registered in digital-arrest cases. The Court is turning a fraud pattern into duties for three parties at once: platforms must show how they will stop the scams, banks face compensation liability where due diligence fails, and the RBI must produce new KYC rules on a fixed timetable. 9. Infostealers / POS malware — over a million cards stolen from unpatched shop tills

What happened. Cybernews reported on 18 November that researchers found an exposed attacker server holding the complete workings of a campaign against point-of-sale terminals: malware source code, exploitation tools, and a carding panel named "VINY JUNIOR TRILHAS" storing at least one million unique credit and debit card records (primary account numbers, validity periods, bank identification numbers, issuing countries and banks). The server held records for 782 compromised systems across more than 20 countries, most of them in Brazil, with the stolen cards skewing toward Brazilian banks; the operator is assessed as Brazilian based on the language in recovered documentation. The malware is a remote access trojan that exploited CVE-2024-49138, a Windows Common Log File System (CLFS) privilege-escalation flaw Microsoft patched in December 2024; every compromised terminal seen was running software that had not been updated for at least one year and nine months when the server was found in August 2026. The exposed server was observed closed on 31 August 2026 and the findings were reported to Brazil's CERT (CTIR) on 8 September 2026. The initial access route onto the terminals is unconfirmed, and the specific RAT family is not named.

How it works. Once on a terminal, the malware scrapes card data from memory as transactions are processed — the same approach POS malware families have used since Backoff and BlackPOS in 2014 — and feeds it into the carding panel. Because the terminals stayed in daily use, the stolen dataset kept growing as customers paid.

What it signals. A flaw patched nearly two years ago is still yielding a million-card harvest because the endpoint fleet was never updated — patch debt, not a new vulnerability, is the story. It is a research finding (an exposed attacker server), not a single victim breach, and the card count is a lower bound from the panel, not a confirmed count of fraud losses.

BFSI relevance. Direct. Over a million card records, mostly Brazilian-issued, are in criminal hands; issuing banks should watch for unauthorised transactions and consider reissue for cards used at affected merchants. The lesson for acquirers and merchants is fleet patching: POS terminals that cannot take updates are a standing card-data exposure.

10. Supply chain — OPTIMJACK: one trusted script put malware on 178 websites

On 16 November, attackers exploited a vulnerability in Optimizely's Web Experimentation sign-up process — dubbed "OPTIMJACK" by independent researcher Amirul Cyber — to add malicious JavaScript to customer projects. Optimizely's CDN then delivered the code with a valid certificate from a domain every affected site already trusted. The payload, rebuilt 26 times in a single day, swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses (Bitcoin, Ethereum and EVM chains, TRON, Solana, TON, Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash, Dogecoin) in page text, form fields, links, images, the clipboard and outgoing requests; a card skimmer added at 13:43 UTC watched form submissions and POST requests for card number, expiry and CVV and sent them to 1688360.cc/card.php; it also collected non-HttpOnly cookies and page screenshots, and tried to deliver the Coruna and DarkSword exploit kits to iPhones and iPads on older iOS versions. How it works. No victim organisation was breached. The attacker edited one field — the custom Project JavaScript in Optimizely customer projects — and the trusted CDN did the distribution. The vulnerability, per Optimizely's incident report, was a sign-up-flow flaw allowing administrator impersonation without stolen customer passwords.

What it signals. This is the week's cleanest supply-chain lesson: the trust boundary is the third-party script, not the perimeter. A single SaaS experimentation tool turned 178 major websites, including banks, into malware distributors for a day, and the payload's evolution within hours (clipper, then skimmer, then exploit kits) shows how quickly a foothold in a trusted channel is monetised.

BFSI relevance. Direct. Thirty-two banking and finance organisations were among the affected sites, and their visitors — including customers entering card details on first-party forms — were exposed to skimming. Banks should inventory third-party scripts on customer-facing pages, apply subresource integrity or script allowlisting where feasible, and treat a supplier's sign-up flow as an attack surface.

1. AI as a target

1.1 Dragos canary research — prompt theft from inside AI inference providers; OpenRouter removes three providers and 125 Zero Data Retention endpoints

What happened. On 19 November, Dragos (HD Moore, Chief Research Officer) published five months of canary-token research: about two million prompts carrying unique URLs and canary credentials (AWS keys, DNS names, git and SSH targets) were sent to widely used AI services between July and November 2026, and the team watched where those secrets surfaced. Three findings: attackers were actively stealing data from inside at least three AI inference providers — the earliest breach began in mid-July and none of the three knew until Dragos told them; marketplace resellers could view and retain customer prompts using standard logging, with no exploit needed; and providers appeared not to honour privacy settings, including requests marked Zero Data Retention (ZDR). In the largest breach, a couple of hundred planted AWS keys were stolen in short windows in late October — every request sent in those windows, including requests the provider rejected before a model ran. The thief tested keys within hours, listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, tried creating Bedrock API keys and adding IAM users, driving tens of thousands of AWS API calls. On 1 November the same attacker used ARTEX, the LLM-driven penetration-testing framework, "only to confirm that keys it already held were still live" — the ARTEX user agents artex-agent and artex-judge-r121 appear in CloudTrail and, Dragos says, in no public indicator list. On 22 November OpenRouter off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from ZDR eligibility (some as a precaution) and notified all potentially affected customers. Provider notices followed: Phala (preliminary report, 10 November) — an attacker took control of an inference server; requests to DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 routed via OpenRouter 21 October 00:00 UTC to 8 November 05:35 UTC should be treated as exposed and all secrets rotated; contained 8 November. Together AI (21 November) — prompt samples kept in a private Hugging Face repository for testing were downloaded by an unauthorised party after an AI coding agent uploaded the repository credential to a public repo; five tested credentials were Dragos canaries. Novita (22 November) — some MiniMax requests from 28 May to 11 November were forwarded to MiniMax's official API instead of Novita-hosted infrastructure.

Why this role. The AI system itself is the victim: the inference supply chain — routers, resellers, marketplaces, hosting providers — is where customer prompts and credentials were read, retained and stolen. The attack surface is the AI service, not a conventional application behind it.

Evidence (source's own wording). Dragos: "We found attackers actively stealing data from three different hosting providers. Although our prompts were bait, they were processed along with real customer requests, and those were almost certainly copied too." On ZDR: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside." On the resellers: "The marketplace sellers that we caught only had to list a model at the lowest price, enable request logging, and wait." OpenRouter: "Off-boarded three providers from OpenRouter, based on the results of our investigations" and "Removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention (ZDR) eligibility", adding "These findings did not show any breach or unauthorized access of OpenRouter."

BFSI relevance. Banks and payment firms that route prompts containing customer data, credentials or internal code through third-party inference providers or routers cannot rely on a Zero Data Retention flag: Dragos shows the flag binds only the first hop, and real customer prompts processed alongside the bait were almost certainly copied too. Any key, token or password ever sent in a prompt to an affected provider should be treated as exposed and rotated; firms should inventory which third-party inference providers and routers handle their prompts, verify privacy settings actually change routing, prefer direct first-party providers or self-hosting for sensitive workloads, and seed canary credentials in AI pipelines as tripwires — Dragos' own recommendation.

Status caveat. Whether the three providers Dragos caught are the same three OpenRouter removed is unconfirmed — neither side has named them; do not present them as identical. Dragos does not name the three compromised providers or the Chinese lab whose systems fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including roughly two-thirds of requests marked no-collection or ZDR.

1.2 Operation ArgonHunt — an automated pipeline harvesting and validating AI API keys at scale

What happened. SOCRadar reported (first reported 17 November, re-reported 20 November) that routine threat hunting found a Linux staging server serving its /tmp directory over HTTP port 8099, exposing about 700 MB / 1,600+ files of attack scripts, logs and stolen data. The same operator ran two operations from that server: a deep compromise of an unnamed Türkiye-based cryptocurrency exchange (read/write access to the production database, forged withdrawal requests), and an automated pipeline holding 44,096 unique AI API keys across Gemini (26,633), OpenAI (14,504), OpenRouter, Anthropic and xAI, of which the operator's own recheck logs marked 2,686 as active — plus 519 Google Cloud tokens and sniffed live service-account tokens. The exchange has not publicly confirmed the breach, and SOCRadar did not verify on-chain settlement of the forged withdrawal requests, so those figures are attempted theft, not confirmed losses.

Why this role. The harvested asset is AI access itself: provider API keys are collected, validated and stockpiled as a commodity, ready to be spent on inference or resold. The keys are the target, not a by-product.

Evidence. SOCRadar: "The same operator runs an automated pipeline holding 44,096 stolen AI API keys, 2,686 of them confirmed working." The analysis was passive — SOCRadar "did not use any credential found in the directory and did not touch victim systems."

BFSI relevance. AI provider keys used by banks, fintechs and their vendors are being harvested and validated at scale; a leaked key is a billable, attributable asset that can be spent on inference or used to impersonate the firm to a model provider. Rotate any AI provider key that may have transited a shared or third-party service, review AI API billing for anomalies, and prefer project-scoped keys with spend limits over administrative keys.

1.3 Microsoft agent-framework PyPI hijack — a Cairn-labelled credential stealer in the AI-agent supply chain

What happened. At 20:35 UTC on 17 November, a malicious version (1.26.1) of Microsoft's official agent-framework Python package (Microsoft Agent Framework, the SDK for building AI agents) was uploaded to PyPI; a second version, 1.26.2, followed at 21:08 UTC. Both were unavailable by about 21:30 UTC. Wiz assesses that a client certificate and private key for Microsoft's Engineering System Release Pipeline (ESRP) were compromised to publish them. The stealer drops a .pth file into the site-packages root, so it runs whenever Python starts in that environment, even if agent-framework is never imported. It harvested environment variables and credential files — including LLM API keys and Hugging Face tokens — from AI-agent development environments, and requested fresh identity tokens from GitHub Actions, Azure DevOps and Azure. The tooling identified itself as "Cairn", an open-source AI penetration-testing framework: it staged the package at /tmp/cairn_pkg and sent a CAIRN_ESRP_PROBE2 connectivity probe. Wiz could read only the 50 requests stored on the attacker's free-tier webhook — three victim exfiltrations were observed in that sample, not a verified victim total. The most recent safe version is 1.26.0. Microsoft has still published no security advisory.

Why this role. The target is the AI-agent development stack itself: a package that AI-agent builders install, compromised through the vendor's own release pipeline, with a stealer aimed at the credentials AI developers hold — LLM API keys, Hugging Face tokens, CI identity tokens.

Evidence. Wiz: "The tooling identifies itself as Cairn, an open-source AI penetration-testing framework: it staged the package at /tmp/cairn_pkg and sent a CAIRN_ESRP_PROBE2 connectivity probe." And: "The attacker appears to have compromised a client certificate and private key for Microsoft ESRP (Engineering System Release Pipeline), the internal service Microsoft uses to sign and publish this package."

BFSI relevance. Banks building AI agents on Microsoft's framework, or whose vendors do, had a window of about an hour in which a routine pip install pulled a credential stealer from the legitimate publisher account — package name, publisher and earlier releases all looked correct. Anyone who installed 1.26.1 or 1.26.2 should rebuild from 1.26.0 and rotate every secret the environment could reach, including LLM API keys. The Cairn self-identification is the tooling's own label, not proof of the operator's identity.

1.4 LiteLLM CVE-2026-40217 — the LLM gateway in CISA KEV; attribution contested

What happened. LiteLLM — the open-source LLM gateway/proxy that sits in front of model providers and holds their API keys — had CVE-2026-40217 added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 November (federal remediation due 2 December). Evidence. The KEV listing itself is the confirmed fact; the attribution is contested and is not presented as settled. The flaw itself is CVSS 4.0 5.9.

2. AI as a weapon or accelerator

2.1 Cairn / MONAI Label — autonomous AI agents find and exploit a zero-day in the wild, reaching root

What happened. On 20 November, Amazon Threat Intelligence (published by Amazon CISO CJ Moses on the AWS Security Blog) reported that a threat actor used autonomous AI agents running the open-source Cairn framework to find a previously unknown flaw in MONAI Label, an open-source medical-imaging annotation server, and gain root access on a university server. Amazon found the operator's own Cairn control panel exposed on the internet without authentication on 10 September, which revealed task settings, prompts and run history. The campaign ran about 48 hours from 8 September against university IP addresses in East Asia outside mainland China. The operator gave the agents one instruction — "obtain shell access" — and the agents planned, executed and adjusted their approach themselves. Against a MONAI Label server they mapped the API, found an upload endpoint with a filename parameter, and went from mapping the API to arbitrary file write in about 11 minutes; in a later session they planted a Python reverse shell in sitecustomize.py and triggered a training job to spawn a Python process as root. The flaw is a path traversal in PUT /datastore/image and PUT /datastore/label, affecting MONAI Label 0.8.5 and earlier, which runs with authentication off by default. It is now CVE-2026-133968 (9.8); the fix was merged into the source tree on 19 October and shipped in MONAI Label 0.8.6 on 22 November, the first packaged release containing it. Amazon assesses with high confidence it was a zero-day and with moderate confidence the actor is China-nexus (Simplified Chinese prompts, China Standard Time script timestamps, targeting of universities likely of strategic interest to China); no group is named. No patient scans or clinical systems were touched.

Why this role. This is the week's clearest weapon case: AI agents did the vulnerability discovery and exploitation themselves, with minimal human direction — the operator set the goal and the targets, the agents chose the methods. It is also the first publicly documented case of offensive AI agents finding a novel vulnerability during a live intrusion and turning it into a working exploit.

Evidence (source's own wording, verified verbatim from the AWS Security Blog). "The actor gave the agents a simple goal: 'obtain shell access.' During the campaign, the agents found a zero-day in an open-source application and used it to gain full control of a server." "In about 11 minutes, the agents went from mapping the application's API to arbitrarily writing files on the server." "Amazon Threat Intelligence assesses with high confidence that it was a zero-day based on the absence of any corresponding fix, issue, advisory, or prior mention in vulnerability databases for the affected open-source project." On tempo: "An agent that discovers and exploits a zero-day in minutes completes its activity faster than many security operations centers can manually triage an alert. The agents that failed against the hardened Redis target ran continuously for 16 hours without breaks, sleep, or working-hour constraints."

What the claim rests on, and its limits. The "first confirmed case" reading is Amazon's own reconstruction from the exposed Cairn panel — Amazon states it is its own investigation and it has not been independently confirmed. The zero-day assessment is high-confidence, the China-nexus attribution moderate-confidence, and the second university network the actor tunnelled into is unconfirmed as compromised ("we infer the actor had used the zero-day to compromise it"). The agents also failed elsewhere in the same campaign: a 16-hour, 148-action attempt against an open Redis instance, a 6-hour GPU-server credential spray of 30,000+ combinations, Log4Shell injection and SSRF attempts all failed. The lesson is not that agents always succeed; it is that they persist without human direction and, against a soft target, find and exploit a novel flaw in minutes. Amazon also notes Gambit Security documented a separate, financially motivated actor using Cairn against online retailers in September — Cairn is being adopted in both espionage and criminal operations.

BFSI relevance. No financial target was hit in this campaign, and no bank system is implicated. The BFSI point is the precedent and the control lesson: agent frameworks left publicly accessible without authentication hand defenders the attacker's own run history — and hand attackers a machine-speed intrusion capability. Any AI annotation, agent or automation framework a bank or vendor runs must never be internet-reachable with authentication off; MONAI Label servers should be upgraded to 0.8.6 or isolated.

Sources: AWS Security Blog — Chinese-nexus actor's Cairn AI agents found a zero-day on their own (20 Nov) · Daily briefing 23 Nov 2026 — watchlist row, CVE-2026-133968

2.2 ARTEX — named victims this week: Barracuda and Bitrise

What happened. ARTEX, the open-source LLM-driven autonomous penetration-testing framework, stopped being only a vendor-research story this week and produced named victims with first-party disclosures.

  • Barracuda (disclosed 11 November; covered in the window's briefings). Barracuda's CISO described how an attacker entered its cloud environment using a cloud access key Barracuda had issued to an outside AI vendor for a past project and never retired; Barracuda believes the attacker stole the key from the vendor's GitHub repository in October. Between 1 and 2 November the attacker moved through about ten cloud accounts in 44 hours, chaining one secret to the next — stored secrets, a source-code access token, build-system secrets, Kubernetes clusters — hunting credentials and cryptocurrency (wallet, mnemonic and seed-phrase material). Barracuda matched the activity to ARTEX from probe names, the order of operations and built-in credential-hunting routines, and called it a "machine-speed intrusion". It was discovered on 4 November; an attempt to create a new administrative cloud identity was blocked. The FBI's FLASH of 13 November (pre-window background) warned ARTEX had been in malicious use since at least August 2026, with some intrusions reaching privileged access in under three hours.
  • Bitrise (incident report published 19 November). Over 25 days (25 October–18 November) an operator pointed ARTEX at the mobile CI/CD platform, at first posing as an "authorised pentest" while mapping the public surface, then returning as an ordinary customer to run its own builds and test cross-customer access. On 13 November at 11:46:48 UTC the attacker signed in to a dormant internal test bot account with no two-factor authentication, using a valid username and password; less than eleven minutes later the agents were using cloud keys from that workspace's projects, and over the next ten hours chained one credential into the next — signing files, source code, infrastructure state and build logs. Bitrise detected the activity at 16:32 UTC and switched the whole platform off at 20:34 UTC. Files in 15 customer workspaces were accessed without authorisation (one code-signing certificate file, one provisioning profile, 13 diagnostic build logs from May 2026, one holding a credential and another an email address); all 15 customers were contacted. Bitrise revoked all 919 cloud service-account keys and every GitHub OAuth app token, wiped and reinstalled thousands of build machines, and when the attacker returned on 18 November and replayed the attack, every attempt was refused. Bitrise identifies ARTEX by three fingerprints matching the framework's public source code, and notes it could not establish which language model drove the agents or who the operator is.

Why this role. In both cases the AI framework is the accelerator: a planner agent chooses next steps and worker agents run tools and record results, so a single stolen credential becomes a multi-system intrusion in hours, at machine speed, without the operator choosing each step. The entry points were ordinary — an unretired vendor key, a dormant bot account with no MFA — and the AI tooling did the chaining.

Evidence. Barracuda: the attack "used machine-style labels, particular probing methods and built-in hunting routines that match ARTEX". Bitrise: "Between October 25 and November 18, 2026, an operator pointed ARTEX, an open-source AI penetration-testing platform, at Bitrise."

BFSI relevance. The pattern is directly transferable to banks: a vendor key that outlives its project, or a dormant service account without MFA, plus an agentic framework that chains credentials on its own. The controls are the boring ones — retire vendor credentials when the work ends, enforce MFA on every service and bot account, and hunt for the ARTEX user agents (artex-agent, artex-judge-r121, ARTEX-Nuclei-Fingerprint/1.0, ARTEX-CredHunter/2.0) and the X-ARTX-KEY request header. The FBI FLASH and the Barracuda disclosure are pre-window (11 and 13 November) but were covered in the window's briefings and are the background for the in-window Bitrise report and the Netskope skill below.

Sources: Barracuda — Forty-four hours: Anatomy of a machine-speed intrusion (11 Nov) · Bitrise — Anatomy of an AI-driven attack: What ARTEX looks like in action (19 Nov)

2.4 Ukraine / FBI arrests — AI-voice vishing confirmed as a ransomware entry point

What happened. Ukrainian police, working with the FBI, arrested four suspected key members of a ransomware group on 18–19 November after a two-year investigation. The group won initial access with AI-generated voice calls impersonating IT support: it built its own platform with AI-generated voice messages to automate fraudulent calls, tricking employees into disclosing credentials or installing remote-access software (TeamViewer / AnyDesk). In one incident an attacker kept an employee on the phone while another accessed the company's systems. At least 28 companies paid more than $40.5 million in ransoms. Evidence. BleepingComputer: the group "built its own platform with AI-generated voice messages to automate fraudulent calls" impersonating IT support. The 23 November briefing adds the named victims and the seizure details from the Ukrainian announcement.

2.7 ruby2shell — an AI agent does lab-only exploit development on a critical flaw within days

What happened. Ruby disclosed CVE-2026-132825 ("ruby2shell") on 19 November: a one-byte heap overflow in string case conversion, rated Critical by Ruby, reachable without authentication in default Ruby on Rails and classic-style Sinatra applications. On 20 November a repository (genaforvena, verified genuine by the daily briefing's code read) showed an autonomous AI agent (OpenCode, about €500 credit budget) taking the flaw from crash reproduction to tcache poisoning in a single session, then to what the repo grades as rung 5 (working remote command execution) under lab conditions — one multipart POST driving a forged CRuby T_DATA finalizer, with an independent clean-archive replay. 3. AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

3.1 MAS — do not give AI agents your bank login; a "know your agent" protocol is coming

What happened. On 19 November, Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) Managing Director Chia Der Jiun told the Singapore FinTech Festival that "some users are giving agents their login credentials to financial accounts" and: "I would not advise giving personal agents such access, as there is insufficient assurance of safety." His concern is that a bank cannot tell whether an agent logging in as a customer has the customer's full authority, or whether it is staying within its mandate — and who is responsible if it transacts incorrectly or beyond its instructions. MAS will facilitate a common "know your agent" protocol so banks can verify an agent's identity, whom it represents, what authority and mandate it holds, and its origins and reliability, as the next phase of its Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime (SAFR) initiative. MAS will also set up an Agentic Money Sandbox with the Future of Finance Institute, covering corporate treasury management and payment services with tokenised deposits. Agents could move money and allocate assets more quickly, Chia warned, raising liquidity and financial-stability risks — financial institutions may need to impose limits on agent-directed transactions. No timeline was given for the protocol and no start date for the sandbox. Context: the CSA / GovTech / IMDA joint advisory AD-2026-015 (6 November, pre-window standing guidance) already warns against giving personal AI agents one-time passwords, and AXS said on 18 November it is opening its network of 800+ Singapore billers to AI agents.

Why this role. This is the week's sharpest control problem stated by a regulator: the agent is a new kind of account holder that existing authentication cannot describe — a login proves the credential, not the mandate.

Evidence. Chia, quoted in the Straits Times: "Some users are giving agents their login credentials to financial accounts. I would not advise giving personal agents such access, as there is insufficient assurance of safety."

BFSI relevance. Direct and immediate for Singapore banks and any firm whose customers use AI agents: credential-sharing by customers defeats the bank's ability to establish authorisation, and the liability question (who bears a loss when an agent oversteps) is open. Until a "know your agent" channel exists, banks need terms and channels for agent access, limits on agent-directed transactions, and customer guidance not to hand logins to personal agents.

Note on sourcing. The MAS speech text itself was not retrieved verbatim in this run; the account rests on the Straits Times report of the 19 November keynote, corroborated by Fintech News Singapore and TechNode. The 20 November daily briefing carries the same item.

3.2 UK — Bank of England / FCA / PRA consult on cyber resilience, with AI and quantum effective practices

What happened. On 19 November the Prudential Regulation Authority, the Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority issued a joint consultation (PRA CP17/26, FCA GC26/9) on cyber risk and resilience management — principles-based, outcome-focused expectations for how firms manage cyber risk and resilience, aligned with the NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework. The consultation closes on Friday 26 February 2027; final guidance is expected in 2027. Alongside it, the regulators published separate effective practices on AI and quantum risks, covering vulnerabilities to threat actors using AI and the implications of quantum computing. The proposed guidance adds no new rules; it clarifies how firms meet existing requirements on governance, operational resilience, operational risk and third-party risk in a cyber context, across six areas: governance; identification and assessment; protection; detection; response and recovery; and assurance and continuous improvement. Context: October 2026 saw the second-highest monthly number of cyber incidents reported to the FCA in five years, 52% above the previous 18-month peak in May 2025, and the Bank's Systemic Risk Survey cites cyber as one of the risks that would have the greatest impact on the UK financial system.

Why this role. The AI element here is governance, not an incident: regulators are writing AI into the cyber-resilience expectations for financial firms — what threat actors can do with AI, and what firms must be able to show about their own defences.

Evidence. Bank of England: "On 19 November 2026, the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), the Bank of England (the Bank), and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) (collectively the regulators) issued a joint consultation paper (CP) on cyber risk and resilience management." Computer Weekly on the AI element: the regulators are "separately publishing additional 'effective practices' which will build on the wider cyber guidance by zooming in on specific risks linked to the runaway evolution of AI".

BFSI relevance. UK banks, insurers, financial market infrastructures and FCA-regulated firms in scope of operational-resilience requirements should respond by 26 February 2027 and, in the meantime, treat the AI effective practices as the direction of travel for supervisory expectations on AI-driven threats and AI use inside the firm.

3.5 Visa Fall 2026 Biannual Threats Report — AI is accelerating traditional fraud, not creating new categories

What happened. Visa's Fall 2026 Biannual Threats Report (released 17 November, covering January–June 2026) finds that AI is not creating entirely new categories of fraud. Incidents involving third-party agents rose 62% against the prior six months, with third parties linked to about 56% of compromised payment credentials identified; Visa Scam Disruption identified more than $1.4 billion in potential fraud, up 40%. Why this role. This is the control framing for the whole week: the fraud looks like ordinary fraud, only faster — and the third party, the agent and the platform are now part of the attack surface.
