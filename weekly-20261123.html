The week in one paragraph

Third-party risk was the week's spine: a marketing platform (Plaid KARTE), a data-platform supplier (reported, not confirmed, in the n0n case), a digital-banking middleware vendor (BankWeaver), a payments platform contractor (Paydon) and a credit-union IT provider (Wellington IT) each turned one compromise into many firms' incidents. Japan was the centre of gravity, with Rakuten Securities confirmed at up to about 15.7 million accounts and KARTE client disclosures passing 140 million records as a tally, not a headcount. Korea's breach wave produced another securities firm and a sector-wide inspection, India produced payout-API and loan-diversion fraud at scale, and regulators in the UK, Singapore, Korea, Japan and India all converted incidents into deadlines or new oversight. The customer-trust cost is now measurable in share prices, class-action recruitment and public apologies from chief executives.

1. Plaid KARTE — one marketing platform, about 2,200 companies' data

One marketing-platform breach became a credential incident for hundreds of firms at once, and handovers from 24 November will produce a second disclosure wave.

What happened. Attacked 13 November: a vulnerability behind a public data-retrieval route was exploited, then stolen credentials reached the KARTE admin console. Investigation completed 19 November, published 20 November: data from about 2,200 companies leaked or potentially leaked in four overlapping groups — integration credentials (secret keys and access tokens) for 754 client companies; data customers had linked into KARTE for 570 companies; end-user behaviour data for 487 companies; cached data treated as potentially leaked for up to 409 companies, an estimate, because the cache is overwritten quickly and Plaid cannot tell which items were actually read. No attribution published. KARTE stays offline, staged restart targeted for mid-January 2027; per-company handovers begin 24 November; Plaid forecasts a ¥2 billion loss.

Disclosures: JINS, about 18.29 million member accounts, 17 November; MUFG Bank, about 6.5 million records, 18 November (wider MUFG group totalled about 8 million records across five businesses — a different scope, not a conflict); SMBC, up to about 14 million records, 20 November — largest single-bank figure, a maximum, not a confirmed count, exposing date of birth, age band and sex, with no names, contacts or account numbers; ZOZO, about 29.18 million records, and Marui Group, about 17.33 million (including about 8.5 million EPOS card holders), 20 November; DHC, about 19.11 million, and Sharp, up to 3.26 million, 18–19 November; Bank of Yokohama, about 116,500. Published counts passed 140 million records — a tally with overlaps, not 140 million people; no deduplicated count of affected people has been published. Former clients also hit: Stripe International's data remained after it ended KARTE in May 2026, and overflow ended its contract in November 2023 yet its data remained for about three years. (Separately, the SMBC Consumer Finance incident, up to 320,118 people, is a different incident, not part of the KARTE total.)

2. Rakuten Securities — up to about 15.7 million accounts, regulator now involved

A test environment holding real customer data became a production-grade incident, with regulator and legal exposure arriving in the same week.

What happened. Disclosed 17 November that customer data in a test-environment system may have leaked; confirmed 18 November. Up to about 11.25 million accounts' login IDs exposed, and up to about 4.47 million accounts had names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, employers, withdrawal bank accounts, declared income and financial-asset data exposed — the two groups do not overlap, so the maximum is about 15.72 million accounts. Possible unauthorised access detected 16 November; test-environment data may have leaked 11 November; cause under investigation. No passwords or PINs confirmed leaked; no unauthorised transactions attributable to the incident confirmed. Japan's Financial Services Agency issued a reporting order 19 November; Financial Services Minister Satsuki Katayama addressed the leak 20 November, and the FSA will consider a business improvement order if information-management flaws are found. Rakuten Bank shares fell about 15% on 18 November. A lawyer-led class-action recruitment site passed 20,000 registrants by 14:00 JST on 20 November. A fake "compensation policy" screenshot is circulating; Rakuten has announced no compensation, and contacts asking for lawsuit fees are fake.

3. n0n publishes Visa, JPMorgan Chase and Mastercard customer data — via a shared supplier, reported

Three global financial brands were hit through one shared supplier, reported not confirmed, turning supplier concentration into a single point of failure.

What happened. After its 48-hour deadline set on 16 November expired, n0n published customer marketing data attributed to all three firms on 19 November. JPMorgan confirmed third-party service providers were targeted and marketing data that may include customer names and email addresses was accessed; Visa confirmed an incident at an Asia-Pacific third-party provider, its own systems unaffected; Mastercard confirmed a limited exposure from one benefit programme. Published sets: about 16,000 JPMorgan Reserve customer email addresses (plus 124 employee emails), over 6,000 Visa premium customers' details in Asia-Pacific, and about 2,000 alleged Mastercard entries that researchers say look like test data rather than genuine customer records. No card numbers, passwords or balances in the published sets. BleepingComputer reported 20 November that n0n claims it attacked data-platform provider Qubole on 11 November and used compromised Qubole credentials to reach customers' AWS environments; Qubole has not responded and none of the three firms has named a vendor, so that link stays reported, not confirmed. Bitdefender's controlling caveat stands: 100+ n0n listings against just over 20 real targets — the group inflates victim counts.

4. Korea — the FSI audit becomes a sector-wide inspection, then another securities firm is breached

A breach at the sector's own assessment body became a 274-firm regulatory inspection, while the breach wave kept finding new victims through peripheral systems.

What happened. Financial Security Institute (FSI) breach, before this window: a webshell attack at 13:54 on 9 November failed, then an SMS-authentication bypass created a fake FSS-level account. The Financial Services Commission (FSC) gave that timeline to the National Assembly on 13 November; its special audit of the FSI began 16 November. The FSI evaluation system was still shut down at the end of the window, with no restoration date, and 2,912 firms form the assessment population whose appeals are blocked. Wider Korean wave: 37 firms and about 473,000 records in one month.

On 17 November the Financial Supervisory Service (FSS) called in CISOs and security officers from 227 electronic-finance and 47 online investment-linked firms — 274 firms in total — for an intensive external-attack-surface inspection, with first-round self-assessments due 30 November. Same day it disclosed new incidents at Spoon (Giftiell, about 18,000 people's data) and Zoomfund (about KRW 300 million in customer deposits), and began surprise on-site inspections of high-risk branches at four banks, the first in about ten years. The FSC will require large firms to run vulnerability assessments twice a year instead of once, and to identify and classify all IT assets.

More victims: Coinone, via customer-support contractor MetaM (disclosed 15 November; passwords, resident registration numbers, bank account numbers and wallet addresses may have leaked; victim count unconfirmed; disclosure took about 70 hours 40 minutes); KOMSCO's chak gift-certificate platform (3,083 customers confirmed); Mirae Asset Life Insurance (10,418 victims as of 5 November, up from about 6,800 — crossing the 10,000-person 72-hour reporting duty). KB Kookmin count revised from 119 to 2,210 after FSS inspection. Then, on 23 November, Samsung Securities disclosed that an unauthorised outsider accessed its Voice of Customer complaints system — traces found on the afternoon of 21 November — and exfiltrated attachments from 8,241 customers across 13 fields, including name, account number, address, contact details and driver's licence number. Only customers who had filed through the complaints system are affected; trading and payment systems are not reported breached. The firm blocked the IP, suspended the service, reported to the FSS and the Korea Internet & Security Agency, and chief executive Park Jong-moon apologised; it says it will compensate customers if actual loss is confirmed. The firm is described as another Korean securities firm in the current breach wave, not numbered, because the other firms are not named in the wire entry or any briefing in the window.

5. KEPCO — a utility breach whose exposed data is financial data

A utility lost bank and card data for about 650,000 bill-payment users, so Korean banks face fraud arriving from outside the sector.

What happened. State-run Korea Electric Power Corp. confirmed 22 November that personal data of about 1.52 million people leaked from two systems: 650,487 users of its KEPCO ON customer-service and bill-payment platform and 869,761 users of its electronic procurement system (SRM), for a combined 1,520,248 records. KEPCO ON exposed 12 categories including names, resident registration numbers, phone numbers, addresses, bank names, account numbers, account-holder names, card issuers, card numbers and cardholder names, plus billing records; SRM exposed corporate representatives' and employees' names, phone numbers and emails. An external attacker accessed both systems; KEPCO discovered the breach 20 November, blocked external IP access and suspended some services, filed with the Personal Information Protection Commission on 22 November, and is working with financial companies to prevent secondary damage. Its president issued a public apology. Second breach in as many months, after an October leak of employee records. Per-system counts differ slightly between outlets (650,487 vs 654,487 KEPCO ON users) — neither figure is chosen here.

6. Paydon / Infcurion — a payments platform halted across four banks

One contractor's breach halted a payment service at four banks at once, showing how shared regional platforms turn one incident into a joint outage.

What happened. Infcurion, contractor running the Wallet Station payment platform behind Japan's Paydon app, confirmed unauthorised access on 19 November. A total of 251,172 records leaked, including member names, dates of birth, phone numbers, sex, email addresses and passwords, plus bank account information, point balances and regional promotion voucher point balances, transaction history and merchant information. Four Kagoshima financial institutions — Kagoshima Bank, Minami Nippon Bank, Kagoshima Mutual Credit Cooperative and Kagoshima Shinkin Bank — halted all Paydon services at 06:20 JST on 20 November. Infcurion's third report (noon JST, 21 November) confirms no new unauthorised access since the system was cut off on 19 November; from 20 November it paused Wallet Station for providers that were not the target of the leak so emergency checks could be carried out, and providers whose checks found no intrusion and no leak began restarting in stages from 21 November. The provider whose data leaked remains suspended with no restart date, and no misuse of the leaked information had been confirmed. Missed by the 20, 21 and 22 November briefs and caught as a wire catch-up on 22 November.

A vendor-run analytics tool exposed corporate customers' credentials and contact data, while the bank's core systems were untouched.

Fukuoka Financial Group announced 19 November that a data analysis tool used by FFG Industries to aggregate data from its ordering and other systems — set up and run by an outside vendor — was accessed by a third party, and that part of the data in the databases connected to the tool may have leaked. 8. Banco do Brasil — a misused valid credential, no count disclosed

A valid credential, misused, gave access to the fields needed to impersonate the bank — with no victim count disclosed to judge the scale.

What happened. Banco do Brasil identified unauthorised access to customer data on 12 November and told affected customers on 18 November. Access came from misuse of a valid credential by third parties; the credential has been revoked and monitoring reinforced. Exposed fields may include names, CPF tax IDs, email addresses, branch numbers and, in some cases, account numbers. The bank says there was no invasion of its core systems and that passwords, tokens, security codes and financial transactions were not exposed or linked to the incident. It has reported the case to the authorities under Brazil's LGPD data-protection law. It has not disclosed how many customers were affected, whose credential was misused, or how the third parties obtained it. A forum sale claiming 22.7 million records is unverified and is not used here.

9. Wellington IT — a credit-union platform breach, disclosed weeks late

One provider serving more than a third of Ireland's credit unions turned one vulnerability into a sector-wide incident, disclosed weeks after the attack.

What happened. Wellington IT, a Belfast-based IT provider whose CU Online platform runs mobile banking for more than a third of Ireland's credit unions, was exploited through a vulnerability in its software. Attack ran between 2 and 4 October, when an automated script using valid credentials made about five million requests for data and successfully retrieved saved payee records — payee names, bank names, IBANs, BICs and payment reference codes — linked to member accounts. (The Irish Times reported a 6 October attack date and about 1.4 million requests; the flaw was closed on 6 October.) About 40 credit unions in the Republic of Ireland are affected (press reporting put the figure at 43; the Data Protection Commission has received around 40 breach notifications), and the data may cover payees of hundreds of thousands of account holders. Member accounts, balances, passwords and PINs were not accessed, and the vulnerability has been closed. The Department of Finance was first made aware on 17 November; affected credit unions were told on 17–18 November — weeks after the attack. Minister of State Robert Troy, who met Wellington IT on 19 November, said the delay was "not acceptable"; Wellington IT said it alerted customers as soon as it had "confirmed the potential risk". At present there is no evidence members' funds have been accessed or fraudulent activity has occurred, and the full extent is still being established by external cybersecurity experts.

Sources: Department of Finance (Ireland) — Statement from Minister of State Robert Troy following meeting with Wellington IT (19 Nov) · The Irish Times — Minister concerned over delay in disclosing cyberattack (18 Nov; 6 October attack date, ~1.4m malicious requests)

10. Cleverbridge — card numbers and CVVs stolen from a payment processor

A processor breach put full card details — number, expiry and CVV — at risk for an unknown population of buyers across many merchants.

What happened. Payment processor Cleverbridge disclosed 22 November that hackers breached its systems and copied payment card data: card number, expiry date and card security code (CVV/CVC) used for a purchase through Cleverbridge. It detected unauthorised activity on 4 November, took systems offline the same day and restored them the same day; its investigation later determined a third party had copied the data. The company has not said how many customers were affected, how attackers got in, how long they were inside, or which merchants' buyers were hit — its listed clients include Red Hat, SUSE, Tenable, Veeam, Jamf, Sony and Parallels. It confirmed the incident was not ransomware and nothing was encrypted, and says it has notified data protection authorities, law enforcement, payment card networks and payment partners, and is working with TransUnion's Cyberscout on notifications and complimentary credit monitoring. Malwarebytes is the only merchant so far to confirm impact, saying only "a small subset" of its customers was affected.

11. BankWeaver — bank middleware turned into a backdoor

The attacker needed no vulnerability: a misconfigured authentication service, open to the internet, handed over an administrator token.

What happened. Forescout Research (Vedere Labs) disclosed "BankWeaver" on 19 November: attackers abused a Temenos Quantum Fabric authentication service configured for anonymous access and reachable from the internet, exchanged an anonymous token for an administrator token without credentials, and deployed two malicious Java components — FabricShell for command execution and database queries, and FabricShadow for host reconnaissance and data exfiltration. One unnamed financial services institution was confirmed compromised in a breach running from early September 2026. Six further banks appear to share the same exposure, but compromise has not been confirmed for them, and Temenos confirmed the cause is a misconfiguration. No MITRE IDs were published.

Source: Forescout Research — BankWeaver (19 Nov)

12. OPTIMJACK — a trusted script becomes a skimmer on 178 organisations' sites

No bank was breached, yet bank customers were skimmed on bank websites through a trusted script the banks themselves had chosen.

What happened. On 16 November, attackers used a vulnerability in Optimizely's Web Experimentation platform — a flaw in the account signup flow that allowed administrator impersonation and unauthorised changes to Project JavaScript — to inject malicious JavaScript into customer projects. Researcher Scott Helme (Report URI) identified 188 infected snippets associated with 178 organisations, including HSBC, Equifax, Ledger, Zoom, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab and IKEA. The payload (track.js) replaced cryptocurrency wallet addresses, captured payment-card details entered into affected first-party forms, collected non-HttpOnly cookies and page screenshots, and attempted to deliver exploit kits to some iPhone and iPad users. It could not read card fields inside cross-origin hosted payment iframes. Optimizely fixed the vulnerability and removed the injected code the same day; the campaign was largely cleaned by 05:52 SGT on 17 November. No organisation's own servers were breached. 32 of the 178 organisations were in banking and finance. Crypto amounts taken are unreconciled ($7,040 vs $2,300 in different reports) — neither figure is used here as the total; the card-loss count is unknown.

Sources: ReversingLabs — OPTIMJACK: When Trusted JavaScript Becomes a Supply Chain Attack (16 Nov) · Cato Networks — OPTIMJACK Threat Analysis & Risks

13. India — payout-API theft, loan-diversion fraud, and a court setting KYC duties

Fraud abused legitimate money-moving flows — a payout API and approved-but-undisbursed loans — while the Supreme Court turned the pattern into duties for platforms, banks and the RBI.

What happened. PayU Payments' cloud-server breach saw a manipulated payout API siphon ₹25.67 crore (about US$2.9 million) through 265 unauthorised transactions to 156 accounts; two men were arrested on 15 November. Access method still under audit; case follows a CERT-In / CSIRT-Fin payment-API advisory of 9 October. Separately, Delhi Police reported that 12 non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) in Delhi-NCR lost over ₹65 crore in three months to loan-disbursement fraud, with more than 150 NBFC complaints nationwide totalling over ₹300 crore; CyberStrikeAI was used in at least one October case. The fraud diverted approved-but-undisbursed loans: one lender lost ₹41.27 crore in about 90 minutes on 16 November, and Delhi Police report "traces of AI" in the frauds. A wider figure of about ₹105.67 crore across 14 NBFCs is also in circulation — the two figures remain unreconciled, so neither is used here as the loss total. Delhi Police also arrested 22 people in a fake-banking-app crackdown linked to 22,139 victims and proceeds of more than ₹20 crore.

The Supreme Court of India is hearing a suo motu case, In Re: Victims of Digital Arrest Related to Forged Documents, on "digital arrest" scams — video calls from someone impersonating police, CBI or customs, threatening the victim and demanding money transfers. In an order dated 29 October, uploaded on 10 November, the Court directed WhatsApp and other intermediaries to submit proposed preventive measures to the Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C), asked the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for a list of authorised financial institutions, and directed state law-enforcement agencies to report the FIRs registered in digital-arrest cases. The RBI's proposed Know Your Customer (KYC) Amendment Directions are to be notified by 30 November 2026 and operationalised by 31 December 2026. The matter was listed for further consideration on 23 November 2026; the outcome of that hearing was not verified in this window.

14. Binance aftermath — proof of reserves restores some confidence; laundering accelerates anyway

Proof of reserves restored some confidence, but did not touch the laundering, which accelerated sharply after 17 November.

What happened. Binance's 198-token zero-knowledge proof of reserves went live, formally announced on 14 November (snapshot 12 November 00:00 UTC, block height 975,493; BTC at 100.05%). Withdrawals had reopened on 87 networks, with full reopening aimed at about 22 November — but no completion confirmation had appeared by 23 November, and no final root-cause report had appeared in the window's briefings. Binance obtained a $6.5 billion worldwide freezing order from the Abu Dhabi Global Market Courts on 18 November (per the 22 Nov briefing record, not the Elliptic article). Attribution to North Korea's TraderTraitor is per the FBI (31 October).

Elliptic's 20 November update: at least 2,310 BTC (about $149 million) of the stolen funds has gone into coinjoin mixers, about 90% of it since 17 November — 480 BTC on 17 November alone, more than the previous two weeks combined — almost all coordinated by Kruw, a Wasabi Wallet coordinator. Five of the nine largest ETH wallets have been emptied since 17 November, moving about 488,000 ETH (about $860 million). About $2.35 billion remains in addresses linked to the theft, mostly ETH and BTC, and about $2.85 billion is frozen; very little has been cashed out, and the 18,270 BTC stolen directly from Binance has still not moved. 15. Regulators as amplifier — the UK, Singapore, Korea, Japan and India all set deadlines

Incidents became sector-wide work at unusual speed, in the same direction everywhere: third-party risk, AI agents touching money, and shared infrastructure.

What happened. UK: HM Treasury made the second designation tranche under the Critical Third Parties regime on 16 November (SI 2026/1203, in force 23 November): BlackRock Financial Management, Bloomberg, Bottomline Technologies, Broadridge Financial Solutions and Form3 UK join the four cloud providers designated in July, taking the total to nine, with joint Bank of England / PRA / FCA oversight of their systemic services. On 19 November the Bank of England, PRA and FCA jointly consulted on cyber risk and resilience management (CP17/26 / GC26/9) — principles-based guidance clarifying how firms should meet existing governance, operational-resilience, operational-risk and third-party-risk requirements in a cyber context, with no new rules; consultation closes 26 February 2027, and the Bank's latest Systemic Risk Survey cited cyber as one of the risks that would have the greatest impact on the UK financial system.

Cross-cutting read: what the week's geopolitical picture adds up to

The week's incidents were mostly not new kinds of attack; the week's regulation mostly is — suppliers, AI entry points, and regulators amplifying in concert.

Three patterns run through all ten cascades:

  1. Supplier concentration is the main cascade. The FSI, MetaM, KARTE's 754 keys, Wellington IT's credit-union platform, Maileva's postal pipeline, and the suspected shared vendor behind the n0n Visa / JPMorgan / Mastercard publications (Qubole — reported, not confirmed; Qubole has not responded) all show the same shape: one provider's failure becomes dozens of firms' incidents.

  1. Regulators are the amplifier — and this week they amplified in concert. The FSS call-in, Japan's FSA reporting order, India's Supreme Court directions, the UK consultation, Singapore's MAS warning and CSA alert, Ireland's minister convening a supplier, France's prosecutor opening an investigation, and the US KEV due-date cluster all convert single incidents into sector-wide work. The 30 November convergence — Korean self-assessments and RBI KYC notification due the same day — is a coincidence of two separate regimes, not a coordinated deadline, but firms operating in both will feel it as one. The deeper convergence is the direction of travel: from incident response toward standing duties — twice-yearly assessments in Korea, clarified resilience expectations in the UK, "know your agent" identity in Singapore, and compensation liability for banks in India.

An information-disclosure flaw in TYPO3's Form Framework: files uploaded through FileUpload or ImageUpload elements are stored in an unguessable sub-folder that stays the same for the whole form session, but if storing an upload failed — for example because the file name exceeded the filesystem length limit — the raw error message was shown to the frontend user, disclosing the storage path including the unguessable folder name and the server's temporary upload directory. With that, an attacker can determine the public URL of files they upload. If a form allows uploading files browsers can execute, such as HTML documents, this can lead to cross-site scripting on web servers that do not apply the Content-Security-Policy TYPO3 recommends for the form upload target folder. TYPO3 rates it Medium, 6.3 (CVSS 4.0). Affected: TYPO3 10.0.0–10.4.62, 11.0.0–11.5.56, 12.0.0–12.4.51, 13.0.0–13.4.39 and 14.0.0–14.3.11. TYPO3 released fixes for ten vulnerabilities on 17 November; this is the one the vendor confirms is actively exploited.

Exploitation status: vendor-confirmed actively exploited. The advisory states plainly: "This vulnerability is known to be actively exploited." No victim detail has been published, and it was not in CISA KEV as of 23 November.

Fix status. Fixed in 10.4.63, 11.5.57, 12.4.52, 13.4.40 and 14.3.12. Apply the recommended Content-Security-Policy to the form upload target folder as well — the patch closes the disclosure, the CSP limits what an uploaded file can do.

17. ConnectWise ScreenConnect — CVE-2026-84869

For banks this is third-party risk, not a direct one: ask every MSP and vendor whether their technicians' ScreenConnect clients are at 26.6.5 or later — exploitation was confirmed in-window, with full compromise in about nine seconds.

What it is / who is affected. A missing-authorization flaw in the ScreenConnect host client: in both Support and Access sessions, a guest could trigger file-transfer actions that the technician's host client processed without authorization or confirmation — including running the file with elevated rights. No prompt appears on the technician's side. Rated 9.9. Affected: ScreenConnect host clients and Access agents before 26.6.5. Servers are not affected — the flaw lives on the technician's side — so updating the server alone does not fix it. MSP technicians are the target because their workstations hold RMM consoles, saved credentials and access to every customer.

Exploitation status: exploited in the wild (confirmed in-window). The flaw was disclosed 8 September and entered CISA KEV on 11 September, both before this window. What changed this week is confirmation: Huntress reported on 17 November that on 12 November a technician at one MSP joined a blank-named Access session and the client ran a guest-pushed batch file — full compromise in about nine seconds, ending with a rebranded ScreenConnect client connecting to the attacker's relay. Between 20 October and 16 November, at least 688,000 guest "run file" requests were sprayed across 42 MSP ScreenConnect servers from 29 source IPs. A separate campaign planted crypto-exchange-named guest sessions on more than 100 MSP servers, and a further campaign used a vulnerable third-party extension (SCKit) — Huntress distinguishes these from the file-push campaign.

Fix status. Fixed in host client and Access agent 26.6.5, available since 8 September. Interim mitigation: remove the TransferFiles permission.

18. Splunk Enterprise — CVE-2026-76536 and CVE-2026-76535

High for SOCs: two unauthenticated authentication-bypass flaws in Splunk Enterprise, rated 9.4 and 9.1 — Splunk holds the logs and credentials that make every other incident worse.

19. Ghostscript "Upload2Shell" — CVE-2026-125396

Patch the rendering chain, not just the host: a sandbox escape rated 8.5 lets a crafted document run commands as the rendering process — and any service accepting uploads may pass files to a bundled Ghostscript.

What it is / who is affected. A sandbox-escape flaw in Ghostscript's Type 1 font loader: a privileged .forceput operation is called without validating operand types, and a crafted document can break the -dSAFER boundary and run commands as the rendering process. Rated 8.5 (CVSS 4.0) with a local attack vector — but the practical reach is remote, because network-facing services pass user-supplied files to Ghostscript: email previews, uploads and print queues. Affected: Ghostscript before 10.08.0 — including copies bundled inside container images, Python virtual environments and vendor products, which an operating-system package update does not touch. Researcher Paulos Yibelo (pwn.ai) demonstrated command execution through WordPress (via Imagick/ImageMagick media previews), Roundcube webmail (in Roundcube 1.7.2, the sender needed no webmail account — viewing the message triggered it), MediaWiki, CUPS print servers and Google services. Distribution status at the end of the window was uneven: Debian shipped fixes, while Amazon Linux 2, 2023 and the 2027 preview still shipped a vulnerable Ghostscript.

Exploitation status: PoC public, no confirmed exploitation in the wild. The research and a self-contained proof of concept were published 17 November; the CVE itself was published 9 November, and the upstream fix had existed since 8 September — a two-month window in which defenders had no name for the bug. CISA's record lists exploitation as none, and the flaw was not in CISA KEV as of 23 November.

Fix status. Fixed upstream in Ghostscript 10.08.0 (8 September). Update to 10.08.0 or a vendor build with the backport — and verify the copy each service actually uses, not just the system package. Interim mitigation: restrict Ghostscript handling in ImageMagick's policy.xml. Check any bank service accepting document or image uploads — webmail, customer portals, claims and onboarding document intake — for a bundled Ghostscript.

20. MONAI Label — CVE-2026-133968

Upgrade to 0.8.6 and treat any instance exposed before 22 November as compromised: AI agents found and exploited this path-traversal zero-day on their own, reaching a root reverse shell.

What it is / who is affected. A path-traversal flaw in the datastore upload endpoints (PUT /datastore/image and PUT /datastore/label) of MONAI Label, an open-source medical-imaging annotation server. Caller-controlled image IDs and label tags were joined onto storage paths without confining the destination to the datastore, giving arbitrary file write. Authentication is off by default (MONAI_LABEL_AUTH_ENABLE=False), so any network client that can reach the port can trigger it; with authentication on, the ANNOTATOR role is enough. Rated 9.8 (CVSS 3.1). A second path traversal in session uploads (PUT /session/?uncompress=true) was fixed in the same release. Affected: MONAI Label 0.8.5 and earlier — all versions. Research and healthcare software, not core banking; it matters to banks through university and hospital partners, shared research infrastructure, and as the clearest in-window example of AI agents finding and exploiting a zero-day on their own.

Exploitation status: exploited in the wild, by AI agents. Amazon Threat Intelligence reported 20 November that a suspected China-linked operator ran autonomous Cairn AI agents against East Asian university networks for about 48 hours in September: the agents mapped the API and reached arbitrary file write in about 11 minutes, then in a later session planted a Python startup file (sitecustomize.py) and triggered a training job that started a new Python process as root, giving a root reverse shell. Amazon found the operator's own Cairn control panel exposed on the internet without authentication — how the campaign was reconstructed; the account has not been independently confirmed. The CVE record notes exploitation in the wild starting in September 2026.

Fix status. The source fix was merged 19 October, but no packaged release existed at disclosure — anyone installing with pip install monailabel was still getting vulnerable 0.8.5. Version 0.8.6 shipped 22 November, the first packaged release with the fix; it requires Python 3.11 or later and drops 3.9 and 3.10. Run it unprivileged. Any instance reachable from an untrusted network before 22 November should be treated as compromised and checked for unexpected files before it returns to service — upgrading does not evict an attacker already inside.

Cross-cutting notes for the week. Three patterns repeat: (a) old flaws become KEV items when exploitation is confirmed — Synology's CVE is from 2025, SAP's from 2025, and Roller's September fix turned out to be incomplete; (b) the fix is often per-product, not per-flaw — Axis has no vendor fix at all, and Java4Shell, ruby2shell and Ghostscript all depend on each bundling vendor shipping its own build; (c) several items are reachable only through a third party — vCenter, ScreenConnect and Axis through MSPs and vendors, LiteLLM through internal AI gateways — so the inventory must cover what others run for you, not just what you run yourself.

4. Vishing at scale — Unit 42 "Dialing for Data": the Com's voice-first extortion waves

Unit 42's "Dialing for Data" report shows the industrialised version of the §3 arrests: Com-affiliated actors use voice calls to reach an adversary-in-the-middle proxy, capture password and MFA approval in real time, enrol their own MFA device and exfiltrate cloud data — no malware needed until the endpoint stage.

What happened. 1. AI as a target

The AI supply chain itself is now the asset attackers steal — and the controls around it do not hold.

1.1 Dragos canary research — prompt theft from inside AI inference providers; OpenRouter removes three providers and 125 Zero Data Retention endpoints

Attackers were already inside at least three AI inference providers stealing customer prompts and credentials, and a Zero Data Retention flag did not stop them — treat any secret sent in a prompt to an affected provider as exposed.

What happened. Dragos published five months of canary-token research on 19 November (HD Moore, Chief Research Officer): about two million prompts carrying unique URLs and canary credentials (AWS keys, DNS names, git and SSH targets) went to widely used AI services between July and November 2026. Attackers were stealing data inside at least three AI inference providers — earliest breach mid-July, none knew until Dragos told them; marketplace resellers could view and retain customer prompts via standard logging, no exploit needed; providers appeared not to honour privacy settings, including Zero Data Retention (ZDR). In the largest breach, a couple of hundred planted AWS keys were stolen in short windows in late October — every request in those windows, including ones rejected before a model ran. The thief tested keys within hours, listed S3 buckets, searched Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, tried creating Bedrock API keys and adding IAM users, driving tens of thousands of AWS API calls. On 1 November the same attacker used ARTEX, the LLM-driven penetration-testing framework, "only to confirm that keys it already held were still live" — its user agents artex-agent and artex-judge-r121 appear in CloudTrail and, Dragos says, in no public indicator list. On 22 November OpenRouter off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from ZDR eligibility (some as a precaution) and notified all potentially affected customers. Phala (preliminary report, 10 November): an attacker took control of an inference server; DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 requests via OpenRouter 21 October 00:00 UTC to 8 November 05:35 UTC should be treated as exposed, all secrets rotated; contained 8 November. Together AI (21 November): prompt samples in a private Hugging Face repository for testing were downloaded by an unauthorised party after an AI coding agent uploaded the repository credential to a public repo; five tested credentials were Dragos canaries. Novita (22 November): some MiniMax requests from 28 May to 11 November were forwarded to MiniMax's official API instead of Novita-hosted infrastructure.

Evidence (source's own wording). Dragos: "We found attackers actively stealing data from three different hosting providers. Although our prompts were bait, they were processed along with real customer requests, and those were almost certainly copied too." On ZDR: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside."

BFSI relevance. A Zero Data Retention flag binds only the first hop: rotate any secret ever sent in a prompt to an affected provider, inventory the providers and routers handling prompts, verify privacy settings change routing, prefer first-party or self-hosted inference for sensitive workloads, and seed canary credentials as tripwires — Dragos' own recommendation.

Status caveat. Whether the three providers Dragos caught are the same three OpenRouter removed is unconfirmed — neither side has named them; do not present them as identical. Dragos does not name the three compromised providers or the Chinese lab whose systems fetched secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models, including roughly two-thirds of requests marked no-collection or ZDR.

Sources: Dragos — AI Supply Chain Security: What Canary Tokens Found (19 Nov) · OpenRouter — Data Processing Incidents on Inference Providers (22 Nov)

1.2 Operation ArgonHunt — an automated pipeline harvesting and validating AI API keys at scale

One operator stockpiled 44,096 stolen AI API keys, rechecking them until 2,686 were confirmed working — AI provider keys are now a harvested, validated commodity.

What happened. SOCRadar reported (first reported 17 November, re-reported 20 November) that threat hunting found a Linux staging server serving its /tmp directory over HTTP port 8099, exposing about 700 MB / 1,600+ files of attack scripts, logs and stolen data. The same operator ran two operations from it: a deep compromise of an unnamed Türkiye-based cryptocurrency exchange (read/write access to the production database, forged withdrawal requests), and an automated pipeline holding 44,096 unique AI API keys across Gemini (26,633), OpenAI (14,504), OpenRouter, Anthropic and xAI, of which the operator's own recheck logs marked 2,686 as active — plus 519 Google Cloud tokens and sniffed live service-account tokens. OpenRouter credentials showed a 41% survival rate on recheck, against 6% for Gemini and under 2% for OpenAI. The exchange has not publicly confirmed the breach, and SOCRadar did not verify on-chain settlement of the forged withdrawal requests, so those figures are attempted theft, not confirmed losses. SOCRadar's analysis was passive — it "did not use any credential found in the directory and did not touch victim systems."

BFSI relevance. A leaked AI provider key is a billable, attributable asset: rotate any key that may have transited a shared or third-party service, review AI API billing for anomalies, and prefer project-scoped keys with spend limits over administrative keys.

1.3 Microsoft agent-framework PyPI hijack — a Cairn-labelled credential stealer in the AI-agent supply chain

For about an hour, a routine pip install of Microsoft's official AI-agent SDK pulled a credential stealer published through Microsoft's own release pipeline — package name, publisher and earlier releases all looked correct.

What happened. At 20:35 UTC on 17 November, a malicious version (1.26.1) of Microsoft's official agent-framework Python package was uploaded to PyPI; a second version, 1.26.2, followed at 21:08 UTC. Both were unavailable by about 21:30 UTC. Wiz assesses that a client certificate and private key for Microsoft's Engineering System Release Pipeline (ESRP) were compromised to publish them. The stealer drops a .pth file into the site-packages root, so it runs whenever Python starts in that environment, even if agent-framework is never imported. It harvested environment variables and credential files — including LLM API keys and Hugging Face tokens — from AI-agent development environments, and requested fresh identity tokens from GitHub Actions, Azure DevOps and Azure. The tooling identified itself as "Cairn", an open-source AI penetration-testing framework: it staged the package at /tmp/cairn_pkg and sent a CAIRN_ESRP_PROBE2 connectivity probe. Wiz could read only the 50 requests stored on the attacker's free-tier webhook — three victim exfiltrations were observed in that sample, not a verified victim total. The most recent safe version is 1.26.0. Microsoft has still published no security advisory.

BFSI relevance. Anyone who installed 1.26.1 or 1.26.2 should rebuild from 1.26.0 and rotate every secret the environment could reach, including LLM API keys. The Cairn self-identification is the tooling's own label, not proof of the operator's identity.

Sources: Wiz — Microsoft agent-framework Compromised: Credential Stealer Targets Azure and CI Pipelines (17–18 Nov)

1.4 LiteLLM CVE-2026-40217 — the LLM gateway in CISA KEV; attribution contested

The LLM gateway that concentrates a firm's provider API keys behind one endpoint is in CISA KEV — but who exploited it, and by which flaw, is contested, not settled.

What happened. LiteLLM — the open-source LLM gateway/proxy holding model providers' API keys — had CVE-2026-40217 added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 November (federal remediation due 2 December). Google Threat Intelligence Group attributes an LLMjacking intrusion — attackers who bought ordinary LiteLLM virtual keys escalating them to proxy-admin credentials — to this CVE in its 2 November report and 16 November AI Threat Tracker. Corgea disputes the attribution, saying the key-escalation technique is a separate bug fixed in 1.84.0. Separately, SANS Internet Storm Center reported scanning of LiteLLM gateways on 20 November (target endpoint /mcp-rest/test/tools/list, a fake bearer token with a body asking the server to run id via the stdio transport). The flaw itself is CVSS 4.0 5.9.

2. AI as a weapon or accelerator

The weapon cases this week are no longer proofs of concept: autonomous agents found and exploited a zero-day in the wild, ARTEX produced named victims, and AI voice calls became a confirmed ransomware entry point.

2.1 Cairn / MONAI Label — autonomous AI agents find and exploit a zero-day in the wild, reaching root

AI agents given one instruction — "obtain shell access" — found a previously unknown flaw and reached root in minutes: the first publicly documented case of offensive AI agents finding a novel vulnerability during a live intrusion and turning it into a working exploit.

What happened. On 20 November, Amazon Threat Intelligence (published by Amazon CISO CJ Moses on the AWS Security Blog) reported that a threat actor used autonomous AI agents running the open-source Cairn framework to find a previously unknown flaw in MONAI Label, an open-source medical-imaging annotation server, and gain root access on a university server. Amazon found the operator's own Cairn control panel exposed on the internet without authentication on 10 September, which revealed task settings, prompts and run history. The campaign ran about 48 hours from 8 September against university IP addresses in East Asia outside mainland China. The agents planned, executed and adjusted their approach themselves: against a MONAI Label server they mapped the API, found an upload endpoint with a filename parameter, and went from mapping the API to arbitrary file write in about 11 minutes; in a later session they planted a Python reverse shell in sitecustomize.py and triggered a training job to spawn a Python process as root. The flaw is a path traversal in PUT /datastore/image and PUT /datastore/label, affecting MONAI Label 0.8.5 and earlier, which runs with authentication off by default. It is now CVE-2026-133968 (9.8); the fix was merged into the source tree on 19 October and shipped in MONAI Label 0.8.6 on 22 November, the first packaged release containing it. Amazon assesses with high confidence it was a zero-day and with moderate confidence the actor is China-nexus (Simplified Chinese prompts, China Standard Time script timestamps, targeting of universities likely of strategic interest to China); no group is named. No patient scans or clinical systems were touched.

Evidence (source's own wording, verified verbatim from the AWS Security Blog). "The actor gave the agents a simple goal: 'obtain shell access.' During the campaign, the agents found a zero-day in an open-source application and used it to gain full control of a server." On tempo: "An agent that discovers and exploits a zero-day in minutes completes its activity faster than many security operations centers can manually triage an alert. The agents that failed against the hardened Redis target ran continuously for 16 hours without breaks, sleep, or working-hour constraints."

What the claim rests on, and its limits. The "first confirmed case" reading is Amazon's own reconstruction from the exposed Cairn panel — Amazon states it is its own investigation and it has not been independently confirmed. The zero-day assessment is high-confidence, the China-nexus attribution moderate-confidence, and the second university network the actor tunnelled into is unconfirmed as compromised ("we infer the actor had used the zero-day to compromise it"). The agents also failed elsewhere in the same campaign: a 16-hour, 148-action attempt against an open Redis instance, a 6-hour GPU-server credential spray of 30,000+ combinations, Log4Shell injection and SSRF attempts all failed — but they persist without human direction, and against a soft target find and exploit a novel flaw in minutes. Amazon also notes Gambit Security documented a separate, financially motivated actor using Cairn against online retailers in September — Cairn is being adopted in both espionage and criminal operations.

BFSI relevance. No financial target was hit and no bank system is implicated — but any AI annotation, agent or automation framework a bank or vendor runs must never be internet-reachable with authentication off; MONAI Label servers should be upgraded to 0.8.6 or isolated.

Sources: AWS Security Blog — Chinese-nexus actor's Cairn AI agents found a zero-day on their own (20 Nov) · Daily briefing 23 Nov 2026 — watchlist row, CVE-2026-133968

2.2 ARTEX — named victims this week: Barracuda and Bitrise

ARTEX produced named victims: a single stolen credential became a multi-system, machine-speed intrusion in both cases, through entry points as ordinary as an unretired vendor key and a dormant bot account with no MFA.

What happened.

  • Barracuda (disclosed 11 November). An attacker entered Barracuda's cloud environment using a cloud access key Barracuda had issued to an outside AI vendor for a past project and never retired; Barracuda believes the attacker stole the key from the vendor's GitHub repository in October. Between 1 and 2 November the attacker moved through about ten cloud accounts in 44 hours, chaining one secret to the next — stored secrets, a source-code access token, build-system secrets, Kubernetes clusters — hunting credentials and cryptocurrency (wallet, mnemonic and seed-phrase material). Barracuda matched the activity to ARTEX from probe names, the order of operations and built-in credential-hunting routines, and called it a "machine-speed intrusion". It was discovered on 4 November; an attempt to create a new administrative cloud identity was blocked. The FBI's FLASH of 13 November (pre-window background) warned ARTEX had been in malicious use since at least August 2026, with some intrusions reaching privileged access in under three hours.
  • Bitrise (incident report published 19 November). Over 25 days (25 October–18 November) an operator pointed ARTEX at the mobile CI/CD platform, at first posing as an "authorised pentest" while mapping the public surface, then returning as an ordinary customer to run its own builds and test cross-customer access. On 13 November at 11:46:48 UTC the attacker signed in to a dormant internal test bot account with no two-factor authentication, using a valid username and password; less than eleven minutes later the agents were using cloud keys from that workspace's projects, and over the next ten hours chained one credential into the next — signing files, source code, infrastructure state and build logs. Bitrise detected the activity at 16:32 UTC and switched the whole platform off at 20:34 UTC. Files in 15 customer workspaces were accessed without authorisation (one code-signing certificate file, one provisioning profile, 13 diagnostic build logs from May 2026, one holding a credential and another an email address); all 15 customers were contacted. Bitrise revoked all 919 cloud service-account keys and every GitHub OAuth app token, wiped and reinstalled thousands of build machines, and when the attacker returned on 18 November and replayed the attack, every attempt was refused. Bitrise identifies ARTEX by three fingerprints matching the framework's public source code, and notes it could not establish which language model drove the agents or who the operator is.

BFSI relevance. The pattern is directly transferable to banks: a vendor key that outlives its project, or a dormant service account without MFA, plus an agentic framework that chains credentials on its own. Retire vendor credentials when the work ends, enforce MFA on every service and bot account, and hunt for the ARTEX user agents (artex-agent, artex-judge-r121, ARTEX-Nuclei-Fingerprint/1.0, ARTEX-CredHunter/2.0) and the X-ARTX-KEY request header.

Sources: Barracuda — Forty-four hours: Anatomy of a machine-speed intrusion (11 Nov) · Bitrise — Anatomy of an AI-driven attack: What ARTEX looks like in action (19 Nov)

2.3 European Commission — an AI-generated cyber campaign confirmed; the ARTEX link is not

2.4 Ukraine / FBI arrests — AI-voice vishing confirmed as a ransomware entry point

AI-generated voice calls impersonating IT support are now a confirmed ransomware entry point — at least 28 companies paid more than $40.5 million in ransoms.

What happened. Ukrainian police, working with the FBI, arrested four suspected key members of a ransomware group on 18–19 November after a two-year investigation. The group won initial access with AI-generated voice calls impersonating IT support: it built its own platform with AI-generated voice messages to automate fraudulent calls, tricking employees into disclosing credentials or installing remote-access software (TeamViewer / AnyDesk). In one incident an attacker kept an employee on the phone while another accessed the company's systems. At least 28 companies paid more than $40.5 million in ransoms. Sectors spanned oil and gas, pharmaceutical, manufacturing, logistics, financial and critical infrastructure. Named victims in court records include Warren Oil Company (August 2024, 1.4 TB copied, $5 million paid after a $10 million demand), Newpark Resources (December 2024, $1.5 million) and Quantum Health (June 2026, $5 million after a $15 million demand). Police carried out 66 simultaneous searches and seized computers, phones, bank cards, about $370,000 in cash and €20,000. The four face up to 15 years. No group name was published, and no MITRE mapping was published.

BFSI relevance. Any firm whose IT support resets credentials or installs remote-access software on the strength of a phone call is exposed — especially financial firms, where a single help-desk interaction can hand over VPN or remote-access credentials — and SMS-based MFA is defeated by the same path. Unit 42's separate "Dialing for Data" vishing waves are not labelled an AI campaign and are not re-labelled here (see §4).

2.5 n0n — an AI-assisted ransomware operation: opencode agent, $7,216+ in LLM spend

2.6 NBFC loan-diversion fraud, India — "traces of AI" in a disbursement-control failure

Delhi Police reported that 12 non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) in Delhi-NCR lost over ₹65 crore in three months to loan-disbursement fraud, with more than 150 NBFC complaints nationwide totalling over ₹300 crore; CyberStrikeAI was used in at least one October case. 2.7 ruby2shell — an AI agent does lab-only exploit development on a critical flaw within days

Within days of disclosure, an autonomous AI agent took a critical Ruby flaw from crash reproduction to working remote command execution in the lab — the patch-to-exploit window is compressing.

What happened. Ruby disclosed CVE-2026-132825 ("ruby2shell") on 19 November: a one-byte heap overflow in string case conversion, rated Critical by Ruby, reachable without authentication in default Ruby on Rails and classic-style Sinatra applications. On 20 November a repository (genaforvena, verified genuine by the daily briefing's code read) showed an autonomous AI agent (OpenCode, about €500 credit budget) taking the flaw from crash reproduction to tcache poisoning in a single session, then to what the repo grades as rung 5 (working remote command execution) under lab conditions — one multipart POST driving a forged CRuby T_DATA finalizer, with an independent clean-archive replay. The same repo grades PHP2Shell at rung 4 (demonstrated impact, no RCE).

Evidence. The repo itself states the limits plainly: it needs custom grooming/GC middleware plus heap oracles, ASLR stays on, and "stock-app oracle-free RCE/persistence are not claimed". No complete stock-Rails RCE chain is published; Veria Labs' own PoC is still due about 25 November.

BFSI relevance. That is not evidence of in-the-wild exploitation (none reported) — it is a reason to patch Ruby to 3.3.14, 3.4.13 or 4.0.10 on the disclosure timeline, not the PoC timeline.

2.8 Netskope — the maintained ARTEX successor ships a PHP2Shell exploit skill

3. AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

Regulators are catching up to agents — while Visa's data shows AI accelerating ordinary fraud, not inventing new kinds.

3.1 MAS — do not give AI agents your bank login; a "know your agent" protocol is coming

MAS's chief told Singapore FinTech Festival audiences not to give personal AI agents financial-account logins — a bank cannot tell whether an agent has the customer's full authority, or who is responsible if it oversteps.

What happened. On 19 November, Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) Managing Director Chia Der Jiun told the Singapore FinTech Festival that "some users are giving agents their login credentials to financial accounts" and: "I would not advise giving personal agents such access, as there is insufficient assurance of safety." His concern is that a bank cannot tell whether an agent logging in as a customer has the customer's full authority, or whether it is staying within its mandate — and who is responsible if it transacts incorrectly or beyond its instructions. MAS will facilitate a common "know your agent" protocol so banks can verify an agent's identity, whom it represents, what authority and mandate it holds, and its origins and reliability, as the next phase of its Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime (SAFR) initiative. MAS will also set up an Agentic Money Sandbox with the Future of Finance Institute, covering corporate treasury management and payment services with tokenised deposits. Agents could move money and allocate assets more quickly, Chia warned, raising liquidity and financial-stability risks — financial institutions may need to impose limits on agent-directed transactions. No timeline was given for the protocol and no start date for the sandbox. Context: the CSA / GovTech / IMDA joint advisory AD-2026-015 (6 November, pre-window standing guidance) already warns against giving personal AI agents one-time passwords, and AXS said on 18 November it is opening its network of 800+ Singapore billers to AI agents.

3.2 UK — Bank of England / FCA / PRA consult on cyber resilience, with AI and quantum effective practices

What happened. On 19 November the Prudential Regulation Authority, the Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority issued a joint consultation (PRA CP17/26, FCA GC26/9) on cyber risk and resilience management — principles-based, outcome-focused expectations for how firms manage cyber risk and resilience, aligned with the NCSC's Cyber Assessment Framework. 3.5 Visa Fall 2026 Biannual Threats Report — AI is accelerating traditional fraud, not creating new categories

What happened. Visa's Fall 2026 Biannual Threats Report (released 17 November, covering January–June 2026) finds that AI is not creating entirely new categories of fraud.
