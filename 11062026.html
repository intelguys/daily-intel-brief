Executive summary

1

Two SMFG disclosures in one afternoon — and both came through a third party, not the bank's own front door. SMBC Consumer Finance said on 5 November that unauthorised access to its card-loan guarantee system exposed data for up to 320,118 people — names, birth dates, addresses, phone numbers, email, driver’s licence numbers, employer and income details and loan guarantee amounts — covering customers of 189 partner banks, credit unions and online banks. Separately, Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation said 227,216 email addresses leaked through its contractor Arara’s email-delivery service. No account numbers, balances or PINs were in the email leak, and no misuse of either leak has been confirmed so far. The two incidents are separate systems and separate contractors; do not add the figures together. The week’s pattern holds: SinoPac and Arkéa were also reached through a supplier or provider system.

2

CERT-In has named the AI-assisted campaign hitting Indian payment APIs — and the same tooling is now documented from three angles in one day. CERT-In’s advisory CIAD-2026-0058 (Critical, 4 November, widely reported 5 November) calls the actor “Crafty Falcon”: it uses publicly available AI offensive tools — ARTEX, PentAGI, Cairn, CyberStrikeAI — to run full attack chains at machine speed and invoke victims’ own payment and disbursement APIs for unauthorised transfers to mule accounts, after ₹1-scale micro-test transactions and deliberate re-targeting of firms that remediated incompletely. Microsoft (5 November) observed ARTEX agents hunting exposed secrets on GitHub and reaching an Azure Key Vault in five minutes; Check Point (5 November) found 1,608 live ARTEX instances and campaigns running near-autonomously; and a backdoored copy of ARTEX is circulating on Docker Hub, with 62 exposed consoles serving it. No victim names or loss total have been published for Crafty Falcon, and the operator’s identity is unknown.

3

WordPress falls due today with a public proof-of-concept and a quarter of a million blocked attempts — but no confirmed victim yet. CVE-2026-119977 (WordPress core upgrade-window race, CVSS 8.1) reaches its CISA federal deadline today, 6 November. A working public PoC was published on 4 November and Wordfence has blocked 240,000+ attempts, yet no site takeover tied to this flaw has been publicly confirmed — blocked attempts are not compromises, and the distinction matters. WSO2 CVE-2025-11969, due yesterday, has no closure confirmation in-window and should now be treated as overdue. New in the window is a critical OpenSSH password bypass on musl Linux (CVE-2026-123158, CVSS 8.1) affecting Alpine-based container and cloud images: no exploitation reported, but working reproduction labs already exist, so patch rather than wait.

Supply chain & third-party risk

SMBC Consumer Finance partner banks — NEW

The breach is not one firm’s problem: the guarantee system served 189 financial institutions, and by the morning of 6 November at least 14 partner banks had disclosed impact. Docomo SMTB Net Bank (formerly Sumishin SBI Net Bank) says up to 122,635 of its customers may be affected and its own systems were not breached; Dai-shi Hokuetsu Bank up to 1,307; Aichi Bank 1 person; Hokuyo Bank, Tsukuba Bank and Chugoku Bank suspended new card-loan applications or limit increases while they investigate. All these figures are subsets of the 320,118 — do not add them to it. The control question for any bank that outsources loan guarantees: which of your customers’ data sits in a guarantor’s system, and how would you know it had leaked?

SMBC / Arara email-delivery breach — NEW (separate incident)

Arara’s wider incident, reported in its 2 November second report, is larger than the SMBC slice: about 77 million email addresses possibly taken from 97 servers in individual contractor environments (up to ~380 companies), 28–31 October, plus about 7.13 million phishing emails sent impersonating a card company from news@arara.com, 28–30 October. Arara says the 77 million figure counts addresses, not people, and categories may overlap, so figures must not be summed. An NTT East subsidiary was also hit (non-BFSI). Peer banks using third-party email-delivery services should check their own suppliers’ exposure and warn customers that leaked addresses will be used for phishing.

DigiCert — NEW, 5 Nov

DigiCert warned of a breach after a February 2025 server migration mistakenly exposed an internal business system to the internet (previously VPN-only). Self-registration allowed external account creation, and an unauthorised account accessed archived customer data read-only from 27 June to 2 July 2026: names, business email, phone and address, and where provided an ID document (driver’s licence, passport or utility bill). Discovered in June 2026, initially misclassified, confirmed 19 October. DigiCert says CA services, CertCentral and DigiCert ONE were not compromised and credentials and issued certificates remain secure; the number of people affected was not stated. BFSI relevance is trust infrastructure: a certificate authority’s customer ID documents are directly usable for KYC fraud and impersonation.

CWPdoor trojanised web server — NEW (watch)

XLab (QiAnXin) disclosed CWPdoor, a passive Linux backdoor in a trojanised cwpsrv (Control Web Panel web server), discovered 29 June via a fake updater script replacing cwpsrv 1.15.10 / 1.24.0. It stays dormant until a crafted HTTP request with an encrypted X-Ngx-Integrity-Data header, timestamp and RSA signature. More than 340,000 CWP instances were exposed on the public web in the past month — that is exposure, not confirmed infections. No actor is identified, initial access is unknown, and no confirmed victim organisation or data theft has been established. Include only as a watch item: relevant if a financial firm or its hoster runs Control Web Panel.

Emerging fraud & attack vectors

Mirax device-takeover RAT — NEW

Group-IB’s Mirax campaign (see Threat actor activity) is a retail-banking fraud vector, not just a malware story: full device takeover via Accessibility Service lets the operator act inside the victim’s own banking app, and confirmed losses are already in the millions of dollars, with unauthorised international transfers routed to suspected mule accounts abroad. The campaign spiked over the Eid holidays and at the end of July — holiday timing is a pattern, not a coincidence, and it matches CERT-In’s warning that Crafty Falcon times attacks for low-monitoring windows. Customer advice should focus on sideloaded apps impersonating “Google Play Services”, not on passwords alone.

Phishing volumes surged in Q3 — NEW statistics

Microsoft’s email threat landscape report for Q3 2026 (5 November) detected about 7.3 billion email-based phishing threats in the quarter, including nearly 3 billion in September alone, up 41% from August. About 129 million malicious messages spoofed tracked business-service sender domains — DocuSign the most spoofed. Credential theft remained the dominant objective. Two campaigns are directly relevant to finance workflows: an August device-code phishing campaign against legal organisations (personalised salary-review documents and QR codes, 10,000+ users across 4,800+ organisations, almost all in the US) and September purchase-order / payroll lures delivering Agent Tesla via disguised archives. QR-code phishing fell 67% against Q2, while CAPTCHA-gated phishing recovered, partly via SVG attachments.

Japan’s police to share fraud-account data nationwide — NEW (defensive)

Japan’s National Police Agency announced on 5 November that its information-sharing scheme against special fraud (tokushu-sagi) moves to nationwide unified operation from 9 November 2026. The NPA will collect information on fraud-used accounts from all 47 prefectural police forces and provide it centrally to 30 newly contracted financial institutions — including Mizuho, MUFG, SMBC, Resona, PayPay Bank, Rakuten Bank, Sony Bank, Docomo SMTB Net Bank, au Jibun Bank, Japan Post Bank, Norinchukin and SMBC Trust Bank. Banks will check whether their own customers transferred money to those accounts and pass the information to police so police can contact potential victims. The NPA’s provisional statistics: January–September 2026 special-fraud cases 33,329, losses ¥261.04 billion. This is a regulator / fraud-prevention measure, not a breach — but it is a direct response to the same fraud ecosystem behind the SMFG disclosures.

Restoration, refund and loan-guarantee phishing — ongoing

Arkéa, Binance and Mercado Bitcoin stories remain live phishing and laundering bait, and the SMBC Consumer Finance breach adds a new one: affected customers are to be contacted individually by email or SMS, and SMBC and its partners warn not to click links in such messages and to verify via official sites. Any message citing loan-guarantee details, an email address from the Arara leak, or a withdrawal-restoration offer should be treated as suspect until verified through a known channel. Verify via official status pages only, never via links in messages claiming to restore withdrawals, refunds or loan access.

AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI.

AI as a target — attacker tooling itself is compromised; agents hunt exposed keys

A copycat ARTEX Docker image (mianhuatang11/artex, about 9,000 downloads) hides a verifyCaptcha() routine that runs attacker input via sh -c as root on unauthenticated login requests; as many as 62 internet-exposed ARTEX consoles served the backdoored build between 2–4 November, and 1,645 hosts in total were serving the ARTEX console as of 4 November. A compromised ARTEX instance holds its operator’s targets, live credentials, captured sessions and network position — so the backdoor turns attacker tooling into a target. Separately, Microsoft observed agentic AI frameworks hunting exposed secrets on GitHub since August 2026 — credentials, OAuth application secrets and AI-inference API keys — and in one case an agent went from searching GitHub for OAuth client secrets to exfiltrating data from an Azure Key Vault in five minutes. No new LLMjacking victim or incident was published on 5 November; the Google / Mandiant LLMJacking research is dated 2–3 November and is carried as standing risk, not a new incident.

AI as a weapon / accelerator — Crafty Falcon and near-autonomous ARTEX campaigns

CERT-In’s Crafty Falcon advisory is the strongest new AI item in this window: publicly available AI offensive tools (ARTEX, PentAGI, Cairn, CyberStrikeAI) are used to execute complete attack chains autonomously against Indian BFSI payment and disbursement APIs at machine speed. Check Point’s Inside ARTEX research (5 November) shows how far that autonomy goes: 1,608 live, publicly accessible ARTEX instances; one crypto campaign ran 292 objectives producing 230 findings and 400,791 agent messages with no further operator prompting; agents independently found RCE, exposed databases and cloud credentials, and executed a supply-chain compromise. But the same research is a useful corrective: across that crypto campaign the attacker made $0, because agents mistook successful API responses for successful theft — autonomy is real, but it is not competence. The 3AM “hybrid AI” ransomware case (Bitdefender, 3 November) is context only: a UK school estate, AI involvement circumstantial and not confirmed, and no BFSI victim — do not present it as a BFSI AI campaign.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — first examiner framework, and agents abused inside the endpoint

The US Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued Bulletin 2026-54 on 4 November (reported 5 November), the first examiner framework for banks’ generative and agentic AI use: 16 components across 6 pillars, with consequence-based tiering — autonomous payment agents, real-time credit decisioning, fraud-hold release and market access are Tier 1 Critical. Read it honestly: it does not set new supervisory expectations or enforceable standards, it is not for bank self-assessment, and human-in-the-loop must be “more than a nominal click”. Elastic’s threat report (4 November, reported 5 November) found attackers executing commands through Claude Code on Windows and ChatGPT desktop on macOS in confirmed intrusions, and stolen credentials used on average 8 minutes after infostealer infection — inventory approved AI tools and judge by behaviour, not by approval status. Google Cloud (5 November) added isolation and governance controls to its CodeMender AI vulnerability-fixing tool and Agent Compute Engine. OpenAI’s agentic pipeline (4 November, reported 5 November) generated 948 Linux kernel privilege-escalation exploits as defensive research — not an attack; the patches are candidates for maintainer review, not merged fixes. Standing controls still apply: key inventory and rotation, endpoint exposure (no unauthenticated Ollama / LiteLLM on the internet), spend and invocation monitoring with revoke-and-rotate, and agent-tool detection.

No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake item in this window

No newly reported AI-generated phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a BFSI victim was found for 5–6 November. That is absence of reporting, not evidence it has stopped. The Crafty Falcon and ARTEX items in this edition are agentic-intrusion and API-abuse activity, not phishing or deepfake campaigns, and they are not re-labelled as such. The SinoPac, Arkéa and SMFG cases are not re-labelled as AI campaigns — none attributes them to AI tooling.
