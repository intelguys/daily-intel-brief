Executive summary

1

A quiet Saturday for new vendor research — but the patching queue is full, and two critical flaws the earlier editions missed need attention now. No new CISA KEV additions on 21 November and no new Tier 1 research in the window. The four KEV items added on 20 November — Apache Roller, VMware vCenter, Cisco Expressway and SAP NetWeaver — fall due for US federal remediation tomorrow, Monday 23 November. New on vCenter: aggregators now list six public GitHub proof-of-concept repositories, none verified and at least one reported fake — do not run them. Catch-ups: ruby2shell (CVE-2026-132825), a critical pre-authentication remote code execution flaw in Ruby disclosed on 19 November and reachable in default Rails applications, and TYPO3 CVE-2026-125160, which the vendor says is actively exploited.

2

The FBI went public with its offensive cyber campaign as the JPMorgan vendor breach stayed in the headlines — and the n0n picture got clearer, not bigger. Director Kash Patel posted on 20 November (US time) that the FBI is “going on offence” under Operation Winter SHIELD, a 60-day campaign with more than 600 engagements across 14 critical-infrastructure sectors; the companion Operation Riptide produced more than 200 arrests and over $23 million seized or frozen, and the FBI has begun returning $31 million in virtual assets stolen by North Korean actors. On n0n, Sophos Counter Threat Unit’s Rafe Pilling cautions that the group’s claims overstate direct enterprise compromise: the data appears to come from third-party providers running marketing, loyalty or concierge programmes, not from the brands’ own networks. The Qubole link remains unconfirmed, and the Ekwiik deadline falls later today. Pixelleaks claimed two unnamed financial-services firms on 21 November; neither claim is verified.

3

AI moved further into attackers’ operating routines — in OT intrusions and in live incident-response caseloads — while no new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a named bank appeared. Recorded Future reports that Iran-linked GreenJuliett has built AI into its operational-technology attack chain: a LiteLLM gateway and Persian-language chat apps on likely group infrastructure, with servers in its anonymisation network talking to infrastructure hosting Anthropic’s Claude. Arctic Wolf reports agentic penetration-testing frameworks (ARTEX, AntiFox) operating from public cloud in live intrusions, and malware that calls LLM APIs during execution. Neither report ties AI use to a bank. The control problem is concrete: exposed AI gateways concentrate provider keys, and agent frameworks keep appearing in attackers’ hands.

AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — kept separate, with forecasts marked as forecasts.

AI as a target

GreenJuliett runs its own AI gateway (NEW, confirmed — Recorded Future, 20 Nov, catch-up). The group’s LiteLLM gateway and Persian-language chat apps sat on infrastructure assessed as likely group-linked, and servers in its anonymisation network communicated with infrastructure hosting Anthropic’s Claude. The exposure class is the same one defenders worry about — an AI gateway concentrating provider keys behind a single interface — now operated by the attacker. No new exposed-LLM-endpoint or LLM/API-key theft incident was found in-window. The open item stays LiteLLM CVE-2026-40217 (KEV 18 Nov, due 2 December): update to 1.83.11+, lock the gateway off the internet, rotate LLM API keys. The vCenter and ruby2shell proof-of-concept circulation raises the cost of leaving AI gateways and agent frameworks exposed.

AI as a weapon or accelerator

GreenJuliett (NEW, confirmed — Recorded Future, 20 Nov, catch-up). AI is now part of the group’s OT attack chain: it shortens the time to identify vulnerable device types and write targeting scripts, and has moved from individual accounts into the group’s operating environment. Arctic Wolf (NEW, confirmed — 20 Nov, catch-up). Agentic penetration-testing frameworks such as ARTEX and AntiFox are operating from public cloud infrastructure in live intrusions; Alibaba Cloud-hosted AntiFox interfaces were linked to confirmed malicious activity against internet-facing web services and associated AWS environments. Some malware now contacts an LLM during execution to retrieve instructions on demand, generate filenames, customise payloads and produce social-engineering messages. Arctic Wolf’s October caseload rose sharply — 78% more non-customer cases than the monthly average, network intrusions up 96%, ransomware cases up 77% — but those figures are its own caseload, not industry-wide rates, and do not establish that AI caused the increases. No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a named bank was found in-window. Unit 42’s Com vishing campaign is conventional vishing and is not labelled an AI campaign by Unit 42 — do not re-label it.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI

The FBI’s Winter SHIELD warning and the return of $31 million in North Korean-stolen crypto show law enforcement treating AI-enabled crime as a priority, but no new MAS, UK or EU governance development appeared in-window. The control problem this edition sharpens is concrete: exposed AI gateways concentrate provider API keys (LiteLLM scanning, 20 Nov, carried); agent frameworks can be left publicly accessible without authentication (Cairn lesson, carried); and “agentic” labels from vendors are not evidence — ask what the agent did, with what tools, and what it could reach (Bell Canada, carried). Arctic Wolf’s advice for the same problem is structural: reset response timelines for machine-speed attacks, map everything attackers can see, strengthen identity controls (including service accounts and API keys), monitor behaviour at runtime, and practise with AI-driven scenarios.
