Executive summary

1

The Korean wave reaches a major brokerage — and a Malaysian bank’s website is defaced the same night. Mirae Asset Securities said on 7 November that unauthorised access on 4–5 November exposed about 3,800 customer account numbers and personal data of 35,255 current and former employees and contractors; no account-holder names leaked with the account numbers, and no financial loss has been confirmed. Hours earlier, Bank Rakyat’s corporate website was briefly defaced by an actor calling itself “Marlanwg”, claiming internal access and a data sample — the defacement is confirmed, the internal-access claim is not, and the bank has only confirmed an outage. The Mirae Asset disclosure follows the regulator’s 5 November count of 40 incidents at 37 Korean financial firms since 30 September.

2

Two edge flaws are now confirmed exploited — and one of them is new to CISA’s exploited list. SonicWall’s SonicOS SSLVPN SAML / MFA bypass (CVE-2026-19868, CVSS 7.5) was added to CISA KEV on 6 November with a 20 November federal deadline; it needs valid alternate-method credentials, has no workaround and no public PoC. Separately, OpenSSH’s musl password bypass (CVE-2026-123158) went from “PoC labs exist” to confirmed exploitation within a day: Previdian saw the first attempt under nine hours after disclosure and confirmed root access on an Alpine honeypot — eight attempts, all from one IP. Neither flaw is unauthenticated remote code execution, and the distinction matters for triage.

3

Chainalysis starts bringing its screening tools back while two Latin American fintechs pause crypto movements. Chainalysis began phased restoration of KYT and address screening on 6 November, with password resets, API-key rotation and IP allowlisting required; Reactor and Data Solutions remain offline. The same day, Nubank and Mercado Pago paused crypto deposits and withdrawals, blaming an external verification provider — neither named the provider, and the link to Chainalysis is an inference, not a confirmed cause. Binance’s Recovery Update #9 reopened more withdrawals, and Binance announced on 6 November that the zero-knowledge proof for its 44-token proof-of-reserves snapshot has been generated — though the public proof-of-reserves page still showed blank verification fields late on 6 November, so live user verification was not yet confirmed.

Supply chain & third-party risk

Chainalysis — phased restoration begins, 6 Nov

Chainalysis began restoring access to its Know Your Transaction (KYT) monitoring service and Address Screening products in phases on 6 November (company update 03:30 EST / 16:30 SGT). Customers must first submit trusted IP addresses for an allowlist, then reset passwords and rotate API keys before registering transactions again. Reactor (investigations) and Data Solutions (threat intelligence) remain offline while external security experts harden the systems; no restoration date has been given. This follows the 4 November disclosure that names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all Chainalysis users were exposed to a malicious actor. The company says it has no evidence investigation data was accessed or attribution data manipulated — that is the company’s statement and still provisional — and that the incident is unrelated to the Binance theft. BFSI relevance is direct: KYT is the transaction-screening tool banks, insurers and crypto firms use for AML monitoring, and its outage is already disrupting downstream services (see Emerging fraud).

SMBC Consumer Finance partner fallout — Update

The breach keeps spreading through the partner network rather than ending at the guarantor: up to 188 partner institutions have stopped accepting new card-loan applications while the guarantee system stays suspended, with resumption aimed at mid-November. Resona Group’s 5,935 records (Resona Bank 139, Kansai Mirai 5,793, Minato Bank 3) and Ehime Bank’s still-uncounted records are all subsets of the up-to-320,118 figure — do not add them to it. The control question for any bank that outsources loan guarantees: which of your customers’ data sits in a guarantor’s system, and how would you know it had leaked?

Okinawa Bank via contractor LENDY — NEW, separate incident

A second Japanese contractor route in the same window, but a different incident: unauthorised access on 30 October to LENDY, a Tokyo contractor handling debt-collection negotiation records for Okinawa Bank’s “Chekit” personal-loan product, possibly exposed 211 records (names, email addresses, phone numbers, agent names, repayment amounts and negotiation details). No misuse has been confirmed yet. The pattern to note is not the size — 211 records is small — it is that customer data held by a small, specialised contractor is reachable and leakable, and that banks keep learning about it from the contractor’s disclosure rather than their own monitoring.

Scry Analytics — UNVERIFIED leak-site claim

The N0n extortion group claims data from US analytics and AI firm Scry Analytics, alleging it holds core banking customer and signer records of a US private-banking client plus a global insurance group’s party master of 7,904 legal-entity records. Neither Scry Analytics nor the bank has confirmed it, and the client is not named. If genuine, it is a vendor-holding-bank-data case of the kind that matters most to third-party risk teams; until verified, it is a claim only, and peer banks should check what client data sits with their own analytics and AI vendors rather than wait for confirmation.

Emerging fraud & attack vectors

Nubank and Mercado Pago pause crypto movements — NEW, 6 Nov

Nubank suspended crypto deposits and withdrawals in its app on 6 November, blaming a failure at an external transaction-verification provider; buying and selling for reais inside the app continues, and no timeline was given. Mercado Pago posted a similar notice citing an external data supplier. Neither company named the supplier, and neither has linked the outage to Chainalysis — the link is the reporting outlet’s inference (Nubank’s direct-transfer feature runs on Chainalysis screening, per Nubank’s own launch materials). Treat this as a downstream-outage report, not a confirmed Chainalysis consequence — but it is exactly the kind of disruption the Chainalysis outage was expected to cause, and other firms using the same screening provider should check their own crypto flows now rather than after a customer complaint.

Mirae Asset and Bank Rakyat phishing watch — NEW

Both of today’s named cases create immediate phishing material. Mirae Asset’s leak gives attackers 3,800 customer account numbers plus the names, email addresses and mobile numbers of 35,255 employees and contractors — enough to impersonate the brokerage to customers and to impersonate staff internally. Bank Rakyat’s defaced pages were still visible in Google search results at 03:50 SGT on 7 November, and the actor’s extortion-style message will be copied into phishing within days. Customers should be told to use the official apps and known channels only: Mirae Asset is running a temporary helpline (7 November, 10:00–17:00 SGT), and Bank Rakyat has directed customers to iRakyat, BRICK, QR2E and i-bizRAKYAT.

Restoration, refund and loan-guarantee phishing — ongoing

Arkéa, Binance and Mercado Bitcoin stories remain live phishing and laundering bait, and the SMBC Consumer Finance breach adds a new one: affected customers are to be contacted individually by email or SMS, and SMBC and its partners warn not to click links in such messages and to verify via official sites. Any message citing loan-guarantee details, an email address from the Arara leak, a withdrawal-restoration offer, or the Bank Rakyat incident should be treated as suspect until verified through a known channel. Verify via official status pages only, never via links in messages claiming to restore withdrawals, refunds or loan access.

AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI.

AI as a target — malicious agent “skills” are the new supply-chain risk, not a new incident

Sophos X-Ops (6 November) previews research for a BSides Sydney talk on 7 November: malicious AI-agent “skills” — a SKILL.md description plus helper code, shared via repositories and marketplaces — whose code does more than the description claims, for example a CSV-summariser skill that also reads credentials, contacts external hosts or runs shell commands, with the extra behaviour hidden by obfuscation (string-splitting, character codes, Base64, zero-width or lookalike Unicode). The proposed defence is deterministic deobfuscation first, then a small local language model judging whether the behaviour fits the declared intent. This is defensive research on a synthetic benchmark — no live incident, no in-the-wild malicious skill and no IOCs were named — so it belongs here as a control problem, not as an attack. No new LLMjacking victim or incident was published in this window; the Google / Mandiant LLMJacking research is dated 2–3 November and is carried as standing risk, not a new incident. The Chainalysis breach’s “offensive agentic techniques” description is context from its 30 October / 4 November disclosures, not a new in-window finding.

No new confirmed AI-driven attack campaign against a BFSI victim was found in this window. The one item that needs care: a Korean outlet (Maeil Business) characterised the Mirae Asset Securities attack as a suspected AI-agent attack. That characterisation appears in the newspaper’s reporting, not in the company’s statement as reported by Yonhap, and the company has disclosed no attack method at all — so it is not presented here as confirmed AI attribution. CERT-In’s Crafty Falcon advisory and the Microsoft / Check Point ARTEX research are 4–5 November items with no new development in this window; they remain standing context, not new sightings, and they are not re-labelled as phishing or deepfake campaigns.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — Singapore’s three agencies warn on personal AI agents

CSA Singapore, GovTech and IMDA issued a joint advisory on 6 November on the safe use of personal AI agents (named examples: Dots, Hermes, Muse, Grok Bot, OpenClaw), following Meta’s Muse launch in Singapore. The warning is practical: personal AI agents act autonomously — they can send messages, make purchases and access accounts without a click — and hidden malicious instructions in websites, emails, calendar invitations or documents could trick an agent into transferring money or leaking personal data. Their five precautions: connect only the apps genuinely needed; do not give agents access to banking, government or healthcare accounts; never share passwords, OTPs, Singpass or credit-card details; require approval before consequential actions; and users remain responsible and liable for what their agent does. If money is at risk: use the banking app’s kill switch, lock the cards and call the bank’s official hotline. This is preventive guidance, not an incident report — no incident figures and no named scam campaign — but it is directly relevant to banks’ fraud and customer-education messaging and to AI-governance controls (agent-tool access, invocation monitoring). Standing context: the US OCC’s Bulletin 2026-54 (4 November) examiner framework for banks’ generative and agentic AI use, and Elastic’s finding that attackers execute commands through Claude Code and ChatGPT desktop in confirmed intrusions. Standing controls still apply: key inventory and rotation, endpoint exposure (no unauthenticated Ollama / LiteLLM on the internet), spend and invocation monitoring with revoke-and-rotate, and agent-tool detection.

Indicators & defensive actions

Notable IOCs / behavioural indicators

  • OpenSSH sshole (Previdian, 6 Nov): source IP 89.116.89[.]18 (Japan-based) — eight exploitation attempts, all from this address; sshd log lines “Accepted password for” an account that has no password (alpine, root, key-only users), followed by non-interactive sessions running id, uname, hostname.
  • SonicWall CVE-2026-19868: no IOCs published. Detection is configuration-based: SSLVPN with SAML authentication enabled on SonicOS 7.3.3-7015 and older (Gen7) or 8.2.2-8015 and older (Gen8); review SSLVPN login events for alternate-method logins that bypassed SAML.
  • NetScaler CVE-2026-88771: attacker-controlled text (for example a username containing a semicolon) POSTed to /nf/auth/doAuthentication.do, written to the appliance log and later executed by the maintenance script ns_monuploadd_err.pl — execution can lag the request by up to 24 hours, so check logs and persistence, not just the patch.
  • Mirae Asset: no IOCs published. Leaked account numbers (about 3,800) and employee names, email addresses and mobile numbers are phishing and impersonation fuel — expect messages impersonating the brokerage and its staff.
  • Bank Rakyat: actor handle “Marlanwg”; defaced pages included personal-financing sections and the iRakyat login page; the defacement directed bank officials to the Session messaging app. Defaced content was still visible in Google search results at 03:50 SGT on 7 November.
  • ARTEX (carried): service-principal sign-ins from IP 199.19.77.84 from 28 Oct across multiple application IDs; Docker image mianhuatang11/artex (about 9,000 downloads) — backdoored build served by 62 exposed consoles 2–4 Nov; 1,608 live ARTEX instances, 2,557 hosting; 10 misconfigured instances exposing full operator databases.
  • WordPress (carried, updated): top attacking IPs per Wordfence — 103.59.160.171, 2001:df1:e8c0::106b, 51.89.103.78, 185.177.72.61, 195.3.220.7; requests for staged installer paths under wp-content/upgrade/ around a core upgrade window — including during the fix install itself.

  • Arara phishing (carried): about 7.13 million phishing emails sent impersonating a card company from news@arara.com, 28–30 Oct.
