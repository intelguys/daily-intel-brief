Threat actor tracking TLP:AMBER First profile

n0n

A data-theft and destructive-extortion group that surfaced in September 2026 and built its reputation on an AI-assisted operation and a leak site listing hundreds of victims — while its own exposed server showed a far smaller operation. Tracked here: history, attack path, behaviour and procedures, indicators and victimology, with every claim tied to its source and every leak-site listing labelled as a claim until a victim confirms it or data is published.

First seen: 18 September 2026Status: ActiveLast updated: 27 November 2026Sources: 71
What it is Data-theft extortion with destructive impact

Credential-only access — no vulnerability exploitation has been documented in any intrusion. The group exfiltrates data, then encrypts files with the .n0n extension or destroys data and backups instead, and pressures victims through a Tor leak site.

SOCRadar, from the group’s own exposed workspace, October 2026.

What to believe The leak-site count is inflated

Bitdefender: the site listed more than 100 victims, but data from the group’s own file server shows it targeted just over 20 organisations. Treat every listing as a claim, not a confirmed breach.

Attack path

One lifecycle, with the impact shifting between exfiltration, encryption, destruction and network disruption depending on the target. Reconstructed from the exposed operator workspace (SOCRadar).

  1. Initial access — credentials only

    Credential stuffing against 305 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances through residential proxies (requests to /remote/logincheck carrying Cookie: APBSID=n0n), weak or default RDP/WinRM administrator credentials, exposed long-lived AWS IAM access keys, and reused hosting-provider portal sessions. The earliest reporting attributes the credentials to third-party infostealer malware. No exploit payloads and no CVE references were found in the operator’s files.

    Maritime-wave variant: for the 12 October batch, the evidence points the other way — small, application-scoped data sets and incomplete infostealer coverage across the victims suggest an exposed shared application or vulnerability (a terminal system, EDI integration or file-transfer product), not 38 separate credential intrusions. The shared source is unconfirmed.

  2. Persistence and collection

    Hidden administrator accounts disguised as services (svc_upd, svc_reg, svc<slug>), scheduled tasks named *Sync, NetAssist and similar, covert proxies, and SSH authorized keys on cloud hosts. The operator steals browser credentials (including Chrome’s app-bound key), live session cookies via cloned Chrome profiles driven over DevTools, mailboxes (a full IMAP dump of a provider staff mailbox), and emailed MFA codes. Discovery sweeps cover 445/3389/5985/1433 internally, plus EC2/ECS/SSM mapping and S3 bucket listing.

  3. Exfiltration

    Per-target pipelines with data budgets, sending everything to the operator’s central storage (the “monster” server). S3 bulk pulls run with per-bucket byte caps; exfiltration uses zip-base64 POSTs to the C2 channel (8 GiB budget) and uploads to a public file-hosting service. Data typically leaks about four days after public disclosure.

  4. Encryption and destruction

    Wrapper scripts stop services (MSSQL, SQL Agent, SSAS, SSIS, SQLWriter, W3SVC, MySQL), delete shadow copies and wipe free space before the locker runs. The locker is AES-256-CBC with a single hardcoded key per build. In some cases the operator deletes data and backups instead of encrypting — S3 mass deletion in 1,000-item batches, and a hosting provider’s servers and backups removed. On Cisco Firepower Management Center appliances, the operator flips firewall rules to BLOCK and injects a Snort rule (n0n-lock, sid 2100001); in the Inter case the group claimed this severed national network traffic.

  5. Extortion and publication

    Ransom notes, countdowns, keyed negotiation rooms (opening one requires a 10% upfront payment) and direct extortion emails. To raise pressure, n0n contacts victims’ clients, MSPs, unions and the media — in one case emailing PayPal’s security team over a breach at its contact-centre provider. Leaks are published as scrubbed ZIP archives and torrents distributed from a VPN-bound seedbox. Each victim gets a unique Bitcoin address; a check of all 55 collected addresses showed zero transactions as of 16 October 2026.

Telemetry and things worth knowing

The details that change how to think about this group.

An AI-assisted operation, not an autonomous one

The operator uses opencode, an open-source AI coding agent, configured with 154 model entries across seven providers — including “uncensored” and “abliterated” models with safety training removed, and a self-hosted model. The agent wrote and debugged hundreds of attack scripts; gateway spending passed $7,216 by 22 October 2026. The evidence points to a human-directed operation supported by AI; no fully autonomous attack system was observed. The group’s own server also held the AI environment alongside 829 scripts and five encryptor sources.

The economics do not add up — yet

Demands range from 0.5 BTC to 33 BTC per victim, and up to $28 million per organisation in the maritime wave (the reviewed subset ran $2.4–$14 million). Each victim gets a unique Bitcoin address, and opening a negotiation room requires a 10% upfront payment. A check of all 55 collected addresses showed zero transactions as of 16 October 2026. One Canadian financial-services company carries the leak site’s “HONORED” label with a settlement date of 7 October 2026 — the only settlement marker found. The gap between the claimed scale and the observed payments is unresolved.

Key reuse is a recovery opportunity

Each locker build uses a single hardcoded AES-256 key with no per-victim derivation, and one key is likely shared across two victims — so a decryptor for one could unlock the other. Bitdefender notes the same reuse. Because the operator also deletes shadow copies, wipes free space and removes provider-hosted backups, offline or immutable backups remain the only reliable recovery path.

Third-party leverage

Pressure is not limited to the victim. n0n contacts victims’ clients, MSPs, unions and the media, and in one case emailed PayPal’s security team over a breach at its contact-centre provider. The maritime wave shows the same logic at scale: the Nam Dinh Vu Port posting states its archive would expose the cargo flows, booking relationships and operational records of every carrier that called at the terminal.

Leak-site mechanics

Listings carry a status and a countdown. “PENDING EXPOSURE” anonymises the victim as “Company #N” with only a sector and location, one to three days out. “NOT PAID, EXPOSED” names the victim with a publication date. “HONORED” marks a victim that settled before its deadline. Data is offered as a direct .zip archive and a .torrent, distributed from a VPN-bound seedbox. The maritime batch broke the earlier rhythm: named postings with a single shared publication time, seven days out. Data typically leaks about four days after public disclosure.

The Fortinet connection

OSec notes a recent Fortinet brute-forcing campaign (dubbed “Fortibleed”) likely fuelled the group’s access. The group’s own credential-stuffing pipeline targeted 305 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances through residential proxies. No Fortinet vulnerability is involved — the attacks use weak or stolen credentials.

Possible connections — unconfirmed

Links reported by others, kept separate from the group’s own record until evidence ties them together.

Azazel and The Gentlemen

CloudSEK’s “Caught in 4K: The Gentlemen Files” (5 Oct 2026) documents a Russian-speaking affiliate of The Gentlemen ransomware-as-a-service group, using the name Azazel, who used the group’s tooling, negotiation channels and ransom-note template but posted victims on his own leak site (LEAKNED) and kept the proceeds. His exposed servers showed 24+ victim directories and about 6 TB of staged data, and the first reported operational use of MCP’s exec_in_session as a command-and-control channel — a Penelope reverse-shell handler registered as a tool in an AI coding assistant, driven through a local MCP endpoint at 127.0.0.1:35367.

Aggregator detection content (Threadlinqs TL-2026-5634) associates this cluster with n0n at low confidence. The association is not adopted here: the two exposed workspaces are different servers with different toolsets (OpenCode and credential stuffing for n0n; Penelope and MCP for Azazel), SOCRadar’s n0n mapping contains no MCP technique, and Bitdefender treats the two as separate stories in the same debrief. If evidence later ties them together, it will be recorded in the TTP change log.

Attribution

No definitive geographic or national attribution exists. Some threat-intelligence vendors list n0n as likely Russia-based, but the evidence behind that assessment has not been shown. Cydome notes evidence that the group is not related to Iran, China or North Korea, and a detail in the maritime analysis was read as a possible indication of Christian origin — neither is attribution. SOCRadar assesses with medium confidence that the exposed server is a private workspace for a single operator or small team, while the group’s broader framework, tooling and master wallet control indicate a multi-operator operation.

Defensive actions

Drawn from SOCRadar, Fortra and Bitdefender. Prioritised for identity, remote access, cloud and backup owners.

AreaAction
Remote accessEnforce phishing-resistant MFA on RDP, SSL-VPNs and admin portals; monitor /remote/logincheck for credential stuffing (hunt Cookie: APBSID=n0n); invalidate credentials found in infostealer logs; remove internet-exposed RDP.
Firewall managementRestrict FortiManager and FMC interfaces with IP allow-lists and MFA; require approvals for policy changes; flag REST token creation from unknown IPs; hunt the injected Snort rule n0n-lock (sid 2100001).
Cloud keysReplace long-lived keys with short-lived roles; separate S3 list, read and delete permissions; enable Object Lock and versioning; alert on high-volume DeleteObjects calls.
BackupsKeep air-gapped or immutable backups and test restores — n0n purges shadow copies, wipes free space and deletes provider-hosted backups.
CredentialsEnforce strong password policy and regular rotation; hunt hidden admins (svc_*), UserList=0 accounts and root@3090-AI authorized_keys entries.
DetectionSearch EDR, CloudTrail, WinRM/RDP and firewall logs for the indicators and behaviours in this page; consider restricting PowerShell, Python and other advanced scripting for users outside IT.
Extortion readinessPrepare a data-breach and extortion-specific response plan, not just ransomware recovery — stolen data remains stolen even if systems are restored from backup. Ask suppliers first when a company appears on a leak site: much of n0n’s claimed data is the kind suppliers hold.

Could not verify

Open questions, stated plainly rather than filled in.

  • The maritime shared source. The CyberLogitec connection is unverified — the provider has not confirmed an incident — and no listed maritime organisation has publicly confirmed one. The shared-application hypothesis rests on data shape, not on an identified system.
  • The Qubole link for the 16 November batch. Ransomware.live assesses the listings as most likely Qubole-sourced, and Wiz’s 11 November update relays a report that n0n claimed the Qubole campaign — but Wiz has not verified the claim, Wiz found no ties between the campaign and other publicly reported activity, and Expedia has not named Qubole. The link is assessed, not confirmed. The group’s claimed haul figures (53.9 GB across 30,743 files; nearly a petabyte from customer networks) are actor claims, unverified. Check Point’s 19 November update independently assesses the same supply-chain conclusion with moderate confidence, on the shared file structure of the samples.
  • Whether any victim paid. One “HONORED” settlement marker exists (a Canadian financial-services company, 7 Oct 2026), but the 55 collected Bitcoin addresses showed zero transactions as of 16 Oct 2026. The two do not reconcile publicly.
  • Whether the maritime data was actually exfiltrated. The listings describe small, application-scoped data sets; whether data was published after the 19 October deadline in the volumes described has not been established here.
  • The Azazel link. Aggregator-reported at low confidence; the two operations are documented separately and the toolsets differ.
  • Attribution. No definitive geographic or national attribution; “likely Russia” is a vendor assessment without published evidence.
  • Backup destruction, case by case. S3 mass deletion and provider backup removal are documented in specific cases; the group’s declared threat to destroy backups is broader than the documented executions, and no verified count of backup-destruction incidents exists.
  • The full victim list. Trackers disagree by methodology (listings vs named victims vs placeholders vs workspace evidence). This page carries the counts side by side and does not merge them.
  • No CVE is tied to any n0n intrusion. Every documented compromise relied on weak or stolen credentials; that is an absence in the available evidence, not proof no vulnerability was ever used.
