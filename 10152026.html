The dominant development of the last 24 hours is confirmation by South Korea's Financial Services Commission that 16 financial firms — banks, savings banks, a credit-finance company, P2P lenders, an e-payment provider and two virtual-asset businesses — were breached in a campaign that regulators publicly characterise as AI-assisted, with roughly 100,000 customer records exposed. This is the first time a national financial regulator has attributed an intrusion campaign of this shape to agentic AI tooling in an official setting, and it is the reference case the sector should be reading this week.

Two other items in the window matter for control design rather than for their victim count. In India, CERT-In's advisory on the abuse of payout and fund-transfer APIs describes attackers calling disbursement endpoints from the victims' own whitelisted infrastructure, bypassing loan creation, KYC, sanctions, beneficiary and per-transaction limit checks entirely. That is a business-logic bypass rather than a software exploit, and it transfers to any institution exposing disbursement APIs. In Europe, a Unit 42 report describes an unnamed bank entered through weak authentication on an externally managed third-party gateway, followed by months of undetected reconnaissance. Both describe failures of authorisation and monitoring, not of patching.

The regulatory backdrop is tightening on the same theme. The ECB's action plans on AI-enabled cyber threats are due by 31 October, and the ECB letter names frontier-model exploit generation as the driver. US and European supervisors are separately questioning banks about their own use of AI agents. For most institutions the binding near-term deadline is the ECB one; the strategic exposure is that AI-assisted intrusion is no longer a forecast in supervisory documents but an evidenced incident pattern.

03APAC

South Korea: agentic-AI-assisted intrusions across 16 financial firms

High confidence Actor unattributed Confirmed by regulator

On 14 October the FSC, the Financial Supervisory Service and the Financial Security Institute convened a second emergency response meeting and confirmed — as reported by the Korea Times and Yonhap — that the incident that began at Shinhan Bank had extended to 16 firms: commercial banks (Shinhan, KB Kookmin, Busan, Hana), savings banks (Yegaram, Welcome, DB, OK), credit-finance companies (Hyundai Capital, KDB Capital), P2P lenders (PFCT, Mowda), an e-payment provider (BrightFix), virtual-asset businesses (Streami, operator of Gopax, and Prabang) and MG Community Credit Cooperatives. Approximately 100,000 records — names, contact details, resident registration numbers and email addresses — are affected. The FSC states no account passwords or card numbers were exposed, and that the count of affected firms may still grow.

The same review covered all 555 financial companies against a 12-point checklist: 425 were assessed as adequate and 130 (23.4%) reported shortcomings, concentrated in patch management and control systems and heaviest among e-finance and P2P lending firms. Regulators signalled twice-yearly mandatory vulnerability testing, a joint AI-based monitoring system, and briefings holding executives accountable.

CrowdStrike's 7 October report on the campaign is the technical anchor. It describes an unidentified, likely Chinese-speaking and financially motivated actor using ARTEX, a Chinese-developed open-source agentic penetration-testing tool, alongside LLM agents — Claude Code session histories and memory files were recovered from an exposed directory, with DeepSeek v4.1-flash named as the primary model backend and GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6 also observed. Entry points reported in industry coverage were a loan-progress inquiry service used by financial brokers at one bank and an employee mobile work-support system at another.

Two cautions belong with this finding. First, no actor is named: CrowdStrike assesses a "likely" Chinese speaker "with moderate confidence," and the personal details it recovered from a résumé the tooling was asked to generate are explicitly not confirmed as the operator's. Treat any named individual surfacing in secondary coverage as unverified.

Numeric conflict — report both, trust the regulator

At least one aggregator (FlagThis) has circulated a figure of roughly 12 million records and a loss of about USD 210 million. The FSC's own disclosure on 14 October states approximately 100,000 records, and the regulator is the primary source. The two cannot both describe the same event. Use the regulator's figure; treat the larger number as unsourced until a primary source supports it.

Infrastructure published by CrowdStrike, reproduced here for hunt purposes: 38.244.50[.]120 (ARTEX instance and open directory containing a /.claude/CLAUDE.md document), an unnamed Hong Kong–based host described as primary attacker infrastructure, a suspected LLM API proxy at xcai[.]pro, and nine proxy addresses: 101.53.80[.]20, 205.214.59[.]31, 124.155.252[.]63, 154.201.79[.]246, 23.248.249[.]90, 23.158.220[.]98, 103.248.148[.]84, 203.160.133[.]172, 209.209.85[.]38.

India: payout-API abuse drained funds through whitelisted infrastructure

High confidence National advisory

CERT-In, jointly with the financial-sector CSIRT, issued advisory CIAD-2026-0046 on 9 October after attacks against roughly 20 financial institutions; Indian financial press reported the content on 14–15 October. The technique is the notable part. Attackers obtained stolen credentials for payment aggregator, gateway and partner-bank APIs and then called disbursement and fund-transfer endpoints directly, from the victims' own whitelisted infrastructure, bypassing loan creation, KYC, sanction, beneficiary verification and per-transaction limits.

Funds moved as fictitious loan references and over-limit payments via IMPS and NEFT into mule accounts, then withdrawn rapidly. CERT-In notes log deletion and EDR tampering after the fact, and states the evidence suggests AI-assisted offensive tooling. Contributing weaknesses it lists include deprecated or unauthenticated APIs, hard-coded long-lived tokens in source code, plaintext credentials and absent MFA. A payout platform from a Chennai-based software vendor, with weak KYC and merchant-verification controls, is reported among the exploited components.

The recommended controls are the substance: suspend compromised APIs, rotate credentials, enforce MFA, require maker-checker approval, impose per-transaction and daily API ceilings, use penny-drop and beneficiary name matching, cryptographically bind multi-stage flows, move secrets to a vault or HSM, allow-list outbound traffic, retain 180 days of centralised logs, and protect EDR from tampering.

Why this generalises: the control that failed is authorisation on a business process, not a patchable vulnerability. Any institution that lets a privileged API caller drive a multi-step financial workflow with weak binding between the steps inherits this exposure regardless of geography.

Hirudo: Android banker that replaces the real banking app

Medium-high confidence Single vendor research

Research published 14 October describes Hirudo, a newly identified Android banking trojan that takes an unusual approach to hardened targets. Rather than injecting into the legitimate app, it runs the real app's uninstaller, downloads a patched copy, and uses its Accessibility permission to install the replacement. The patched app carries a hooking engine built on the open-source YAHFA and Dobby frameworks, which returns a substituted signing certificate when queried, conceals active Accessibility services, disables screenshot protection so the operator can view and record the screen, and rewrites key attestation — routing Play Integrity requests through an operator-controlled server.

Reported targeting covers seven Indonesian banks (eight malicious app copies) with additional activity against institutions in China, India, Mexico, Morocco and Thailand. Distribution is through apparently legitimate apps — a shopping app, an adult-content app and short-drama apps — that present a fake update prompt leading to Accessibility permission. Capabilities include theft of credentials, PINs, device unlock secrets and SMS, live screen streaming, camera access, and fake checkout pages that collect the payment PIN and then display a fabricated success message.

One point in defenders' favour: the bundled attestation keybox has leaked publicly and Google has flagged two of its certificates as compromised, so server-side revocation catches the forged attestations. That is a temporary advantage and depends on the institution actually validating Play Integrity server-side and acting on revocation lists, which many banking apps do not do consistently.

Japan: vendor-mediated exposure

Japan's Financial Services Agency urged banks on 9 October to review cybersecurity, third-party risk and incident response after a run of breaches. Daiwa Securities disclosed on 5 October that a compromise at external vendor Scala Communications may have exposed data on up to 110,000 clients — names, email addresses and securities account numbers, with roughly 220,000 records in scope — while Daiwa's own systems were not breached. Other Japanese incidents in the same period include Times Car Rental (~6.6M accounts) and Lawson (~2.15M customer records). Japanese officials have characterised the period in unusually direct terms.

04EMEA

European bank entered through a third-party gateway, then probed a WSUS server

Medium confidence Institution unnamed, no IOCs published

A Unit 42 report published 13 October and covered the following day describes an unnamed European bank compromised via compromised credentials on an externally managed third-party gateway where authentication was weak. Unit 42 is explicit that the cause was weak authentication on the managed device, not a disclosed software exploit in the gateway.

The intrusion then followed a patient pattern: repeated host and network scanning across several protocols, interacting with thousands of systems, plus months of password guessing aimed largely at administrator accounts. The actor scraped internal pages and attachments and searched operational and network shares. The final observed stage was an attempted remote code execution against the WSUS role on the bank's central configuration management server — a high-value target, since controlling software distribution means controlling every managed endpoint. Cortex XDR detected and contained the attempt before escalation; the bank reviewed endpoint records across thousands of systems and found no backdoors, then rotated credentials and expanded detection coverage.

Unit 42 characterises the actor as opportunistic — "noisy actions exposed basic security gaps rather than a confirmed, advanced spying operation" — and does not assign a WSUS CVE to the incident from the available evidence. No IOCs were published, which limits what detection teams can do with the report beyond reviewing the control set it implicates.

Italy: Directa SIM trading outage handled under DORA

Medium-high confidence DORA procedures invoked

A DDoS attack disabled the trading platform of Directa SIM, an Italian brokerage listed on Euronext Growth Milan, on Friday 9 October; the company reported on 13 October. The CEO described abnormal traffic deliberately overloading the systems. The incident was handled jointly with CSIRT Italia under the EU's DORA procedures. The company states no unauthorised access or customer data loss occurred and that no ransom demand was made — notable in itself, since extortion is the usual motive in financial-sector intrusions and a pure availability attack points to a different actor set. Directa offered customers a commission-free trading day in November.

This is a small institution, but it is the clearest worked example this month of DORA's operational-resilience provisions being exercised for a live availability incident at a European financial firm, which makes it worth reading as a process case rather than a threat case.

UAE: Central Bank directs zero-trust audits

Low-medium confidence Single secondary source

Reporting on 15 October states the Central Bank of the UAE has ordered licensed financial institutions to upgrade technology and carry out zero-trust security audits, citing sophisticated cross-border threats. No deadlines, scope or instrument reference are given in the available reporting, and it rests on a single secondary source. Treat the direction as probable and the detail as Unknown pending the Central Bank's own notice. Regional context: UAE and Saudi Arabia accounted for about half of Gulf cyberattacks in the first half of 2026, with the UAE the single most targeted market.

West Africa: microfinance platform compromised, mobile-money cash-out

Medium confidence Local press, single source

Reporting from Senegal on 14 October describes an intrusion into Avia, a digital finance platform operated by GSIE Technology for the microfinance institution ACEP. Roughly 1,219 client accounts were affected, with 842 transfers initiated worth about 168.2 million FCFA and 79 instant transfers executed into Orange Money wallets; losses exceeded 11 million FCFA. The reported technique combined exploitation of a vulnerability with web shells for persistence, and a 22-year-old was arrested after receiving a transfer.

The structure is worth noting because it is the mobile-money version of the Indian case: an application-layer foothold converted directly into instant, largely irreversible cash-out through a retail wallet network. Recovery options are materially worse than in card or wire fraud, where chargeback and recall mechanisms exist. Kenya's draft National Payment System Bill would require M-Pesa and Airtel Money to report such incidents immediately to the Central Bank, and Nigeria's authorities arraigned a suspect on 13 October over an alleged ₦340 million attack on MTN's MoMo Payment Service Bank. Regulators across the region are converging on mandatory incident reporting for exactly this exposure.

Binding deadline — 31 October 2026

The ECB's Supervisory Board Chair wrote to the CEOs of all significant institutions on 7 July 2026 (SSM-2026-0301) requiring a comprehensive action plan on AI-enabled cybersecurity threats, submitted to each institution's Joint Supervisory Team by 31 October 2026 (KPMG analysis). The letter cites frontier models that identify vulnerabilities and generate working exploits at speed, published alongside a European Systemic Risk Board warning describing a collapse of defensive time buffers. Six areas are required: attack-surface prioritisation; accelerated vulnerability and patch management at scale; enhanced monitoring and AI-enabled defence; governance, training and supply-chain assurance; defence-in-depth and legacy modernisation; and operational resilience, crisis management and information sharing. Euro-area subsidiaries of J.P. Morgan, Goldman Sachs, Citi and Morgan Stanley fall within scope. The annual IT Risk Questionnaire was deferred to February 2027 in partial relief; a separate ECB letter on post-quantum cryptography is signalled.

05AMER

Brazil: Incransom claims 1.53 TB from H2 Financiamento

Leak-site claim Unverified

On 14 October at 16:33 UTC the Incransom group listed FINANCIAMENTO LTDA (H2 Financiamento, Brazil) as a new victim, claiming exfiltration of 1.53 TB across roughly 3.6 million files. The claimed data set includes client identity documents, CPF numbers, driver's licences, risk assessments, bank statements and tax invoices.

Read the named third parties carefully

The listing names Caixa Econômica Federal, Construtora Tenda, Cury Construtora, Santander Brasil and Morgan Stanley. These appear as relationships evidenced inside the stolen material — counterparties or partners named in the compromised company's own records. That is not the same as those institutions having been compromised, and no such institution has confirmed an incident. Reporting that lists them as affected overstates the available evidence. The claim itself is a threat-actor assertion and is unverified.

Incransom has operated as a double-extortion RaaS since mid-2023, with a victim count approaching 1,000. Its documented techniques include valid accounts, exploitation of public-facing applications and phishing; it has previously cited SonicWall SMA1000 vulnerabilities, though no entry vector is confirmed for this incident.

MoneyGram: The Gentlemen claim, unconfirmed by the company

Leak-site claim No samples provided

The Gentlemen group posted a claim against MoneyGram on 12 October — just outside the 24-hour window but still developing. No data samples were published alongside the claim and MoneyGram has not confirmed an incident. MoneyGram serves roughly 60 million customers across more than 200 countries, so a validated compromise there carries substantial downstream social-engineering and fraud risk for any institution whose customers use it for remittances. Absent samples or confirmation, treat this as an unsubstantiated claim.

Regional context: LATAM desktop banking trojans remain active

Medium confidence Single vendor write-up

A vendor write-up published 14 October documents an active Casbaneiro (also tracked as Metamorfo and Ponteiro) campaign pairing the Delphi banking trojan with a malicious Chromium extension. The chain begins with a phishing email pushing a remote-access client, moves to PowerShell execution, and delivers the payload through DLL side-loading of a legitimately signed installer binary, VmLauncher.exe, alongside a malicious zlibai.dll. The companion extension communicates over WebSockets to inject HTML overlays and steal cookies and credentials, reporting to a panel branded Painel Gangster.

Targeting is stated as roughly 65 Portuguese and Brazilian banking portals plus 11 cryptocurrency exchanges, webmail providers and typosquatted webmail domains. The write-up includes C2 domains and eight IP addresses, but publishes no campaign start date, so its novelty rests on the vendor's statement that the infrastructure remained active.

06Cross-regional themes

Actively exploited Ivanti Connect Secure zero-day (CVE-2026-104935)

High confidence Added to KEV 13 Oct

CISA issued an alert on 13 October on an actively exploited authentication-bypass vulnerability in Ivanti Connect Secure, enabling remote, unauthenticated execution of arbitrary commands for credential theft and data exfiltration. The same day it was added to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. Affected versions are 25.1.1.1 and prior, 25.1.2.2 and prior, and 22.8R2.4 and prior, with 22.8 reaching end of life on 31 January 2027.

This is included here rather than as a regional item because of where it lands operationally: SSL VPN concentrators are the standard remote-access path into financial networks, and they sit at the boundary that third-party and administrative access has to traverse. CISA's recommended mitigations are unusually invasive — factory reset, reimaging, restoring configuration, and resetting passwords, certificates, API keys and licence server credentials — which reflects the judgement that a compromised appliance cannot be trusted by inspection alone. Institutions should treat an unpatched or previously compromised appliance as requiring rebuild, not patching, and should check retrospective logs for post-exploitation activity rather than only confirming the patch level.

Authorisation is failing more often than patching

Three of this window's significant incidents turned on authorisation rather than a software vulnerability: the Indian payout-API abuse bypassed business controls using valid credentials, the European bank was entered through weak authentication on a managed gateway, and the Korean campaign reached 16 firms through services that should not have been reachable. This matters for prioritisation. Patch cycles address the smaller share of what actually worked.

Third parties are the common entry surface

The European bank, Daiwa Securities via Scala Communications, the Indian payout platform vendor, and the Korean broker-facing loan service are all instances of the same structure: the institution's exposure runs through a supplier that its own controls do not reach. DORA's third-party pillar and the US banking agencies' third-party guidance both address this, but the incidents show the gap is in assurance depth, not in policy availability.

AI is now an evidenced capability, not a forecast

The Korean campaign is the first instance of a financial regulator publicly attributing a campaign to agentic AI tooling, and the ECB's 31 October deadline is predicated on the same shift. Two consequences deserve attention: detection built on the assumption that reconnaissance and lateral movement take days will generate false negatives, and defences that themselves use AI agents become an attack surface requiring governance — which is precisely what US and European supervisors are now asking banks about.

Reputational and fraud risk outlives the intrusion

Every high-profile item in this window involves personal data — identity documents, contact details, financial records — where the durable harm is downstream fraud against customers and the institution's brand, not operational downtime. The Korean regulator's public assurance that no card or account credentials were exposed is itself a fraud-prevention measure, and one worth noting as a communications pattern.

07Recommendations

Immediate — 0 to 7 days
  1. File the ECB action plan if in scope. The 31 October deadline is 16 days out and applies to all significant institutions and the euro-area subsidiaries of named US banks. The six required areas are enumerated in the section above.
  2. Audit who can call your disbursement and fund-transfer APIs. Map every caller, confirm credential lifetime, enforce MFA and maker-checker on multi-step flows, and verify that loan creation, KYC and beneficiary checks cannot be skipped by a direct API call. This is CERT-In's finding and it is directly transferable.
  3. Hunt the published Korean infrastructure. The nine proxy addresses and 38.244.50[.]120 are available now; the xcai[.]pro proxy is worth blocking outright. Note that proxy infrastructure is a weak selector — treat these as hunt leads, not attribution.
  4. Confirm Play Integrity is validated server-side and that certificate revocation lists are actioned, given the Hirudo keybox leak. Where banking apps do not currently check, this is a cheap fix with a known attacker technique against it.
  5. Inventory externally managed gateways and their authentication. The European bank's entry point was weak authentication on a third-party managed device — an assurance question to put to suppliers this week.
Short term — 1 to 4 weeks
  1. Re-baseline detection for AI-assisted speed. If alerting assumes days-long reconnaissance, compress the assumed dwell time and test whether your detection would catch a scanning phase measured in hours.
  2. Reassess configuration-management servers as a tier-zero asset. The attempted WSUS RCE shows the logic: control of software distribution is control of every endpoint. Confirm these hosts are segmented, monitored and not reachable from third-party networks.
  3. Review mobile banking integrity controls against the app-replacement technique — signature substitution, concealed Accessibility services and disabled screenshot protection — and check whether your app or MDM detects them.
  4. Stress-test DORA operational-resilience procedures using the Directa SIM case as a template: an availability-only incident at a small broker, handled through the national CSIRT. Confirm your own call tree works when there is no data breach and therefore no established breach-notification workflow to follow.
  5. Establish a watch on leak-site claims naming your counterparties. The H2 Financiamento listing shows how a supplier's breach surfaces your institution's name in criminal publications without your having been compromised — and pre-draft the holding statement.
Strategic — 1 to 6 months
  1. Govern your own AI agents as an attack surface. Supervisors are asking whether institutions use foreign-built AI tooling, can control autonomous systems and can shut them down. The Korean case shows agentic tooling on the offensive side; the same governance question applies to defensive deployments.
  2. Shift assurance from vendor attestation to continuous third-party monitoring. The recurring failure is not that suppliers are unaudited but that audits do not reach the specific control that fails — here, authentication on a managed gateway and authorisation on a payout API.
  3. Prepare for post-quantum expectations. The ECB has signalled a forthcoming letter on post-quantum cryptography; inventory of long-lived cryptographic dependencies is the preparatory step.

08Method, confidence and gaps

Confidence rubric applied

High requires two or more independent sources, where independent means the parties producing the evidence are distinct — not a vendor report counted again by each outlet that carried it. The Korean campaign, the Indian advisory and the ECB deadline meet that bar; each has a primary institutional source. Items resting on a single vendor write-up (Hirudo, Casbaneiro, the Unit 42 bank) are Medium or Medium-High. Leak-site claims (Incransom, The Gentlemen) are unverified by construction and are labelled as claims throughout.

Known gaps

  • No actor attribution anywhere in this brief. The Korean campaign's operator is unnamed, and CrowdStrike's assessment of a likely Chinese-speaking, financially motivated actor is explicitly moderate-confidence. The personal details circulating in secondary coverage are not confirmed.
  • Victim counts in Korea are the regulator's as of 14 October and the FSC states the figure may rise. Any number published later this week may differ.
  • The UAE directive rests on a single secondary source with no instrument reference or deadline; it should be confirmed against the Central Bank's own publication.
  • The Unit 42 report publishes no IOCs and does not name the institution, its country or the gateway vendor, so it can inform control review but not detection content.
  • Two aggregator-sourced numbers were rejected: the 12 million record / USD 210 million figure for Korea, which conflicts with the regulator, and a Vietnamese SWIFT "heist" item whose dateline was inconsistent with its content, which described a 2016 event.
  • Reporting window limits. One source retrieved during collection returned HTTP 403 and could not be read; the regulatory-scrutiny item it covered is therefore corroborated but not verified against its original text.
