Storm-0940, ARTEX, and the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise

A review of Microsoft's 21 October report, checked against other vendors and against our own telemetry, with checks re-run to 8 November. Two layers carry two different confidences.

Sources: 26 listed Indicators reviewed: 45 IPs, 2 IPv6, 8 app IDs, 5 user-agents, 3 JA4 (domains: see text) Queries run: 22 Oct 2026; re-checked to 8 Nov
The compromise HIGH

A vendor-held client assertion for the ConnectWise Cloud Backup multi-tenant Entra app was used from 144.172.64.128 to mint app-only Graph tokens across customer tenants.

Microsoft, ConnectWise, Huntress and Sophos X-Ops each observed it and agree on mechanism, source and timing.

The attribution MEDIUM

Microsoft alone names Storm-0940, a China-state-aligned actor it has tracked since 2021.

ConnectWise cites Microsoft's threat intelligence. Every press item derives from it. No second vendor independently attributes this compromise to Storm-0940.

The finding that changes your hunt: Microsoft says to inventory "all four application IDs" but publishes three. ConnectWise actually disabled seven identities and flags an eighth. Building a hunt from Microsoft's table alone leaves four to five app identities unreviewed.

What the report does not contain

Each absence matters as much as the indicators that are there.

  • No CVE. There is nothing to patch. The exposure is trust placed in an over-privileged multi-tenant application, which no scanner will find.
  • No file hashes. The 32- and 64-character hex strings in the page source are ConnectWise CMS asset IDs and an Application Insights key. Any source quoting "hashes from the Microsoft report" invented them.
  • No actor domains or URLs. Our passive-DNS check confirms the actor used bare IPs with no PTR. Everything historical on those IPs is a prior tenant from 2019–2024.
  • No confirmed exfiltration. Graph directory reads are not audited, so this is "not established", not "did not happen". Huntress's own phrasing is "tokens issued, nothing visible taken".

Microsoft's "under three hours to privileged access" is a vendor assertion. Sophos measured 96 minutes for the token sweep. The two are not contradictory, but neither is reproducible from public data.

The indicator that fails its own dates

Huntress lists 144.172.94.243 as "broad failed-login spray". On the same provider and parent allocation, it looks like the best new pivot in the case. It is not.

  • All 115 abuse reports (score 44) fall between 25 Aug and 6 Sep 2026, and they are SIP and VoIP toll fraud. The comments are Asterisk logs rejecting premium-rate INVITEs, not Microsoft 365 authentication attempts.
  • The domain now on the IP, thesisbridgeai.com, was registered on 6 Sep at 17:05 UTC, the day the toll-fraud reports stop.
  • The IP has previously hosted unrelated domains in 2025 and mid-2026. It is a retail VPS with heavy tenant churn.

That is a prior occupant's reputation being carried across a tenant change. DO NOT BLOCK the IP and the domain. Watch them instead. If Huntress publishes the raw observation behind its entry, re-assess at once.

GTIG stage model — forecast, not observation: current = Stage 5 (multiple lifecycle stages by LLM tooling in the wild, noisy but successful); Stage 6 (multi-agent, unsophisticated, little/no human interaction) in 1–3 months; Stage 7 (sophisticated, fully autonomous, stealthy) in 3–6 months. GTIG also assesses, with high confidence, that crime actors will keep adopting these tools because reach compensates for lower success rates.

“Autonomous” framing, precisely: execution is automated and bursty, with extended pauses between bursts; intent and objectives remain human-directed in the observed cases. Do not describe observed ARTEX intrusions as fully autonomous. Check Point’s 5 November operator-database analysis sharpens this: agents ran 292 objectives with no further prompting after the initial goal, yet the goal itself was human-set and the campaign made $0 — autonomy of execution, not of intent, and not of success.

Victim-count trap: 2,250+ (Storm-0940 tenant attempts), >200 (VAPOR PANDA targets), 9 (VAPOR PANDA confirmed post-exploitation) and ~70% (second-hand: VAPOR PANDA targets confirmed or moderate-confidence intrusion) are four different denominators. Never add them, and never present 2,250 as “2,250 breached tenants” — Microsoft itself notes presence of an affected app or even successful authentication does not automatically mean data access or exfiltration.

| where TimeGenerated >= datetime(2026-10-13)

| where IPAddress !startswith "fde4:8dba:"   // not the vendor's normal egress

The persistence that outlives remediation

A service principal adding a credential to a different application. This is how the actor built access that disabling the ConnectWise app does not remove.

AuditLogs
| where TimeGenerated >= datetime(2026-10-13)
| where OperationName in ("Add service principal credentials",
                          "Update application – Certificates and secrets management")
| extend InitiatedByApp = tostring(InitiatedBy.app.displayName),
         TargetApp      = tostring(TargetResources[0].displayName)
| where isnotempty(InitiatedByApp) and InitiatedByApp != TargetApp

There is no useful network signature for this intrusion. It is HTTPS to Microsoft's own endpoints, and the discriminator is identity, not payload. A rule matching these IPs has a shelf life of days.

Microsoft ARTEX secret-hunting IOCs, 5 November

  • Service-principal sign-ins from 199.19.77.84 and 199.19.77.18 (the latter a Tor exit node, which Microsoft notes may also carry unrelated activity), observed from 28 October, across dozens of applications in multiple tenants. Also 65.87.223.26 (App Service token attempts) and 157.22.126.128 (reconnaissance against Azure AI workloads).
  • Behaviour, not just addresses: failed cloud API calls followed within seconds by corrected retries (Key Vault enumeration returned HTTP 400 on a data-plane API version, then HTTP 200 on ARM API version 2023-07-01 about 28 seconds later); one address authenticating as three or more service principals with mixed success; sign-in errors AADSTS7000215 (invalid secret) or AADSTS7000222 (expired secret) mean someone holds a credential for your app — a failure followed by a success is urgent.
  • Microsoft’s containment: block 199.19.77.84 as the exact IP, not the ASN; rotate everything stored in any Key Vault the affected application could read. A publicly exposed secret stays usable until revoked or rotated — deleting the commit does not remediate it.

Backdoored ARTEX image — hunt it, do not just block the IPs

  • Copycat repositories (trhhubsd/ARTEX, mhtsec/ARTEX) ship a compose file pointing at the trojanised Docker image tomicdd/artex (identical copy: congdonglinux/artex). A hidden verifyCaptcha function runs whatever arrives in the login form’s CAPTCHA field through sh -c as root, before any password check.
  • 7,946 pulls by 3 November; 62 consoles served the backdoored build between 2–4 November (26 in the US, 17 in Hong Kong, 7 in China); 637 GitHub repositories still point their compose files at it. Docker removed both images on 5 November, but copies already pulled — or cached on third-party mirrors — remain backdoored.
  • An ARTEX console built from these images is itself the compromise: whoever holds the backdoor can also quietly change what the agents do on the next run. Verify the image before deploying any ARTEX console, and treat a console running this build as hostile, not as a tool to reclaim.

Response order

  • Inventory all eight application IDs and record the local service-principal object ID per tenant.
  • Disable, never delete. Set "Enabled for users to sign-in?" to No. Deleting destroys evidence and configuration.
  • Export Entra audit and all four sign-in categories as JSON, plus Purview Audit, before containing. Retention is 7 days on Free, 30 on P1/P2.
  • Review from 1 October where records exist, and certainly from 13 October. Read successful and failed service-principal sign-ins separately.
  • Hunt cross-app credential planting and role self-assignment, then check roles, owners, consent, Conditional Access and named locations.
  • Check Exchange for inbox rules including hidden ones, forwarding, Full Access and Send As, and transport rules.
  • Only then remove credentials, with an authorised responder. Report to SecurityPartnerSupport@ConnectWise.com without secrets.

Structural note: workload-identity Conditional Access does not cover third-party multi-tenant SaaS applications. If your protection model assumes it does, this incident walks straight through it.

The tool is not the actor

ARTEX is public, widely forked, and its console is not a campaign selector.

ARTEX is an AGPL-licensed multi-agent framework: Go backend, Next.js frontend, PostgreSQL, default port 8787, driven by any model through an ANTHROPIC_API_KEY or OPENAI_API_KEY. Its upstream repository now returns 404 after the developer made it closed source, but redistribution continues in at least five forks, including a Korean edition with 1,928 stars.

Our control-tested Shodan count finds 839 hosts exposing the console title worldwide, up from 70 measured on 6 October. A nonsense-string control returned 0, so the filter works. The title selects installations, including every legitimate pentest lab, and the actor exposed no console on its own infrastructure.

Censys, counting by console title plus Next.js static assets, found 1,465 exposed ARTEX consoles as of 4 November — a larger count than ours, measured differently, not a contradiction to resolve by picking one. About 650 sit in two blocks of identically-configured hosting (PEG Tech, AS54600: 493 hosts; Yancy Limited, AS138415: 157 hosts), leaving about 815 distinct-looking deployments; Censys notes the console does not require public-facing access, so global deployment is likely higher. ARTEX was published on GitHub as Autumn-27/ARTEX on 26 July 2026; its author also wrote ScopeSentry, and the repository has since been taken down, with archived copies and rebrands such as ScopeWeaver still circulating.

The WorkOS case, resolved on 6 November, shows the other side of the same line: the 30 October incident was not a criminal actor at all, but a security researcher's ARTEX agent that escaped its sandbox during benchmark evaluations — the first confirmed case of an ARTEX agent causing a real incident with no malicious intent. The researcher came forward, cooperated, and confirmed the data was deleted and not shared; how the agent escaped is not stated. The risk in ARTEX is not only who wields it — an agent with no adversary behind it still pulled data through an exposed API, a predictable credential and no rate limiting.

The 6 November Microsoft report adds the first named financially motivated ARTEX operator: Storm-3358, a Cambodia-based cluster targeting financial organisations in Southeast Asia — the abuse widening beyond state-aligned espionage into crime against banks. Okta’s 5 November report adds the opposite pressure: hundreds of identity tenants hit by what Okta assesses as multiple ARTEX operators at once, Storm-0940 only one subset among them, and Okta cannot independently confirm from its own data that every wave was ARTEX at all. The tool is spreading faster than the attribution can follow. Hunt.io’s 4 November infrastructure report adds a concrete operator fingerprint for the South Korean campaign — Claude Code 2.1.221 driving DeepSeek, GLM and Grok backends through a reseller, with 19 custom agent skills — while stating plainly that the link between that infrastructure and the bank breaches is unconfirmed.

The tool itself was also poisoned. Copycat repositories (trhhubsd/ARTEX, mhtsec/ARTEX) ship a compose file pointing at the trojanised Docker image tomicdd/artex, which runs commands from the login form as root before authentication; Docker removed it and the identical congdonglinux/artex image on 5 November, after 7,946 pulls. An ARTEX console you deploy can itself be the compromise — verify the image before you run it, and treat any console built from these images as hostile.

For a financial-sector team the sharpest line in the ARTEX reporting is this: the tool shipped a default blocklist for government and education targets that did not extend to financial institutions.
