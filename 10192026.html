Executive summary

The week's defining failure was containment, not detection

The Korean campaign that dominated the previous two briefs did not recede over the weekend. It recurred — at one of the same institutions, four days after the first intrusion, from an address that was not on the blocklist circulated after the first. That detail is the most operationally useful fact in this window: an indicator list is not a containment measure, and a bank that treats it as one will be breached again by the same actor.

Two disclosures in this window carry the second theme. Romania's central securities depository confirmed an intrusion into its retail investor portal exposing national identity numbers, ID-card details and IBANs — the exact combination that makes a follow-up phone call convincing. Separately, two unverified leak-site claims touch market plumbing rather than retail banking: a Dutch FX liquidity provider's FIX session credentials, and a payment orchestration platform's merchant data. Neither is confirmed, and we treat both as claims. Both are cheap to check.

On the regulatory side, the window added statements rather than obligations: the IMF's managing director on 17 October and Korea's FSS governor on 19 October both framed AI-accelerated attack as a supervisory expectation. The binding dates remain unchanged — the ECB's AI-related action plans are due 31 October, nine working days away.

Sources: Reuters via Business Recorder — IMF, 17 Oct · The Next Web — ECB end-October deadline, 14 Oct

Region · APAC

Korea: same campaign, new victims, and a containment that did not hold

All five in-window APAC items are Korean, and all five belong to one investigation thread rather than five separate incidents. We keep them together deliberately: the risk is a single campaign against third-party-facing systems at financial firms, not a wave of unrelated attacks.

OK Savings Bank — second intrusion five days after the first

HighMaterial update

Abnormal access was detected between 03:32 and 07:34 on 15 October against pages tied to the bank's mobile deposit and notification service. The bank disclosed the second breach on 16 October: 268 further customers, exposing names, account numbers, product names, balances, interest rates and last-transaction dates. Combined with the first intrusion — detected at 20:44 on 11 October and not blocked until roughly 10:00 the next morning, a window of about thirteen hours — the confirmed total is 518 customers.

The Financial Supervisory Service began an on-site inspection on 16 October, and has signalled it may escalate to a formal investigation. It is also inspecting Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin and the Yeogaram and Welcome savings banks.

Sources: ChosunBiz, 18 Oct · Yonhap Infomax · Aju Press · Herald Business

The sentence that matters is not the count. The second intrusion used a source address that was not among those the Korea Financial Security Institute had circulated after the first. Whatever remediation followed the 11 October event did not close the exposed pages.

What it means for usAfter an incident, the question to ask is not "did we block the addresses" but "what did we change about the interface that was reached." An indicator list narrows the next attempt; it does not remove the path. If our own post-incident playbook closes with an IOC distribution, it is incomplete.

DB Savings Bank — 142 individuals, and data replayed against a loan-limit API

Medium

Reported on 17 October: personal data of 142 people — 122 current and former staff plus 20 outsourced project contractors — leaked through internet-banking and mobile-web systems. The incident is dated 10 October and was reported to the FSS on 11 October. Outsourced project staff are the suspected route. The leaked data was subsequently used to attempt loan-limit inquiries.

This complements, and does not repeat, the 16 October brief's single-sourced report of roughly 100 loan applications attempted under employee names. The two figures measure different things — individuals whose data leaked, versus applications attempted — and must not be added. The 142 figure is attributable to one Korean outlet; the loan-inquiry mechanism is now corroborated by a second.

Sources: Boannews, 17 Oct (the 142 figure) · ChosunBiz (the loan-inquiry mechanism)

What it means for usThis is a business-logic failure, not a perimeter one. Data obtained in one context was replayed against a credit-inquiry interface that accepted it. The control to test is whether our loan-limit and credit-inquiry endpoints verify that the subject of the inquiry is the authenticated customer — not merely that the caller is authorised.

Crypto exchange Gopax — regulator disclosed before the company did

High

The exchange published a notice on 16 October and an apology on 18 October for a breach affecting nicknames and login records of nine people — seven customers and two current or former employees. No asset losses were identified. The FSC had announced the incident on 14 October, before the company's own notice.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily, 18 Oct · Seoul Economic Daily, 14 Oct (FSC disclosure)

What it means for usThe scope is trivial; the sequencing is not. A regulator naming an incident before the firm does is a disclosure-governance failure, and under a four-hour initial-notification regime it becomes a compliance exposure in its own right.

FSS governor commits to investigation and to security spending

High

At a parliamentary audit on 19 October, FSS Governor Lee Chan-jin pledged a thorough investigation into breaches at roughly 16 financial firms affecting about 100,000 records, and urged institutions to expand security budgets and headcount and to use AI against AI-driven attacks. The regulator's earlier checklist review of 555 firms found 130 deficient, concentrated in patching and end-of-life systems.

Sources: Yonhap, 19 Oct · SBS News, 19 Oct

What it means for usExpect Korean peer institutions to increase security spend and to accelerate patching cadence. If we hold Korean subsidiaries or outsourcing relationships, budget and assurance conversations will move faster than usual.

Japan — the Daiwa contractor ripple is not new, despite 19 October coverage

High — but out of window

Daiwa Securities disclosed a breach via contractor Scala Communications on 2–3 October, with data on roughly 110,000 customers and about 220,000 records. Iwate Bank, Shikoku Bank and Sompo Japan reported possible downstream exposure on 8 October. JPCERT/CC issued advisory AT-2026-0030 on 8 October and the FSA issued a warning on 9 October.

Japanese trade coverage dated 19 October describes IPA and JPCERT/CC urging emergency inspections in finance and telecom. That is in-window reporting of an out-of-window advisory, not a new event; it is listed here so it is not mistaken for one.

Sources: JPCERT-AT-2026-0030, 8–9 Oct · FSA warning, 9 Oct · TechTarget Japan, 19 Oct (in-window coverage)

What it means for usThe mechanism — a service contractor with broad reach into a regulated firm — is the same one appearing in Romania, the Netherlands, Korea and the United States this window. See the cross-regional theme below.

Region · EMEA

Market infrastructure and payment orchestration carry the EMEA risk

One confirmed incident at a securities depository, two unverified claims against trading and payments technology, and an availability event at an open-banking aggregator. The common thread is that none of it happened at a bank.

Romania — Depozitarul Central investor portal breached

High — incidentAttribution unknown

The central depository of the Bucharest Stock Exchange group confirmed on 16 October that its "Servicii Online pentru Investitori" portal — the service where retail investors open accounts — had been the target of a cyberattack. A notice to users, quoted by Romanian outlets, lists the exposed fields: full name, address, personal numeric code (CNP), ID-card series and number, email, phone, and IBAN. The depository states the data was viewed but not modified, and that portal passwords were not compromised.

It states that settlement and registry functions ran normally, holdings and settled-transaction data were not accessed, and that the exchange itself found no BVB-owned system connected to or affected. The portal was taken temporarily offline; counter and postal service continued. The DNSC was notified and an external offensive-security firm engaged.

Three things are Unknown and should be stated as such: the number of affected investors (undisclosed), the attack vector, and attribution. One Romanian outlet places the intrusion around 8 October, which would imply an eight-day gap before disclosure; that date is single-sourced and contested.

Sources: Depozitarul Central press release (PDF) · Romania Journal · RO Times · TSN Media

What it means for usCNP plus IBAN plus phone plus proof of share ownership is a high-quality pretext set. The depository itself warned users about calls citing accurate personal details. The defensive question for any retail-facing portal is narrow and testable: is the boundary between "view" and "export" enforced, and can a single authenticated session enumerate other investors' records?

Regulatory point. A Joint ESAs Q&A published on 2 October (EBA Q&A 2026_7779) clarifies that any successful, malicious and unauthorised access to a financial entity's systems must be assessed for criticality regardless of whether the systems support critical or important functions, and regardless of whether access came via an ICT third party. "Settlement was not affected" is a factual statement; it is not by itself a DORA classification decision.

Netherlands — N0n claims FX liquidity provider's FIX credentials

Unverified claim

The N0n ransomware group listed Solid Trading B.V., a Dutch FX liquidity and trading-technology firm, on 18 October, marked "not paid — exposed." The claimed corpus includes live client trading records, a client database with password hashes, credit limits, clearing paths and fee schedules, trade history, bank matching audit records, and — the item that matters — FIX venue session configurations including login credentials for liquidity providers.

Solid Trading has not confirmed the incident. Every published detail traces back to the group's own leak-site post; the four or five outlets carrying it are reprinting one source, not corroborating each other. Treat the data types as the attacker's claim.

Sources — all downstream of one leak-site post: ransomware.live victim entry · ervik.as · QPulse

What it means for usIf accurate, this is a market-integrity exposure rather than a confidentiality one: LP session credentials permit impersonation of the firm toward its counterparties. The check is inexpensive and low-risk whether or not the claim is true — rotate LP credentials, audit FIX session logs for unexpected source addresses, and watch for order flow inconsistent with mandate.

Luxembourg — claimed cross-tenant exposure at a payment orchestrator

Unverified claim

An underground seller claimed on 18 October to hold roughly 627,000 payment records across 26 merchants, allegedly obtained through a cross-tenant authentication-chain weakness in WhenThen, the payment orchestration platform acquired by Mangopay in 2023. The claimed data includes masked card numbers, BINs, expiration dates, personal data, and fraud-decision "rule traces." Asking price was reported as $5,000.

There is no vendor confirmation and no data sample. A forum listing at a low price is weak evidence; it is also the kind of claim that is occasionally true.

Source: UnderCode News, 18 Oct — a single underground-seller claim, no vendor confirmation, no sample.

What it means for usPayment orchestration platforms sit in the critical-ICT third-party perimeter. If we route card traffic through one, the proportionate response is to confirm the vendor's tenant-isolation assurance and its incident-notification commitment against DORA timelines — not to act on the claim.

UK — open-banking aggregation outage, not an attack

Medium — availability

On 18 October, the open-banking aggregator Tink reported major outages on its Barclays connections affecting all users of manual authentication, refresh and payment initiation, and on its Lloyds Banking Group account-information connections covering Lloyds, Halifax, Bank of Scotland and MBNA. The Lloyds event ran roughly seven hours. Both were attributed to bank-side HTTP 500 errors on token and consent endpoints.

Sources: Tink status, Barclays connection · Tink status, Lloyds Banking Group

We record this as an availability event. No evidence indicates a cyberattack and none should be inferred.

What it means for usAggregation concentrates account-information and payment-initiation availability in a single intermediary. A connection-level fault at one aggregator took out the full user population of that connection, with no workaround at the bank's own app. That belongs in the operational-resilience scenario set alongside cloud-provider disruption.

Region · AMER

Credit-union leak-site claims, and a pattern worth more than the names

No confirmed new intrusion at a large US financial institution appeared in this window. What did appear is a Qilin posting wave in which two US credit unions were listed, and a vendor assessment linking one of them to stolen employee credentials.

Qilin lists Via Credit Union — alleged infostealer route

Unverified claimPattern: medium

Qilin listed Via Credit Union, an Indiana institution serving twelve counties, on 16 October. No data types, file counts or ransom demand were published, and the credit union has not confirmed the incident. Telcoe Federal Credit Union was listed on 15 October (reported in our 16 October brief); Genesis Credit Management on 3 October, outside this window.

A vendor assessment links the Via entry to Cavalier-family infostealer activity: eleven compromised users, 229 passwords of which four are classified critical, and two session cookies, with an external attack surface of twelve. The same assessor tied Qilin's J&D Financial listing on 6 October to the same stealer family.

Qilin reportedly posted sixteen claims between 15 and 18 October across ten countries; only six were confirmed by a second crawler. We treat the count as crawler data and the individual claims as claims.

Sources: QPulse (infostealer assessment) · Cybrmonk · Security Arsenal (16-claim count)

What it means for usThe transferable lesson is credential hygiene, not the victim list. Stealer logs convert an employee's browser session into initial access without any exploitation of our perimeter. The controls that bite are phishing-resistant authentication on administrative paths, detection of session-cookie replay, and monitoring of credential exposure for staff who can reach production systems.

What we looked for and did not find

Unknown — not a negative

We searched for a confirmed new breach at a large US bank, and for an exploited vulnerability in a major core-banking platform (Temenos, Finastra, Murex, FIS). Neither was found. The current KEV additions do not include core-banking vendors; the closest recent item is a credit-union-adjacent billing platform flaw on 15 October.

This is an absence of reporting on the sensors we can reach — open-web search and vendor publication — not evidence that no such event occurred. We do not receive FS-ISAC member bulletins, and those are exactly where institution-specific notifications appear first.

Cross-regional

What outlasts the individual incidents

1 · Third-party reach, not third-party spend, is the exposure

Six jurisdictions this window produced the same mechanism: a small vendor with broad reach into a regulated firm. A securities depository's investor portal in Romania. A service contractor at a Japanese broker with knock-on exposure at two regional banks and an insurer. An FX technology provider in the Netherlands holding counterparty session credentials. An open-banking aggregator in the UK. Outsourced project staff at a Korean savings bank. An audit firm in New Jersey whose breach touched at least nine banks and credit unions.

What it means for usSort the third-party register by reach — how many of our systems, customers or counterparties a vendor can touch — rather than by contract value or criticality label. The vendors that caused loss this fortnight were mostly not the expensive ones.

2 · Crypto losses came from key generation and bridge authorisation

Two incidents on 17–18 October, unrelated to each other. Qubic lost roughly 1.3 million dollars from 1,404 wallets; the root cause was weak 55-character seeds generated by older Apple devices, Safari and early iOS wallet apps — a client-side key-generation weakness, not a chain flaw. Validators halted the network and immobilised about 87 percent of the stolen tokens on-chain; the remainder sent to an exchange was frozen. A fix shipped in client v4.26.4.

Cysic had ten million tokens minted without authorisation through its cross-chain bridge after access to a bridge-supporting database, of which about 7.2 million were sold within roughly 23 minutes and about 306 ETH — around 727,000 dollars — was bridged to Ethereum. The bridge was suspended and roughly 2.8 million tokens blacklisted.

Sources — on-chain verifiable: Crypto Briefing (Qubic) · Bitcoin.com News (Qubic) · The Block (Cysic)

What it means for usFor any digital-asset custody or settlement function, the controls that failed are unglamorous and checkable: entropy of client-side key material, and authorisation of cross-chain mint messages. Neither is a market-risk question.

3 · Detection-to-disclosure is now five to eight days

In this window alone: OK Savings detected 11 October, disclosed 16 October. DB Savings reported to the FSS 11 October, publicly known 17 October. The Romanian depository may have been entered around 8 October and disclosed on 16 October. Gopax was named by its regulator two days before it named itself.

The binding clock is different from the observed one. DORA major-incident reporting runs to a four-hour initial notification (and no more than 24 hours from detection), a 72-hour intermediate report, and a one-month final report.

What it means for usOur internal detection-to-decision path has to fit inside four hours, which means the decision right must be delegated in advance. A disclosure that requires committee approval cannot meet the clock.

Vulnerability note — Fortinet, with a same-day deadline

CISA added two entries to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 16 October. CVE-2025-61624 is a path traversal in the FortiOS auto-script engine affecting FortiOS, FortiPAM, FortiProxy and FortiSwitchManager; Unit 42 reports its use to deploy the memory-only PEERSTITCH implant, with more than 500 firewalls exposed through FortiManager. CVE-2026-102764 is the BeyondTrust Remote Support authentication bypass already covered in our 16 October session. The federal remediation deadline for both was 19 October.

Sources: CISA KEV addition, 16 Oct · CISA BeyondTrust alert · Security Affairs (Unit 42 PEERSTITCH reporting)

What it means for usIf FortiManager reaches branch or perimeter estate, treat this as an emergency change rather than a patch-cycle item. Because the implant is memory-only, a clean disk image is not evidence of a clean device — the options are memory capture or rebuild from known-good configuration.

Recommendations

What we would do, and by when

Immediate · 0–7 days

  1. Test the loan-limit and credit-inquiry path for subject verification. Confirm the endpoint validates that the person being enquired about is the authenticated customer, not merely that the calling application holds valid credentials. This is the control that appears to have failed at DB Savings Bank, and it is the same class of failure CERT-In described in India, where payout APIs were called from the institution's own trusted infrastructure.
  2. Treat CVE-2025-61624 as an emergency change wherever FortiManager manages estate we depend on, and check for PEERSTITCH rather than assuming a clean disk scan is sufficient.
  3. Rotate liquidity-provider FIX credentials and audit FIX session logs if we connect to or act as an FX liquidity provider. The action is justified by the consequence, not by the claim's verification status.
  4. Review view-versus-export boundaries on retail investor and customer portals that display national identity or IBAN data, and pre-brief the contact-centre on pretext calls that quote accurate personal details.

Short term · 1–4 weeks

  1. Rewrite the post-incident playbook so that it must close on a change to the exposed interface. Blocking observed addresses is an output, not the outcome. The OK Savings recurrence is the test case: the same institution, four days later, from an address not on the distributed list.
  2. Re-rank the third-party register by reach and add the categories this window exposed: payment orchestration, open-banking aggregation, fund administrators and auditors, trading and FIX connectivity, investor onboarding.
  3. Confirm the four-hour notification path is delegated. Identify who can decide to notify without convening a committee, and rehearse once.
  4. Close the ECB AI action plan due 31 October, including the supplier map — the requirement covers AI use through third parties, not only in-house models.

Strategic · 1–6 months

  1. Measure time-to-detect against time-to-exploit, not against patch SLA. The IMF's October assessment put more than 70 percent of 2026 exploited-vulnerability cases at almost no patch window, against roughly 300 days typical in 2021. If our remediation is measured in weeks and the adversary's reconnaissance-to-action in hours, the calendar is the vulnerability.
  2. Build and test one recovery path independent of the main estate — including independent identity — and prove it end to end rather than documenting it.
  3. Treat credential and session theft as an access-control problem. Phishing-resistant authentication on administrative paths, session-cookie replay detection, and continuous exposure monitoring for staff who can reach production.
