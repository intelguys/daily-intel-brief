Executive summary

1

Six new KEV additions on 10 November, led by a Barracuda chain now confirmed exploited. CVE-2026-116606 (9.3, pre-authentication authorisation bypass) and CVE-2026-111560 (8.6, command injection) give unauthenticated root on the Barracuda Email Security Gateway; Barracuda says a small number of organisations are affected, and CISA set a 13 November federal deadline. The same KEV batch adds the exploited Outlook NTLM-hash zero-day CVE-2026-124194 (6.5, due 24 Nov), a Chrome zero-day CVE-2026-125350 (due 24 Nov), Cisco BroadWorks XXE CVE-2026-20320 (7.5 — note Cisco says it knows of no malicious use) and Openwave CVE-2026-111793 (4.3). Fortinet’s PSIRT advisories for the AI-found FortiOS / FortiManager / FortiAnalyzer flaws are finally live — eight critical advisories, worst CVE-2026-126170 (9.8) — and SAP patched four CVSS 10.0 NetWeaver flaws on its 10 November Patch Day.

2

Korea’s sector security body was itself breached. The Financial Security Institute confirmed its security-assessment support site was hacked, leaking about 200 administration forms, about 300 personal records and about 350 user-linked records, with some financial firms’ security-status material possibly exposed — in a month when 37 Korean financial firms have already reported incidents. In Japan, the SMBC Consumer Finance breach widened to more banks (Hiroshima Bank: 747 records; Fukuoka Financial Group’s three banks confirming exposure), and the Philippines’ National Privacy Commission opened an investigation into the SEC breach. Indonesia’s banking sector faced over 1.2 million blocked attack attempts in 24 hours, but no bank has reported a breach.

3

Enforcement and fraud follow-ups. FinCEN’s MEXC order was published in the Federal Register on 10 November, so the ban on US institutions transacting with MEXC takes effect 10 December; Binance moved immediately, rejecting MEXC withdrawals and holding MEXC-origin deposits for review. Node.js security releases due today (High severity) had not shipped as of 09:00 SGT. Sophos published a detailed ARTEX write-up with hunting indicators, and Fireworks AI widened its breach disclosure — billing data, datasets and partial model weights accessed, with an LLMjacking motive. No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign was found in-window.

AI in the current threat landscape

AI as a target, AI as a weapon or accelerator, and AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI.

AI as a target — Fireworks AI breach widened (NEW, 10 Nov)

Fireworks AI’s 9 November update discloses a second intrusion path — a third-party cloud provider hosting its R&D machines had its own incident on 23 October, and on 24 October the intruder used it to reach Fireworks development machines and run an unauthorised workload — and a wider impact: account / billing information and, for a limited set of accounts, secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. Fireworks links the motive to LLMjacking — selling unauthorised LLM access via illicit gateways — citing Google Threat Intelligence Group / Mandiant research of 2 November; that motive is Fireworks’ attribution, not independently verified. Notion (a customer) says under 1% of workspaces were affected, with Notion AI synthetic test data and some AI chat transcripts involved, no Notion customer data. Standing: the Google / Mandiant LLMjacking research (2–3 Nov) — stolen cloud credentials and LLM API keys, fraudulent bills over $100,000 in a single day and up to about $1 million cumulative at some victims, financial services among the victims, exploitation of CVE-2026-40217 in LiteLLM, and hijacked access resold via “LLM Mixers” including Jet Night; and the Apiiro Claude Code “Don’t Ask Again” research (5 Nov; Anthropic responded 7 Nov) — an approved shell command is saved verbatim, secret included, as an allow rule in .claude/settings.local.json, which is not added to the project’s .gitignore; 1,122 public copies (1.1%) hold at least one real secret, 1,379 secrets in total. Standing controls: key inventory and rotation, endpoint exposure (no unauthenticated Ollama / LiteLLM on the internet), spend and invocation monitoring with revoke-and-rotate.

AI as a weapon / accelerator — Sophos ARTEX write-up (NEW, 10 Nov)

Sophos X-Ops published a detailed ARTEX write-up on 10 November: the open-source multi-agent AI pentesting framework (Go, multiple LLM providers) used by multiple threat actors since July 2026, most prominently in the mid-October ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise attributed by Microsoft to Storm-0940. Sophos reports activity at a small number of its own customers consistent with ARTEX or other AI-assisted offensive tooling — webshell and SQL Server command execution, credential theft, lateral-movement attempts — and says the attack activities are broadly the same as conventional intrusions but scale, noise and persistence increase significantly. Hunting indicators are in the TTP Tracker above; hunt back to at least September 2026 and audit Entra ID service principals and third-party consents since September. The Bank Rakyat AI speculation is an unattributed opinion post, not evidence — do not present it as an AI campaign. CopperDragon (Check Point, 10 Nov) is conventional espionage tooling, not an AI campaign. No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign was found in-window.

AI as a risk and control problem for BFSI — ENISA: advanced AI is industrialising vulnerability discovery (NEW, 10 Nov)

ENISA published its State of Cybersecurity in the EU report on 10 November: the EU threat level is “substantial” (unchanged); ideologically motivated attacks were 57.3% of 2025 incidents; DDoS was 51.3%; and the report notes advanced AI is industrialising vulnerability discovery. Strategic context only — not an incident, and not BFSI-specific. Standing context: the NCSC UK chief executive’s 9 November warning that an AI-powered ransomware attack on UK critical national infrastructure is “highly likely” by 2028 is a forecast / assessment, not a new incident. Microsoft’s FORGE Lab (9 Nov) published defensive research: agentic tools identifying memory-safety issues in Rust core / standard library — AI as defence, not as a threat item. No new OCC, MAS, CSA Singapore or FSS AI-in-finance guidance was issued in this window (the OCC examiner framework is 4 November; MAS’s final AI risk-management guidelines are 7 October). Standing controls: the CSA / GovTech / IMDA personal-AI-agent advisory of 6 November — do not give agents access to banking, government or healthcare accounts; Sophos X-Ops’ research on malicious AI-agent “skills” (6 Nov) — a skill’s description and its code need not agree, and obfuscation defeats static scanners; Haiou “prompt adware” (Check Point, 4 Nov); and Elastic’s finding that attackers execute commands through Claude Code and ChatGPT desktop in confirmed intrusions. Standing controls: agent-tool access, invocation monitoring, key inventory and rotation, endpoint exposure, and spend and invocation monitoring with revoke-and-rotate.

No new AI-scaled phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign in this window

No newly reported AI-generated phishing, vishing or deepfake campaign against a BFSI victim was found for 10–11 November. That is absence of reporting, not evidence it has stopped. The DOJ $129m case is a prosecution of past fraud, not a new campaign, and the Mirae Asset, Bank Rakyat, Arkéa and SMFG cases are not re-labelled as AI campaigns — none attributes them to AI tooling, and the single newspaper characterisation of Mirae Asset as a suspected AI-agent attack is unconfirmed. Microsoft’s 10 November blog on detecting “sleeper agents” in AI model neurons is a re-post of research from February 2026 (updated October 2026), not a new disclosure.
